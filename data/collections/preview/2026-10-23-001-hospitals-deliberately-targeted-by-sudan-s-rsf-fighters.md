# Hospitals deliberately targeted by Sudan's RSF fighters, BBC investigation finds

> [!summary] TL;DR — A BBC investigation reveals systematic attacks on healthcare facilities in el‑Fasher, Darfur, by Sudan’s Rapid Support Forces (RSF), documenting at least 43 assaults between May 2024 and October 2025 and linking them to potential war crimes. The findings have drawn condemnation from the UN and WHO, while the RSF denies the allegations and accuses the government of propaganda.

## Background

The Sudanese civil war, which erupted in April 2023 between the RSF and the national army, has seen repeated violations of international humanitarian law. El‑Fasher, a strategic western city, became a focal point of the conflict as both sides vied for control. Prior to the RSF’s siege, the city’s health system was already fragile, with the Teaching Hospital forced to relocate to the South Hospital. The BBC’s on‑the‑ground reporting now provides a detailed chronology of attacks, documenting injuries, deaths, and abductions of medical personnel, and highlighting a pattern of deliberate targeting that may constitute war crimes under the Geneva Conventions.

## Humanitarian Impact and Medical Workforce Devastation

The investigation uncovers a staggering human cost: 120 medical workers injured, 55 killed, and 10 forcibly disappeared between April 2023 and October 2025. The systematic targeting of hospitals not only deprived civilians of essential care but also eroded the capacity of the health system to respond to the broader crisis. Dr. Muhamed Suliman, a surgeon who served during the siege, described how the RSF’s strategy was to “evacuate the hospitals, to put them out of service because the health system is the cornerstone of any city or any state.” The repeated shelling of the South Hospital, including a devastating raid on 8 June 2024, forced staff to flee and left patients abandoned. The loss of surgical equipment and the destruction of patient records have long‑term implications for public health in Darfur, compounding existing shortages of medicines and power.

## Legal and Geopolitical Implications

UN Human Rights Chief Volker Türk has called the findings “deeply disturbing” and warned that the multiple attacks on civilian infrastructure could amount to war crimes. International humanitarian law explicitly prohibits attacks on medical facilities and personnel, yet the RSF’s alleged campaign appears to be a calculated effort to destabilize the city by removing its health backbone. The RSF has rejected the BBC’s reporting, labeling it propaganda, while the Sudanese military and its allies have been accused of similar violations, including the recent bombing of al‑Daein hospital in eastern Darfur. The World Health Organization (WHO) has condemned the al‑Daein incident and noted that the el‑Fasher events represent “one of the clearest and most severe examples of the wider impact of the conflict on health care in Sudan.” This raises the prospect of international criminal investigations, potentially involving the International Criminal Court (ICC), and could intensify diplomatic pressure on the warring factions.

## Technology, Information Warfare, and the Role of Media

The BBC’s investigation leverages on‑the‑ground sources, satellite imagery, and open‑source data to build a forensic timeline of attacks, showcasing how modern journalism can document war crimes in real time. The use of digital evidence, such as video footage of missile launchers positioned at the South Hospital (claimed by Dr. Alaa Eldin Nugud), illustrates the intersection of technology and propaganda. While the RSF alleges that the hospital was militarized—contrary to international law’s protection of medical facilities—the BBC found no independent corroboration of these claims. This episode underscores the growing importance of verification in conflict zones and the need for robust regulatory frameworks governing the use of drones and remote‑sensing data in humanitarian reporting. Moreover, the editorial priorities of AI‑driven risk analytics and open‑source intelligence (OSINT) are increasingly relevant for monitoring compliance with humanitarian law.

## Key facts

- The BBC documented at least 43 separate attacks on hospitals and health sites in el‑Fasher between May 2024 and October 2025.
- 120 medical workers were injured, 55 killed, and 10 forcibly disappeared in the same period.
- UN Human Rights Chief Volker Türk described the findings as “deeply disturbing” and suggested possible war crimes.
- The RSF denies the allegations, accusing the national army of propaganda.
- The WHO condemned the bombing of al‑Daein hospital in March 2026, which killed dozens and forced closure of the facility.

## Implications

- Potential international criminal proceedings against RSF commanders and any complicit actors.
- Increased scrutiny of health‑care facilities in conflict zones, prompting calls for stronger protection mechanisms.
- Heightened risk of further deterioration of Sudan’s health infrastructure, threatening civilian populations.
- Impact on regional stability as the conflict draws in neighboring states and international actors.
- Advancement of AI and OSINT tools for documenting war crimes, influencing future regulatory policies.

## Outlook

If the alleged systematic targeting of medical facilities continues unchecked, Sudan could face a full collapse of its health system, exacerbating humanitarian crises and prompting larger peacekeeping interventions. International bodies such as the UN, WHO, and ICC are likely to increase investigative efforts, while diplomatic pressure may intensify on the RSF and the Sudanese military to comply with humanitarian law. Simultaneously, the role of technology in conflict documentation will grow, necessitating clearer norms on digital evidence and the ethical use of AI in war‑zone reporting.

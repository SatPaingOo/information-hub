# More than 70,000 social and affordable homes to be built across England over 10 years

> [!summary] TL;DR — The UK government has allocated almost £10bn to build over 70,000 social and affordable homes in regions outside London, the first tranche of a £39bn programme aimed at easing the housing crisis. Critics argue the funding is insufficient and delayed, while housing charities warn the target falls short of the annual need for tens of thousands of units.

## Background

England faces a deepening housing shortage, with record levels of homelessness and a waiting list of 1.34 million households as of March 2025. The Conservative‑led government’s £39bn social housebuilding programme promises 300,000 new units over a decade, but the initial allocation has sparked debate over its scale and timing. Previous administrations, notably under Labour, have struggled to meet demand, and the current plan attempts to balance council‑led construction with housing‑association delivery while navigating fiscal constraints and political pressures.

## Funding Allocation and Regional Distribution

The first tranche of the £39bn programme disburses nearly £10bn to six English regions—Greater Manchester, the West Midlands, West Yorkshire, South Yorkshire, North East England, and Liverpool—while London already secured a separate £6bn. This split reflects historic disparities in housing demand and the government’s effort to decentralise delivery. However, the allocation leaves over £16bn still to be distributed outside the capital, raising questions about equity and the speed at which funds will reach local authorities. The decision to limit council involvement in this round stems from concerns that many councils lack the capacity to deliver the required volume quickly, prompting a reliance on housing associations and private developers for the initial wave.

## Political Reception and Debate

Housing Secretary Angela Rayner framed the announcement as a pragmatic step toward reviving council house building, emphasising collaboration with local authorities. In contrast, Conservative shadow housing secretary Sir James Cleverly dismissed the plan as a “sleight of hand,” noting that the small print postpones delivery until 2039 and that social rent is costlier than market rent, reducing overall output. Labour’s Andy Burnham, once a vocal advocate for dedicating the entire £39bn to social rent, expressed disappointment that only 60 % of the new homes will be for social rent. Meanwhile, housing charities such as Shelter and the National Housing Federation welcomed the funding as a “significant improvement” but stressed that at least 90,000 social homes per year are needed to close the backlog, far exceeding the current trajectory.

## Impact on the Housing Crisis and Social Equity

The immediate effect of the 70,000‑home pledge is modest relative to the scale of need; the UK currently faces a deficit of roughly 200,000 affordable units annually. The new units will primarily benefit low‑income families and those currently in temporary accommodation, exemplified by cases like Natasha in Milton Keynes, who has spent months in a hotel with her infant son. While the funding signals a renewed governmental commitment, critics argue that without a clear annual target and faster disbursement, the programme will not substantially reduce homelessness or ease waiting lists. Moreover, the reliance on housing associations may limit the ability to set strict eligibility criteria, potentially diluting the intended social rent focus.

## Key facts

- The first allocation totals almost £10bn, targeting over 70,000 social and affordable homes across six English regions plus London’s separate £6bn.
- Only 60 % of the newly announced homes will be for social rent; the remainder includes affordable ownership schemes.
- The overall £39bn programme aims for 300,000 homes over ten years, with more than £16bn still to be allocated outside London.
- As of March 2025, 1.34 million households are on social housing waiting lists, including 177,530 children in temporary accommodation.
- Housing charities estimate that at least 90,000 social homes per year are required to meet demand, far above the current projected delivery.

## Implications

- The phased funding approach may delay urgent relief for families in temporary housing, potentially prolonging exposure to substandard conditions.
- Political disagreement over the scale and timing could affect future parliamentary support and the ability to secure additional financing.
- Reliance on housing associations may shift construction risk away from councils, potentially influencing long‑term affordability and tenant protections.
- The emphasis on social rent (60 %) aligns with calls from Labour figures like Andy Burnham, but falls short of the full dedication he previously advocated.

## Outlook

If the government can accelerate the remaining £16bn allocation and clarify annual targets, the programme could gradually reduce the housing deficit and begin to reverse the rise in homelessness. However, without a decisive shift toward council‑led building and faster fund flow, the initiative risks being viewed as a symbolic gesture rather than a substantive solution. Stakeholders—including local authorities, housing providers, and affected families—will be watching for concrete milestones in the next 12‑18 months to gauge whether the pledge translates into tangible improvements in housing security across England.

# Trump hails 'historic' deal for US to control 65bn barrels of Venezuela's oil

> [!summary] TL;DR — President Donald Trump announced a landmark agreement giving the United States operational control over more than 65 billion barrels of Venezuela’s proven oil reserves, a move framed as a boost to American energy security and a catalyst for Venezuela’s economic revival. The deal, brokered with interim Venezuelan leader Delcy Rodriguez and U.S. officials including Secretary of State Marco Rubio, promises massive private investment and a 100‑year concession for a joint venture, while raising questions about sovereignty, legal challenges, and market impacts.

## Background

Venezuela holds the world’s largest proven oil reserves—approximately 303 billion barrels—yet production has fallen sharply since the late 1990s due to sanctions, mismanagement, and political instability. The United States, historically a major importer of Venezuelan crude, has sought ways to diversify its energy supply and reduce dependence on volatile Middle‑East sources, especially after gasoline price spikes linked to the Iran conflict. In early January 2026, U.S. special forces captured Nicolás Maduro, prompting Trump to declare that the U.S. would manage Venezuela until a “safe, proper and judicious transition” could be achieved. The new agreement marks an unprecedented level of foreign control over a sovereign nation’s hydrocarbon assets, surpassing even the Coalition Provisional Authority’s oversight of Iraq’s oil after 2003.

## Geopolitical Realignment and Power Dynamics

The deal reshapes the strategic calculus between Washington and Caracas, effectively turning a long‑standing adversarial relationship into a de‑facto partnership. By granting the U.S. a 55 % stake in a joint venture with an “experienced private operator in Venezuela,” the agreement extends American influence deep into the Venezuelan petroleum sector without direct military occupation. This arrangement mirrors historical precedents such as the post‑Saddam Iraq model, but with a crucial difference: the concession is for a century, embedding U.S. control across multiple generations. The move also signals a broader shift in U.S. foreign policy toward leveraging resource assets as diplomatic tools, especially in regions where traditional alliances are fragile.

## Economic and Energy Market Implications

On the economic front, the agreement is projected to inject over $100 billion in private investment and generate more than $209 billion in tax revenue for Venezuela, according to Delcy Rodriguez. Proponents argue that this capital will modernise aging infrastructure, create thousands of high‑paying jobs, and revitalise a country whose GDP has contracted for more than a decade. For the United States, the deal promises to “more than double” American oil reserves, potentially easing domestic gasoline prices and reducing reliance on OPEC members. However, analysts caution that the market impact will depend on the speed of implementation, the ability to attract multinational operators, and the stability of the security environment in Venezuela.

## Legal, Constitutional, and Sovereignty Concerns

The agreement’s legality remains opaque because the full text has not been released, and Venezuela’s constitutional framework may not accommodate a 100‑year concession of sovereign natural resources. International law, including the United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, could be invoked by opposition groups and civil society. Moreover, the deal may face challenges in Venezuelan courts and from regional bodies such as the Organization of American States, which have historically guarded against external control of strategic assets. The absence of transparent terms also raises the risk of future disputes, especially concerning revenue sharing, environmental standards, and the rights of local communities.

## Key facts

- The U.S. now controls a joint venture holding 65 billion barrels of Venezuela’s proven oil reserves, representing roughly 21 % of the country’s total reserves.
- The agreement grants the U.S. a 55 % ownership stake, while an “experienced private operator in Venezuela” holds the remainder.
- Venezuela’s interim president, Delcy Rodriguez, announced a 100‑year concession for the venture and projected $100 billion in private investment and $209 billion in state tax revenue.
- Trump framed the deal as a means to double U.S. oil reserves, lower gasoline prices, and reduce taxpayer cost.
- The full legal text of the agreement has not been made public, prompting concerns about constitutional and international law compliance.

## Implications

- Potential stabilization of global oil markets through increased supply, but risk of price volatility if implementation stalls.
- Enhanced U.S. energy independence could reshape strategic alliances in the Middle East and Latin America.
- Venezuela may experience a short‑term economic boost, yet long‑term sovereignty questions could deter other investors.
- Regional bodies may scrutinize the deal, possibly leading to diplomatic friction between the U.S. and Latin American nations.

## Outlook

If the joint venture successfully attracts multinational oil majors and modernises Venezuela’s infrastructure, the agreement could serve as a model for leveraging resource wealth to foster political and economic transition. Conversely, delays, legal challenges, or mismanagement could exacerbate existing tensions, undermine investor confidence, and provoke domestic backlash in both countries. Continuous monitoring of the venture’s operational progress, revenue flows, and compliance with environmental and human‑rights standards will be essential to assess whether the “historic” deal delivers on its promises or becomes a contentious footnote in U.S.–Latin American relations.

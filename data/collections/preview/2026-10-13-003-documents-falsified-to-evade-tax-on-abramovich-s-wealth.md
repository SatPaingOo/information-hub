# Documents falsified to evade tax on Abramovich's wealth, BBC finds

> [!summary] TL;DR — BBC Panorama and the Bureau of Investigative Journalism revealed that Deloitte Cyprus helped create backdated paperwork to hide Roman Abramovich’s $672 m in assets and avoid taxes. The scheme moved wealth to offshore entities, raising questions about professional accountability and the effectiveness of transparency laws, especially as Abramovich remains under sanctions and criminal investigations.

## Background

Roman Abramovich, once the UK’s most prominent Russian oligarch, built his fortune through a series of opaque deals, including the purchase and resale of a Russian state‑owned oil company in the 1990s. After Russia’s full‑scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, he was sanctioned by the UK, forced to sell Chelsea Football Club, and placed under criminal investigation in Jersey over alleged financial misconduct. Cyprus, historically a tax‑haven, introduced a transparency law in 2006 requiring public disclosure of company accounts, prompting Abramovich’s network to resort to sophisticated document falsification to preserve secrecy. The BBC’s joint investigation with the Bureau of Investigative Journalism uncovered thousands of leaked documents detailing how offshore companies administered from Limassol, notably MeritServus, were used to conceal assets worth hundreds of millions of dollars.

## Mechanisms of Tax Evasion

The investigation shows a multi‑step process: Deloitte Cyprus prepared draft accounts for Amiscan Investments in 2003, listing $672 m in luxury property, yachts, and private jets. To circumvent the 2006 Cyprus transparency law, Abramovich’s associates and MeritServus transferred these assets to Amiscan’s parent company in the British Virgin Islands, backdating the transaction to 2003. Deloitte then signed off the 2003 accounts in 2006, effectively erasing the assets from the public record. A parallel scheme involving Kravin Investments, holding nearly $2 bn, used similar backdating tactics to avoid paying Cypriot tax on interest income. The leaked documents reveal that Deloitte was aware the accounts were misleading, yet proceeded to certify them, constituting a clear breach of statutory duties.

## Regulatory and Geopolitical Implications

The findings spotlight critical gaps in international financial transparency regimes. While Cyprus’s 2006 law aimed to expose hidden wealth, the ease with which it was subverted underscores the need for stricter verification mechanisms and cross‑jurisdictional cooperation. Geopolitically, the revelations reinforce concerns that sanctioned oligarchs can still leverage professional services to shield assets, potentially undermining the efficacy of sanctions regimes targeting Russia’s war effort. Moreover, the case may prompt the UK and EU to tighten oversight of accountancy firms operating in tax‑haven jurisdictions, and could accelerate discussions at forums such as the G7’s Financial Action Task Force on improving beneficial‑ownership registries.

## Accountancy Profession and Industry Accountability

Lord Sikka, a professor of accounting at the University of Sheffield, described the conduct as “pretty scandalous” and a violation of statutory duty, highlighting the ethical lapses within a major “Big Four” firm. The scandal raises questions about the adequacy of existing professional safeguards, peer reviews, and disciplinary frameworks. It may lead to calls for more rigorous audits, mandatory whistleblower protections for junior staff, and potential revisions to the International Ethics Standards Board for Accountants (IESBA) guidelines. The industry is also likely to face increased regulatory scrutiny, with regulators examining whether current penalties deter large‑scale misconduct.

## Key facts

- Deloitte Cyprus signed off falsified accounts that removed $672 m in assets from Abramovich’s Cypriot company Amiscan Investments.
- The scheme involved backdating asset transfers to the British Virgin Islands to evade Cyprus’s 2006 transparency law.
- Another Cypriot entity, Kravin Investments, held nearly $2 bn and used similar backdating to avoid tax on interest income.
- The leaked documents indicate Deloitte was aware the accounts were misleading yet certified them.
- Roman Abramovich’s lawyers deny any knowledge or authorisation of the falsified documents.
- Abramovich remains under UK sanctions and a four‑year criminal investigation in Jersey.

## Implications

- Potential tightening of cross‑border audit regulations and beneficial‑ownership reporting.
- Increased pressure on accountancy firms to adopt stricter internal compliance controls.
- Renewed geopolitical focus on the effectiveness of sanctions against Russian oligarchs.
- Possible legislative reforms in the EU and UK to close loopholes exploited by offshore structures.

## Outlook

The BBC investigation is likely to trigger a cascade of regulatory actions, including inquiries by the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) and the International Compliance Association. Deloitte may face professional disciplinary proceedings, while the broader accountancy sector could see new standards requiring third‑party verification of high‑value asset transfers. For Abramovich, the scandal adds to an existing web of legal challenges, potentially influencing ongoing Jersey proceedings and future sanction reviews. Stakeholders in the financial services industry should anticipate heightened scrutiny and prepare robust compliance frameworks to mitigate similar risks.

# Spain announces ban on evictions after protests over 87-year-old woman's removal from flat

> [!summary] TL;DR — Spain’s coalition government has approved emergency housing decrees banning evictions until 2030, rolling over rental contracts, and restricting investment‑fund purchases of property, following nationwide protests sparked by the forced removal of 87‑year‑old Maricarmen Abascal from her Madrid flat.

## Background

Spain’s housing market has been under severe strain for years, driven by rapid urbanization, foreign investment, and a shortage of affordable rentals. The case of Maricarmen Abascal—whose family first rented the flat in 1956 and who faced a rent hike exceeding her pension after the property was bought by Urbagestión in 2018—became a potent symbol of the broader crisis. Mass demonstrations in Madrid, Barcelona, Seville, Málaga and Cádiz highlighted public frustration with speculative buying and the lack of tenant protections, pressuring the left‑leaning coalition of Pedro Sánchez to respond with concrete legislative action.

## Political Dynamics & Parliamentary Hurdles

The Sánchez government, a coalition of the Socialists and the Sumar alliance, lacks an outright majority and has previously seen right‑leaning parties block similar decrees (e.g., the April rental‑contract rollover). The new package—presented as two emergency decrees—must be ratified by Congress within 30 days, a timeline the government hopes to meet by this Friday. However, the Junts party, representing Catalan nationalists, has warned that lumping small landlords together with “vulture funds” risks alienating its natural electorate, potentially complicating the vote. The government’s strategy appears to be a calculated gamble: use the emotional momentum of the Abascal case to force a quick parliamentary approval, banking on public pressure to outweigh opposition concerns.

## Economic Implications for the Housing Market

The immediate effect of the eviction ban and the freeze on investment‑fund purchases is to reduce speculative pressure on residential assets. By prohibiting firms such as Urbagestión from buying existing housing stock, the decree aims to redirect capital toward new construction and social housing projects, potentially easing the supply crunch. However, analysts warn that the measures could deter legitimate institutional investors, narrowing the pool of financing for large‑scale developments. The rollover of rental contracts until 2028 will provide short‑term relief for millions of tenants but may also reduce landlords’ incentive to maintain or improve properties, especially if rent caps remain in place. The net impact will depend on how strictly the regulations are enforced and whether complementary incentives for property owners are introduced.

## Social Unrest, Legal Precedent & International Resonance

The protests that precipitated the decree illustrate a growing trend across Europe of citizen‑led movements demanding housing as a fundamental right. The “Maricarmen Abascal decree” could set a legal benchmark, encouraging similar actions in other EU member states grappling with housing affordability. Domestically, the government’s swift response may quell immediate street unrest, but the call for further demonstrations next weekend from groups like the Madrid Tenants’ Union signals that activism will remain vigilant. Legally, the decrees raise questions about the balance between public interest and property rights, potentially inviting challenges in the Spanish courts and from the European Commission’s competition authorities, especially if the measures are viewed as anti‑competitive.

## Key facts

- The coalition approved two emergency decrees banning all evictions until 2030, rolling over rental contracts to 2028, and prohibiting investment firms (“vulture funds”) from purchasing residential property.
- The measures must be ratified by Congress within 30 days; the government aims for a vote this Friday.
- The trigger was the forced removal of 87‑year‑old Maricarmen Abascal from her Madrid flat, a case that galvanized nationwide protests.
- Health Minister Mónica García framed the reforms as relief for over five million people, while opposition politician Míriam Nogueras warned the package penalizes small landlords.
- Previous similar decrees have been blocked by right‑leaning parties, highlighting the fragile parliamentary balance.
- The eviction of Esmeralda Tejero in Zaragoza was temporarily suspended on the same day, underscoring the broader crisis.

## Implications

- Short‑term tenant relief and reduced speculative buying, but potential disincentive for institutional investment in Spanish housing.
- Increased political friction with Junts and other right‑leaning parties, possibly delaying or weakening final approval.
- A potential legal challenge to the eviction ban, testing the limits of state intervention in property rights.
- Precedent for other EU nations facing similar housing pressures, possibly prompting a wave of regulatory responses.

## Outlook

If Congress passes the decrees as planned, Spain will move toward a more tenant‑friendly housing regime, potentially easing immediate social tensions. However, the long‑term success hinges on complementary policies that encourage property maintenance, expand social housing stock, and balance investor interests. Continued monitoring of protest activity and judicial responses will be essential to gauge whether the “Maricarmen Abascal decree” marks a durable shift in Spanish housing policy or a temporary political concession.

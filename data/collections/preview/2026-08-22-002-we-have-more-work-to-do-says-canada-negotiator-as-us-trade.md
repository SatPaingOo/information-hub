# 'We have more work to do', says Canada negotiator as US trade deadline looms

> [!summary] TL;DR — Canada and US negotiators are racing against a midnight Friday deadline to finalize a trade deal that could prevent additional US tariffs on Canadian goods. Key concessions under discussion include reduced tariffs on steel, aluminium, and automobiles, alongside potential Canadian market access for US dairy producers. Provincial leaders across Canada express mixed reactions, reflecting internal divisions over the terms of the agreement.

## Background

The Canada-US trade relationship has long been a cornerstone of North American economic integration, particularly under the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA). Recent tensions emerged when the Trump administration threatened steep tariffs on Canadian exports, citing concerns over market access and trade imbalances. Prime Minister Justin Trudeau's government faced mounting pressure to secure a deal that would protect Canada's key industries, including steel, automotive, and agriculture, while avoiding the economic fallout of retaliatory measures. The current negotiations represent a critical test of Canada's ability to balance domestic interests with the need to maintain its largest trading relationship.

## Economic Leverage and Tariff Concessions

The leaked details suggest a significant recalibration of US tariff policy, with rates on Canadian steel and aluminium dropping from 50% to 25%, and auto tariffs from 25% to 15%. These reductions represent a partial victory for Canada, which had resisted earlier US demands for deeper market liberalization. However, the proposed 25% tariff on steel—still substantially above pre-trade-war levels—raises concerns among Canadian industrialists and provincial governments. The city of Hamilton, home to Canada's second-largest steel producer, has warned that such rates could undermine the sector's competitiveness, potentially leading to job losses and plant closures. The economic calculus for Canadian policymakers is therefore complex: accepting reduced tariffs may prevent immediate job losses from US retaliation, but could set a precedent for future concessions that erode long-term industrial capacity.

## Domestic Political Fractures and Provincial Autonomy

The divergent responses from provincial leaders underscore the fragmented nature of Canada's political landscape. Manitoba Premier Wab Kinew advocates for a firm stance, arguing that premature concessions risk deindustrialization. Conversely, Nova Scotia and the Yukon have signaled willingness to restore US alcohol sales, a move that could unlock further tariff reductions. Quebec Premier Christine Frédéricte's cautious approach reflects the province's dependence on dairy farming, where US market access would directly impact thousands of rural livelihoods. This patchwork of regional interests places Prime Minister Mark Carney in a delicate position: he must navigate not only US demands but also a coalition of provincial leaders whose economic fortunes vary dramatically across the federation. The internal discord mirrors broader debates about Canada's trade strategy, particularly in the wake of Trudeau's 'elbows-up' approach that sought to project strength while securing favorable terms.

## Geopolitical Signaling and Transatlantic Relations

Beyond the bilateral Canada-US dynamic, these negotiations carry symbolic weight in the broader context of North American trade sovereignty. The Trump administration's leverage—threatening 50% tariffs that could cost Canada up to 90,000 jobs—reflects a transactional approach to trade policy that has strained traditional alliances. For Canada, the deal represents a test of its ability to defend economic interests without alienating its largest export market. The leaked audio of US Vice-President JD Vance mocking Carney's 'tough' posture adds an undercurrent of political theater, suggesting that the negotiations are as much about domestic political posturing as economic efficiency. From a geopolitical perspective, the outcome may influence how other trading partners, including the European Union, assess the reliability of US trade commitments in an era of protectionist resurgence.

## Key facts

- Negotiators face a midnight Friday deadline to finalize a US-Canada trade deal.
- US tariffs on Canadian steel and aluminium could drop from 50% to 25% under the tentative framework.
- Auto tariffs may be reduced from 25% to 15% in exchange for Canadian concessions.
- Canada is reportedly considering allowing greater US dairy market access.
- An estimated 90,000 Canadian jobs could be lost if US tariffs are implemented.
- Provincial leaders from Manitoba, Quebec, Nova Scotia, and Yukon express varying levels of support for the deal.
- Conservative leader Pierre Poilievre warns that one-sided tariffs risk deindustrialization.

## Implications

- A finalized deal could stabilize Canada's manufacturing sector, particularly steel and automotive, by preventing immediate US retaliation.
- Accepting reduced but still elevated tariffs may set a precedent for future US demands, potentially weakening Canada's negotiating position.
- The internal political divide may constrain Prime Minister Carney's ability to secure parliamentary support for any agreement.
- Restoring US alcohol sales could serve as a confidence-building measure, but may face resistance from provinces with strong agricultural lobbies.

## Outlook

The final hours of negotiation will determine whether a compromise can be reached that satisfies both US demands and Canadian domestic interests. Given the high stakes and divergent provincial positions, a last-minute agreement appears likely, though its terms may leave key stakeholders dissatisfied. In the broader geopolitical landscape, the deal's outcome will signal the durability of North American trade integration amid rising protectionism. If finalized, the agreement could provide temporary relief, but Canada will likely face renewed pressure on other trade fronts, including its relationship with the European Union and ongoing discussions about digital trade frameworks.

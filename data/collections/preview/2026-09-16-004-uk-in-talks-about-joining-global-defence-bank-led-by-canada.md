# UK in talks about joining global defence bank led by Canada

> [!summary] TL;DR — The UK is in preliminary talks to join Canada‑led Defence, Security and Resilience Bank (DSRB), which promises cheaper loans for defence projects. Chancellor John Healey is weighing the move after his predecessor rejected it, while the government signals a commitment to boost defence industrial capacity. The discussions coincide with heightened NATO activity and a broader push to increase UK defence spending amid ongoing tensions with Russia.

## Background

Rising defence commitments, especially in support of Ukraine and deterrence against Russian aggression, have placed significant fiscal pressure on the UK Treasury. The government’s long‑term goal of reaching 3.5 % of national income by 2035 remains aspirational, and the current trajectory falls short of the 3 % benchmark by 2030. In this context, cheaper financing mechanisms are being explored. Canada’s initiative to establish the DSRB—already backed by Albania, Bulgaria, Belgium, Greece, Latvia, Luxembourg, Romania, Turkey and Ukraine—offers a multilateral platform for low‑cost borrowing. The UK’s previous leadership under former Chancellor Rachel Reeves was sceptical, but the current Chancellor, John Healey, is privately pushing for membership as a way to raise funds without straining the budget. The talks also reflect a broader strategic shift toward deeper defence industrial cooperation among like‑minded democracies.

## Strategic Rationale for UK Participation

The UK’s interest in the DSRB is driven by three inter‑linked factors. First, the need to modernise the British defence industrial base to meet NATO’s collective deterrence requirements, especially as Ukraine’s war demonstrates the cost of outdated equipment. Second, the desire to share risk and leverage economies of scale with allies, reducing the fiscal burden on British taxpayers. Third, the political imperative to demonstrate a “serious about security” stance ahead of the October Budget and the upcoming spending review. By joining the bank, the UK could access loans at rates below those available through conventional sovereign borrowing, freeing up capital for procurement, research and development, and deployment of advanced systems such as Storm Shadow missiles and next‑generation cyber capabilities.

## Financial Mechanics and Cost Implications

Membership for the UK and other G7 nations would require an upfront investment of roughly £870 million spread over three years. This fee funds the bank’s risk‑sharing pool, guarantees, and technical assistance services. In return, member governments receive preferential interest rates—estimated to be 0.5‑1 % lower than market rates—on loans earmarked for defence projects. The DSRB’s capital structure is designed to attract high‑credit‑quality members, thereby lowering the overall cost of capital for all participants. For the UK, the net fiscal impact would be modest relative to its projected defence budget growth, but the real benefit lies in the ability to front‑load major acquisitions without compromising other public services. Treasury officials have stressed that no final decision has been made, indicating that the cost‑benefit analysis is still being refined.

## Geopolitical Implications for NATO and the Wider Alliance

A UK entry into the DSRB would strengthen the transatlantic defence architecture by aligning London more closely with Ottawa’s financial instruments and deepening G7 coordination on defence industrial policy. It would also signal unity within NATO at a time when the alliance is confronting Russia’s “campaign of hostile actions,” including the recent drone shoot‑down over Lithuania and alleged flare‑gun incidents in the Baltic Sea. Moreover, the move could encourage other non‑member states to consider participation, potentially creating a broader “democratic defence financing” network that counters Chinese and Russian influence in global arms markets. Conversely, the bank’s focus on member‑state loans could raise concerns about “splintering” of defence spending, with some allies relying on the DSRB while others continue to use traditional channels, possibly leading to uneven capability development across the alliance.

## Key facts

- Chancellor John Healey is actively considering a UK bid to join the Defence, Security and Resilience Bank (DSRB).
- The DSRB is led by Canada and already has nine European members, including Ukraine and Turkey.
- Full membership for the UK would cost about £870 million over three years, providing cheaper loans for defence projects.
- Former Chancellor Rachel Reeves previously rejected the idea, prompting Healey’s renewed push.
- Prime Minister Andy Burnham’s planned meeting with Canadian PM Mark Carney was cancelled due to a family bereavement.
- NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte is visiting the UK for his first meeting with Burnham and will highlight NATO’s resolve against Russian aggression.
- Recent security incidents include a drone shot down over Lithuania and a Russian warship accused of firing flares at a Danish helicopter in the Baltic Sea.

## Implications

- Fiscal relief: Access to lower‑cost financing could ease pressure on the Treasury’s October Budget and spending review.
- Industrial boost: Cheaper capital would accelerate modernisation of UK defence manufacturing and research capabilities.
- Alliance cohesion: UK participation would deepen defence financial cooperation with Canada and other DSRB members, reinforcing NATO unity.
- Strategic deterrence: Enhanced financing options could improve the UK’s ability to sustain long‑term commitments to Ukraine and deter further Russian aggression.

## Outlook

The UK is likely to reach a decision on DSRB membership before the October Budget, balancing the need for affordable defence financing against the upfront contribution and potential political sensitivities. If approved, the bank would become a cornerstone of Britain’s multilateral defence strategy, complementing existing mechanisms such as the Multilateral Defence Mechanism and strengthening the UK’s role within NATO’s industrial base. Failure to join could leave the UK reliant on more expensive sovereign borrowing, potentially constraining future defence programmes at a time of heightened geopolitical risk.

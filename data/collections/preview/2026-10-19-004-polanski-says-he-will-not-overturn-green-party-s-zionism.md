# Polanski says he will not overturn Green Party's 'Zionism is racism' motion

> [!summary] TL;DR — Zack Polanski, leader of the UK Green Party, has refused to overturn a controversial motion declaring Zionism a form of racism, citing party rules that lock the policy in place until 2028. The motion, passed by a narrow member vote, has sparked internal division, external backlash from Jewish groups, and threatens the party's standing in upcoming elections.

## Background

The motion was approved at the Green Party’s conference earlier this month, defining Zionism as an ideology that supports “an ethnonationalist Jewish state in historic Palestine to the exclusion and/or domination of the non‑Jewish population.” It also calls for a single democratic state across historic Palestine, a full arms embargo on Israel, and support for Palestinian liberation. The vote—1,022 to 813—represented a tiny fraction of the party’s 230,000 members, highlighting the role of a highly motivated minority in shaping policy. Polanski, who previously said he would not have backed the motion without two amendments, now says he respects the democratic will of the membership, even though the decision has drawn criticism from MPs, Jewish campaigners, the government, and the European Green movement. The equality watchdog has written to the party seeking clarification, while Labour has threatened to end power‑sharing agreements with Greens on local councils unless the motion is disowned.

## Leadership Dilemma and Democratic Constraints

Polanski’s stance illustrates the tension between a leader’s personal convictions and the procedural rigidity of a participatory political party. Under the Green Party’s rule book, a motion passed at the annual conference becomes binding policy for two years, leaving the leadership with limited authority to reverse it. Polanski’s refusal to overturn the motion, despite his own reservations, underscores a broader principle: respect for member‑driven decision‑making, even when the outcome is politically toxic. This adherence to internal democracy, however, has exposed a structural vulnerability—policy can be captured by a relatively small, highly mobilised cohort, locking the party into positions that may alienate the broader electorate and its own elected representatives. The case raises questions about whether the Green Party’s governance model needs recalibration to balance grassroots empowerment with electoral viability.

## Impact on the Green Party’s Electoral Prospects

The fallout from the motion has already begun to affect the party’s electoral calculus. In the recent Holborn and St Pancras by‑election, Polanski finished second to Labour, a result that many analysts attribute to the backlash over the Zionism policy. Councillors across the country report heightened voter hostility, and Labour’s threat to sever local‑government pacts with the Greens adds a further layer of institutional pressure. Moreover, the motion has become a focal point for media narratives that portray the Greens as extremist on foreign policy, potentially eroding support among centrist voters who might otherwise be attracted to the party’s environmental agenda. The internal discord also hampers the party’s ability to present a unified front on climate and social justice issues, risking a feedback loop where policy controversy fuels electoral decline, which in turn weakens the party’s capacity to influence policy.

## Broader Geopolitical Repercussions

Beyond the UK’s domestic politics, the motion reverberates through the wider European and Middle‑Eastern diplomatic landscape. The European Green movement has expressed disappointment, warning that the language used could be interpreted as legitimising anti‑Semitic discourse, while the Board of Deputies of British Jews has accused the party of erasing Jewish identity and endorsing the elimination of Israel. In the context of ongoing Israel‑Palestine tensions, the motion’s endorsement of a single democratic state and an arms embargo places the Greens at odds with mainstream European Union positions, potentially complicating cross‑border cooperation on peace initiatives. Additionally, the controversy highlights the growing challenge for Western democratic parties in navigating the fine line between supporting Palestinian rights and avoiding language that can be construed as anti‑Jewish, a dilemma that will likely shape future debates on foreign policy and human‑rights legislation across the continent.

## Key facts

- The motion passed 1,022‑813 at the Green Party conference, representing less than 0.5 % of the party’s total membership.
- Polanski says the policy is locked in until 2028 due to party rules and cannot be amended now.
- The motion defines Zionism as an ideology supporting an ethnonationalist Jewish state to the exclusion of non‑Jewish populations.
- The Green Party’s stance includes support for a single democratic state across historic Palestine, a full arms embargo on Israel, and Palestinian liberation.
- The equality watchdog has written to the Greens seeking clarification on the motion’s implications.
- Labour has threatened to end power‑sharing agreements with Greens on local councils unless the motion is disowned.

## Implications

- Internal party cohesion is at risk, with senior figures like Adrian Ramsay calling for earlier policy review.
- Electoral performance may suffer as voters react negatively to the controversial foreign‑policy position.
- Cross‑party cooperation on local government could be disrupted by Labour’s threat to sever ties.
- The motion may influence the European Green movement’s stance on Israel‑Palestine, potentially causing diplomatic friction.
- The controversy could spur a broader debate within democratic parties about how to address Palestinian rights without alienating Jewish constituents.

## Outlook

Unless the Green Party can reconcile its internal democratic procedures with the electoral and diplomatic costs of the current policy, the controversy is likely to persist and further strain the party’s leadership. A potential pathway forward involves either a membership ballot to reaffirm or revise the motion, or a procedural amendment to allow leadership discretion in policy reversal. The party’s ability to navigate this challenge will shape not only its own trajectory but also the broader discourse on how progressive movements balance principle with political pragmatism in an increasingly polarised global environment.

# Y Combinator’s Garry Tan wants US open-weight AI labs to ‘distill’ frontier models, too

> [!summary] TL;DR — Y Combinator CEO Garry Tan argues that U.S. open‑weight AI labs should be allowed to distill frontier models, mirroring practices currently attributed to Chinese labs, to create a more robust, non‑Chinese open‑source AI ecosystem while avoiding regulatory overreach.

## Background

The AI community is embroiled in a debate over model distillation—a technique where a smaller model is trained by prompting a larger, more capable model to transfer its reasoning and knowledge. Recent reports from Anthropic allege that Chinese labs are conducting “illicit distillation attacks,” using stolen credentials and deceptive tactics to extract frontier model weights without permission. While some U.S. regulators and industry voices have called for tighter controls, Y Combinator’s Garry Tan takes a contrarian stance. He contends that American frontier labs have historically harvested massive amounts of public data—often copyrighted—without seeking permission, and that restricting downstream use of that knowledge is overly prescriptive. Tan’s position is twofold: he wants U.S. open‑weight labs to have legitimate, front‑door access to frontier models for distillation, and he believes government should normalize broad access to intelligence trained on public data as a public good rather than a locked‑down service.

## Strategic Implications for the U.S. AI Ecosystem

Tan’s call for a domestic distillation regime could reshape the competitive landscape by empowering smaller, open‑weight labs to build high‑quality models without relying on proprietary APIs. This would diversify the U.S. AI stack, reducing dependence on a handful of frontier providers such as OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google. By enabling open‑source models to incorporate frontier reasoning, the United States could offer alternatives to Chinese‑originated open models, strengthening national security and economic resilience. Moreover, a robust open‑weight ecosystem could spur innovation in agentic AI and specialized verticals, as developers gain fine‑grained control over model behavior rather than being limited to black‑box API calls.

## Regulatory Landscape and the Distillation Debate

The regulatory tension is stark. Anthropic’s public accusations have prompted calls for crackdowns, framing unauthorized distillation as intellectual property theft. Tan counters that such regulation would be an overreach, arguing that frontier labs themselves have historically amassed training data without consent, often violating copyright norms. He suggests that the government should instead focus on ensuring equitable access to publicly‑trained intelligence, treating it as a public good. This perspective raises questions about the balance between protecting IP and fostering an open AI commons, especially as other nations, notably China, are already advancing distillation techniques. The outcome could set a precedent for how democratic societies govern model knowledge transfer.

## Impact on the Open‑Source AI Community

For open‑source practitioners, unrestricted distillation could unlock a new wave of high‑fidelity models that are both transparent and customizable. Open‑weight labs could iteratively refine frontier capabilities, creating models that are safer, more interpretable, and tailored to specific domains such as healthcare or autonomous systems. This democratization aligns with the ethos of open‑source AI, where community contributions accelerate progress. However, it also introduces challenges: ensuring model quality, preventing misuse, and maintaining ethical standards without the guardrails of proprietary oversight. The community will need robust governance frameworks to manage these risks while preserving the benefits of open access.

## Key facts

- Garry Tan, CEO of Y Combinator, advocates for U.S. open‑weight AI labs to legally distill frontier models, mirroring practices attributed to Chinese labs.
- Anthropic released a report accusing Chinese labs of “illicit distillation attacks,” using stolen credentials and deceptive tactics.
- Tan argues that frontier labs have historically harvested massive public and copyrighted data without permission, making restrictive regulations hypocritical.
- He believes government should treat intelligence trained on public data as a public good, not a locked‑down service.
- The proposal aims to create a diversified, non‑Chinese open‑source AI ecosystem, reducing reliance on a few proprietary providers.

## Implications

- Potential shift in U.S. AI competitive posture, with more open‑source models capable of frontier‑level reasoning.
- Increased regulatory scrutiny on distillation practices, especially concerning IP and data provenance.
- New governance challenges for the open‑source community in ensuring model safety and quality.
- Possible escalation of the U.S.–China AI technology rivalry, as both sides refine distillation capabilities.

## Outlook

If Tan’s vision gains traction, the United States could see a flourishing ecosystem of open‑weight models that rival or surpass Chinese alternatives, fostering innovation across agentic AI, specialized LLMs, and domain‑specific applications. However, this trajectory will depend on striking a delicate balance between openness and accountability. Policymakers will need to craft nuanced regulations that protect legitimate IP while avoiding stifling the public‑good aspects of AI knowledge sharing. The next 12‑18 months will likely reveal whether the industry adopts a “front‑door” distillation regime or continues to navigate the current gray zone of covert knowledge extraction.

# Google rejects UK request for Russell Brand YouTube video to be removed, sources say

> [!summary] TL;DR — Google has declined a UK government request to delete a YouTube video posted by Russell Brand’s wife that contains his claim of innocence, prompting debate over prisoners’ speech rights, platform regulation, and victim protection.

## Background

Former UK Prime Minister Rishi Sunak’s government, through the Ministry of Justice (MoJ) and the Prison Service of England and Wales, asked Google to remove the video on the grounds that it caused distress to victims of Brand’s sexual‑assault convictions. The request follows Brand’s October 2024 conviction to 10½ years in prison for rape and three counts of sexual assault, and a broader push by UK authorities to limit prisoners’ ability to disseminate material that could re‑traumatise survivors. Google’s refusal, citing its own content‑policy rules, highlights the tension between national regulatory expectations and the platform’s self‑governance, a recurring theme in the evolving landscape of digital speech and tech‑policy.

## Legal and Policy Framework

The UK’s Ministry of Justice argues that removal is “only right” for content that is “distressing for victims”, a stance rooted in the Prison Service’s authority to restrict prisoner communications that could breach rehabilitation guidelines. However, Google’s decision underscores that platform‑specific terms of service often diverge from national legal expectations. This case exemplifies the growing friction between sovereign regulatory ambitions and the extraterritorial reach of global tech companies, especially as the UK pushes for stricter enforcement of “online safety” and “content‑removal” obligations under emerging legislation such as the Online Safety Act.

## Victim‑Centric Considerations

The video, shared by Brand’s wife Laura on their YouTube channel, contains a handwritten note in which Brand declares, “I am not guilty of the offences I have been jailed for.” Survivors and advocacy groups have condemned the posting as a re‑victimisation, with Mia Latter, a woman who alleged rape by Brand, describing the message as “upsetting” and “a Christian message alongside absolute lies.” The MoJ’s emphasis on protecting victims aligns with broader UK policy shifts that prioritize survivor voices, yet the continued availability of the video raises questions about the efficacy of unilateral governmental requests when platforms retain editorial autonomy.

## Platform Governance and Enforcement

Google’s refusal is consistent with its prior actions against Brand’s channels, which were suspended from monetisation in 2023 for violating the “creator responsibility policy.” The company’s stance reflects a risk‑averse approach to content that could be deemed extremist or harmful, even when the request originates from a state actor. This incident also spotlights the asymmetry in enforcement: while YouTube retains the video, Meta has disabled Brand’s Instagram, Facebook, and WhatsApp accounts, illustrating divergent moderation standards across the Big Tech stack. The disparity fuels calls for harmonised regulatory frameworks that clarify responsibilities for all major platforms.

## Key facts

- Google declined a formal request from the Prison Service of England and Wales and the Ministry of Justice to remove a YouTube video posted by Russell Brand’s wife.
- The video contains a handwritten note from Brand asserting his innocence of the rape and sexual‑assault convictions that led to a 10½‑year prison sentence.
- The MoJ stated that the request was made under an established mechanism for tech companies to delete material deemed distressing to victims.
- Google said the content did not breach its own policies, leaving the video publicly accessible.
- Brand’s Meta platforms (Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp) were disabled the previous week, while his YouTube channel remains active.
- The case has reignited debate over prisoner speech rights, platform regulation, and the balance between free expression and victim protection.

## Implications

- Potential emboldening of other prisoners or public figures to use platform content for self‑defence, testing the limits of current removal mechanisms.
- Increased pressure on the UK government to clarify legal pathways for content removal, possibly leading to new statutory powers over tech firms.
- Further strain on UK‑EU tech regulatory alignment as divergent approaches to content moderation become more pronounced.
- Possible review of the Online Safety Act’s enforcement provisions, especially concerning state‑initiated removal requests.

## Outlook

The dispute is likely to continue through regulatory dialogues and possible judicial review, with Google’s stance setting a precedent that may influence other jurisdictions facing similar requests. In the near term, the UK may seek to amend its legislative toolkit to give clearer authority for removal, while civil‑society groups will push for stronger safeguards for victims. Ultimately, the case underscores the need for a globally coordinated approach to balancing free speech, prisoner rights, and the protection of survivors in the digital age.

# Israeli opposition accuses Netanyahu of trying to 'delegitimise' election

> [!summary] TL;DR — Israeli opposition and Arab‑led parties condemn Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s claims that foreign actors and Arab parties “stole” the recent election, as his Likud party pushes allegations of Qatari‑Turkish funding and voter bribery. The dispute threatens to block any coalition that includes Arab parties, leaving Netanyahu’s bloc and the opposition’s bloc dead‑locked at 53‑48 seats.

## Background

The 25 Nov 2026 Israeli legislative election produced a fragmented Knesset: Netanyahu’s right‑wing Likud won 53 seats, while the centrist opposition led by Gadi Eisenkot secured 48. Arab parties – the Joint List and Ra’am – surprised analysts by capturing a record 19 seats, positioning themselves as kingmakers. Historically, many Arab Israelis boycotted elections, viewing them as legitimising a system they claim treats them as second‑class citizens. This election’s high turnout was driven by a desire to oust Netanyahu’s historically right‑wing government, raising the stakes for all sides.

## Political Calculus of Delegitimisation

Netanyahu’s Likud has framed the Arab surge as a security threat, alleging that Qatari and Turkish money was funneled to boost Arab voter turnout. By branding the Arab parties as illegitimate, Netanyahu aims to make any coalition involving them politically toxic for his opponents, thereby forcing Eisenkot’s bloc to either accept a right‑wing government or remain in opposition. This tactic mirrors broader global trends where incumbent leaders use foreign‑interference narratives to discredit opposition groups and consolidate power.

## Election Integrity Investigation and Its Limits

The Shin Bet’s probe into alleged illegal funding has been publicly clarified as not implicating entire communities that voted legally. CEC Director Dean Livne stated there was “no basis for systematic violations.” However, the mere existence of the investigation, amplified by Netanyahu’s rhetoric, can erode public confidence in electoral outcomes. The case illustrates how security agencies can become politicised tools, especially when allegations are not fully substantiated before being weaponised.

## AI‑Generated Disinformation and Voter Mobilisation

Netanyahu’s campaign employed AI‑generated images depicting Arab voters at polling stations, aiming to galvanise right‑wing turnout by portraying the Arab surge as an “emergency.” This use of synthetic media reflects the growing intersection of AI technology and electoral politics, raising concerns about deep‑fake manipulation and the spread of misinformation. Regulators and civil‑society groups are increasingly calling for transparency measures and AI‑enabled detection tools to safeguard democratic processes.

## Key facts

- Netanyahu’s Likud won 53 seats; Eisenkot’s opposition bloc secured 48; Arab parties captured 19 seats, a record high.
- Shin Bet is investigating alleged Qatari/Turkish funding of Arab parties; Qatar denies the claims.
- Netanyahu’s campaign used AI‑generated images of Arab voters to mobilise right‑wing supporters.
- The CEC concluded there was no evidence of systematic election‑integrity violations.

## Implications

- Potential deadlock in coalition‑building could force a unity government or early elections.
- The delegitimisation narrative may deepen societal divisions and affect Arab political participation.
- Increased scrutiny of AI‑generated content in elections may spur new regulatory frameworks.
- International actors may view Israel’s democratic health through the lens of these contested claims.

## Outlook

The next few weeks will likely see legal challenges, parliamentary negotiations, and heightened media scrutiny. If Netanyahu’s allegations fail to gain judicial traction, pressure may mount on his coalition to broaden its base, possibly through compromise with centrist parties. Conversely, a successful investigation could further marginalise Arab parties, reshaping Israel’s political landscape for years to come.

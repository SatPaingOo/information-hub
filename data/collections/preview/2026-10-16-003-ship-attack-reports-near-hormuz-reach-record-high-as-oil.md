# Ship attack reports near Hormuz reach record high as oil exports slow

> [!summary] TL;DR — BBC Verify analysis shows a record 12 vessels were struck in the Strait of Hormuz during 5‑11 October, the highest weekly tally since the US‑Israel war with Iran began. The surge has cut daily oil flow through the strait to 12.6 million barrels, down from 15 million, and left two Indian sailors missing. Experts say Tehran is escalating attacks to assert control as the US steps up naval escort missions.

## Background

The Strait of Hormuz, a choke point for roughly one‑third of global oil shipments, has long been a flashpoint in the US‑Israel war with Iran that erupted in February 2024. Since then, maritime incidents have fluctuated, but the past week marks an unprecedented spike. The UK's Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) agency, which collates reports from commercial vessels, recorded 12 attacks in seven days—surpassing the previous weekly high of ten. The escalation coincides with a US Navy initiative to increase the number of escorted tankers through the southern part of the strait, a move intended to protect commercial traffic but which Tehran views as a provocation. The strategic waterway’s importance is underscored by its pre‑war baseline of about 17 million barrels per day, a figure that has not been reached since the conflict began.

## Escalating Iranian Tactics

Iran’s maritime strategy appears to have shifted from sporadic, low‑profile incidents to a near‑daily campaign of strikes. According to Martin Kelly, CEO of Orbital Risk Intelligence, Tehran has “evolved its strategy” and is now conducting “multiple attacks every single day.” The attacks are not limited to the strait itself; recent incidents include a drone‑struck tanker north of Qatar (over 500 km from the strait) and a vessel hit near the UAE’s Al‑Jazirah al‑Hamra. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) has publicly declared that strikes will not be “limited to the Strait of Hormuz” and has announced a “new deployment formation” across the region, signaling a broader operational reach.

## Impact on Global Oil Markets

Industry data from Kpler reveals that oil throughput through the strait fell to 12.6 million barrels per day on 13 October, down from 15 million at the end of September and well below the pre‑war baseline of 17 million. The decline is driven by two factors: the heightened risk premium on tanker insurance and the adoption of a “shuttle” system where smaller tankers load cargo in the Gulf and transfer to larger vessels in the Gulf of Oman to avoid the most dangerous stretch. While this shuttle method has helped keep some flow moving, it adds cost and logistical complexity. The slowdown has ripple effects on oil‑dependent economies, especially in Asia, and raises concerns about energy security ahead of the winter heating season.

## Strategic Responses from the US and Allies

US Central Command (Centcom) has emphasized that “commercial traffic continues to flow through the Strait of Hormuz with coordinated protection from US forces and regional partners.” The US Navy’s assisted transit program, which saw an 85 % increase in the number of vessels receiving navigation assistance during the first ten days of October compared with the same period in August, is a direct response to the surge. Regional partners such as Oman and the United Arab Emirates have offered limited cooperation, but Iran continues to accuse them of facilitating the US‑led escort missions. Meanwhile, the International Maritime Organization (IMO) has called for de‑escalation, warning that unchecked attacks could jeopardize global trade and humanitarian shipping.

## Key facts

- Twelve vessels reported attacks between 5‑11 October, the highest weekly total since the US‑Israel war with Iran began.
- Six attacks occurred on a single day (Sunday, 8 October), including the fire‑damaged Petro Dolphin tanker near Sohar Anchorage, Oman.
- Eleven Indian seafarers were rescued; two remain missing after the Petro Dolphin incident.
- Daily oil flow through the strait dropped to 12.6 million barrels on 13 October, down from 15 million at the end of September.
- US Navy assisted transits rose 85 % in early October compared with the same period in August.

## Implications

- Potential upward pressure on global oil prices and increased volatility in energy markets.
- Higher insurance premiums and re‑routing costs for shipping companies, possibly reshaping trade logistics.
- Escalation risks broader regional conflict, drawing in additional naval forces from other powers.
- Humanitarian concerns as civilian crews become collateral damage and missing persons cases rise.

## Outlook

If the current pattern persists, the Strait of Hormuz could become a permanent high‑risk corridor, prompting a structural shift toward alternative routes such as the Suez Canal or the Cape of Good Hope for a portion of oil shipments. The US and its allies may need to sustain or expand escort operations, while diplomatic channels will be critical to prevent inadvertent escalation. Iran’s strategy appears aimed at coercing the international community into recognizing its maritime claims, but the economic fallout could ultimately undermine its own export revenues. Monitoring of Iranian naval deployments and the effectiveness of assisted transit programs will be essential indicators of whether the recent surge is a temporary spike or the start of a sustained campaign.

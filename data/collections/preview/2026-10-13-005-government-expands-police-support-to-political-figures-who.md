# Government expands police support to political figures who are not MPs

> [!summary] TL;DR — Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood announced new police protection for non‑MP political figures, citing insufficient safeguards after recent high‑profile murders. An urgent independent security review, led by former national security adviser Stephen Lovegrove, will examine physical, online, and societal threats to politicians, peers, and party spokespeople.

## Background

The United Kingdom’s political landscape has become increasingly vulnerable, highlighted by the 2021 murder of Conservative MP Sir David Amess, the 2016 killing of Labour MP Jo Cox, and the more recent fatal attack on Reform UK spokeswoman Ann Widdecombe in August 2024. Data from the Metropolitan Police’s Parliamentary Liaison Team shows a sharp rise in offences against parliamentarians, with recorded allegations climbing from 364 in 2019 to 976 in 2025 and a corresponding increase in death threats. These incidents have intensified calls for a more comprehensive protective framework that extends beyond sitting MPs to include former MPs, members of the House of Lords, party spokespeople, and other public‑facing political figures.

## Scope of the New Protection Measures

The Home Office’s expansion will grant non‑MP political figures access to a designated police officer and broader security assistance. While the precise mechanisms remain to be defined, the initiative signals a shift from a parliamentary‑centric model to a more inclusive approach. This aligns with the government’s recognition that threats can target any individual who influences public policy, regardless of formal legislative status. The move also acknowledges the growing role of party spokespeople and peers in shaping debate, especially in an era of hyper‑charged media cycles and online mobilisation.

## The Independent Review and Its Mandate

Home Secretary Mahmood has commissioned an “root and branch” independent review, to be chaired by Stephen Lovegrove, former National Security Adviser. The review’s brief encompasses three core domains: physical threats (including armed attacks and intimidation), online risks (cyber‑harassment, disinformation, and coordinated trolling), and the broader dangers posed by a polarised society. By involving a former security chief, the government aims to produce actionable recommendations that can be integrated with existing structures such as the Royal and VIP Executive Committee (RAVEC) and the House of Parliament’s security team. The six‑month timeline reflects urgency, but also the complexity of balancing civil liberties with protective oversight.

## Implications for Democratic Resilience

Extending protective measures beyond MPs is a pragmatic response to the evolving threat landscape, yet it raises questions about resource allocation and potential politicisation of security. Critics argue that any preferential treatment for certain political actors could be perceived as partisan, potentially eroding public trust. Conversely, the move may strengthen democratic resilience by ensuring that all voices contributing to public discourse are safeguarded against violence and intimidation. The review’s focus on online harms and societal polarisation also signals an acknowledgment that digital ecosystems are now battlegrounds where political legitimacy is contested, making holistic security strategies essential.

## Key facts

- Shabana Mahmood announced new police support for political figures who are not MPs, including peers, former MPs, and party spokespeople.
- The announcement follows the August 2024 murder of Reform UK spokeswoman Ann Widdecombe, who was killed with a hammer at her Devon home.
- An urgent independent review of security for high‑profile figures, led by former national security adviser Stephen Lovegrove, will report within six months.
- Metropolitan Police data shows alleged offences against MPs rose from 364 in 2019 to 976 in 2025, with death threats increasing from 31 to 50 in the same period.
- The existing protective framework already includes the Royal and VIP Executive Committee (RAVEC) and the House of Parliament’s security team.

## Implications

- Non‑MP political actors will now have formal access to police protection, potentially deterring future attacks and reassuring vulnerable figures.
- The independent review may lead to systemic changes in threat assessment, including greater integration of cyber‑security expertise and community‑based risk mitigation.
- Parliamentary and Home Office resources will be stretched, prompting debate over funding priorities and the balance between protecting individuals and maintaining open political discourse.

## Outlook

If the new protections are effectively implemented and the Lovegrove review delivers concrete, evidence‑based reforms, the United Kingdom could set a benchmark for inclusive political security in democratic societies. However, success will depend on swift coordination between the Home Office, the Metropolitan Police, Parliament’s security apparatus, and the broader tech sector to address online threats. The outcome will shape not only the safety of political actors but also the health of the UK’s democratic debate in an increasingly polarised environment.

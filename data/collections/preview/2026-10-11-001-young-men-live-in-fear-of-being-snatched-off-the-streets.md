# Young men live in fear of being snatched off the streets and sold to Myanmar's army

> [!summary] TL;DR — In Myanmar's southern Karen state, young men are being arbitrarily detained and forced into military service, creating a climate of fear and displacement as the junta intensifies conscription to sustain its war effort.

## Background

Since the 2021 coup, Myanmar has descended into a brutal civil war. The junta’s deteriorating position has prompted a mandatory conscription law targeting men aged 18‑35 and women 18‑27, leading to abductions, extortion, and a humanitarian crisis.

## Conscription Law as a Tool of Attrition

The junta’s new mandatory conscription law, enacted after a series of military setbacks, represents a shift from selective recruitment to a systematic extraction of civilian labor. By legally obligating citizens to serve, the regime can fill depleted ranks without relying solely on volunteer militias. However, the law’s implementation is far from orderly; leaked documents reveal orders to meet aggressive recruitment quotas across all jurisdictions, often through coercion and extrajudicial arrests. This approach mirrors broader patterns of state‑sponsored forced labor observed in other conflict zones, raising concerns about violations of international humanitarian law.

## Humanitarian Impact and Displacement

The human cost is stark. Young men such as Zaw Min in Hpa‑An avoid night travel, live under curfew‑like self‑imposed restrictions, and witness peers being taken from the streets. Families are torn apart, and the psychological toll is amplified by rumors, extortion scams, and the breakdown of trust in local governance. The BBC’s interviews with victims like Ma Moe San Pan illustrate how the conscription machinery infiltrates everyday life, turning ordinary citizens into commodities. The resulting displacement—into Thailand, relatives’ homes, or refugee camps—exacerbates regional instability and creates a refugee outflow that neighboring states must manage.

## Geopolitical and Technological Dimensions

Beyond the immediate humanitarian crisis, the conscription drive intersects with broader geopolitical and technological trends. The junta’s reliance on forced recruitment underscores the limits of traditional diplomatic pressure and highlights the need for advanced monitoring tools, such as satellite imagery and AI‑driven open‑source analysis, to track forced‑labor networks. Moreover, the story aligns with editorial priorities on agentic AI and LLM regulation, as these technologies can be leveraged to detect patterns of abductions and verify testimonies in conflict zones. International rights groups are already employing AI‑enhanced data mining to corroborate missing‑person reports, suggesting a convergence of tech innovation and human‑rights advocacy.

## Key facts

- The junta enacted a mandatory conscription law in 2024, requiring men 18‑35 and women 18‑27 to serve at least two years, with penalties of up to five years imprisonment for refusal.
- Leaked internal orders instruct authorities to meet aggressive recruitment quotas, often through coercion, extortion, and arbitrary arrests of youths in cities like Hpa‑An.
- BBC reporting indicates an estimated 100,000 young recruits have been conscripted over the past two‑and‑a‑half years, many via abduction or sale between local ward officials.
- Open‑source analysis of Facebook missing‑person ads shows a dramatic rise—from four reports in January 2021 to 82 in January 2025—correlating with the implementation of the conscription law.

## Implications

- The systematic use of forced conscription may constitute crimes against humanity, potentially triggering international legal action through bodies such as the International Criminal Court.
- Neighboring countries face increased pressure to manage refugee flows and may need to cooperate on cross‑border security to curb human‑smuggling networks linked to the junta’s recruitment.
- The integration of AI and open‑source intelligence in documenting these abuses could set a precedent for technology‑driven human‑rights monitoring in future conflicts.

## Outlook

If the junta’s attrition strategy continues, the humanitarian crisis will deepen, potentially leading to a broader regional security dilemma. International actors must balance diplomatic engagement with targeted sanctions against those responsible for forced recruitment. Simultaneously, the development of AI‑enabled verification tools and stronger regulation of LLMs and agentic AI will be crucial to ensure that digital platforms are not weaponized for propaganda or to facilitate forced‑labor networks.

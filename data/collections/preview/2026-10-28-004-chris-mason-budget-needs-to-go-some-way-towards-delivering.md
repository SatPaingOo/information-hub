# Chris Mason: Budget needs to go some way towards delivering PM's promises

> [!summary] TL;DR — Chancellor John Healey faces the difficult task of aligning a tight fiscal reality with the Labour government’s ambitious promises on cost‑of‑living support, energy reform, and devolution, all while navigating high debt, inflation, and global shocks.

## Background

The new Labour administration, led by Prime Minister Andy Burnham, entered office pledging a proactive state that would deliver tangible improvements for voters. However, the fiscal backdrop is stark: public debt is at historic highs, the tax burden is generationally elevated, and the UK is grappling with persistent inflation and rising energy costs exacerbated by the Middle‑East conflict. The Institute for Fiscal Studies (IFS) and the Resolution Foundation both stress that the government’s ambitions are constrained by self‑imposed fiscal rules and external pressures, making the upcoming Budget a critical test of political feasibility versus economic reality.

## Fiscal Constraints vs. Political Ambitions

The Budget must reconcile the government’s “proudly proactive” agenda with a limited financial toolkit. Chancellor Healey has already signalled a self‑imposed cap on raising income tax, National Insurance, and VAT, while the IFS notes that the UK’s debt‑to‑GDP ratio leaves little room for expansive spending. Growth, though better than forecast, remains fragile, and the Resolution Foundation warns that Middle‑East conflicts have driven up energy prices and interest rates, further squeezing household finances. In this environment, any significant policy rollout risks destabilising market confidence, forcing the Chancellor to prioritise targeted measures over broad‑based giveaways.

## Energy and Cost‑of‑Living Support

Energy bills are a central battleground. Healey has pledged “long‑term action” to bring down energy costs, potentially funded through a bank levy or surcharge. The plan aims to protect the most vulnerable households, but the effectiveness hinges on the scale of the levy and the speed of implementation. Meanwhile, the minimum wage rise for 18‑20‑year‑olds is modest, reflecting the government’s cautious approach to labour‑market interventions. The Budget’s success will be judged by whether these targeted subsidies can offset inflation without triggering a fiscal blowout.

## Devolution and Local Governance

A hallmark of the Prime Minister’s agenda is “radical devolution” of powers and resources to local leaders. The Chancellor has promised a “really radical devolution of powers and resources to local leaders that are often better placed to get beyond the departmental systems.” However, critics in Westminster warn that extra funding for mayors and local authorities lacks robust safeguards, raising concerns about accountability and efficient spending. The Budget’s devolution package will need to embed clear metrics and oversight mechanisms to ensure that decentralisation translates into tangible outcomes rather than simply shifting fiscal burdens.

## Key facts

- Chancellor John Healey has ruled out raising income tax, National Insurance, and VAT in the upcoming Budget.
- The UK’s national debt is at a record high, with a tax burden that is the highest for a generation.
- Energy prices have risen sharply due to the Middle‑East conflict, prompting a promised bank levy to fund subsidies.
- The minimum wage will increase, but the raise for 18‑20‑year‑olds will be smaller than for older workers.
- The Budget will include a “radical devolution” package, though concerns remain about oversight and accountability.

## Implications

- Targeted cost‑of‑living measures may provide short‑term relief but could be insufficient to curb broader inflation pressures.
- The bank levy and potential surcharges could affect financial sector confidence and lending conditions.
- Devolution without strong fiscal controls risks wasteful spending and could undermine public trust in local government.
- The government’s self‑imposed fiscal constraints may limit its ability to deliver on key campaign promises, potentially affecting voter sentiment ahead of the next election.

## Outlook

The 2026‑27 Budget is likely to be a tempered, pragmatic document that balances the Labour government’s desire for visible action with the harsh realities of a constrained fiscal environment. While the Chancellor will showcase progress on energy affordability and local empowerment, the overall impact will be modest, leaving significant challenges—such as debt reduction and inflation control—to be addressed in future spending reviews.

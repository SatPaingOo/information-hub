# Burnham's Number 10 North goes on multi-million pound hiring spree

> [!summary] TL;DR — Prime Minister Andy Burnham's newly established Number 10 North office in Manchester is launching 50 job adverts, including a 'rewiring the state deputy director', as part of a multi-million-pound expansion aimed at decentralising decision-making. Funded from existing budgets, the move signals a major push to embed regional governance, though opposition parties criticise it as bureaucratic overreach.

## Background

Number 10 North was established in July 2026 by Prime Minister Andy Burnham as a sister office to London’s Downing Street, intended to serve as a regional hub for policy coordination and devolution. Located temporarily in Manchester’s Heron House, the office is set to relocate to a permanent site on First Street in spring 2027. Burnham has framed the initiative as part of a broader effort to 'rewire the state', moving decisions closer to citizens outside Westminster. The office currently employs 120 staff, nearly half the size of No10 London.

## Decentralisation Strategy and Institutional Reform

The hiring spree reflects Burnham’s ambition to institutionalise regional governance through Number 10 North. By embedding policy roles in Manchester, the government seeks to shift operational dynamics away from London-centric civil service structures. The 'rewiring the state deputy director' role, with a salary band of £86,000–£117,000, underscores the strategic importance placed on devolution. This aligns with Burnham’s wider agenda to empower mayors and local councils, potentially reshaping intergovernmental relations in the UK.

## Financial Implications and Civil Service Impact

While the government insists the roles are funded from existing budgets and will not lead to redundancies, the total basic salary cost of £2.8 million—excluding employer pension contributions—raises questions about fiscal prioritisation. The BBC reports that some positions may involve relocations or closures of vacant roles elsewhere, suggesting a reallocation rather than net expansion. Critics argue the lack of transparency around headcounts and attendance undermines accountability.

## Political Opposition and Public Perception

The Conservative Party has dismissed Number 10 North as a 'gimmick', accusing Burnham of expanding bureaucracy without clear justification. Shadow ministers have highlighted the absence of detailed financial disclosures, framing the initiative as emblematic of Labour’s tendency toward centralised control masked as devolution. These critiques resonate amid ongoing debates about civil service efficiency and the equitable distribution of public investment across regions.

## Key facts

- Number 10 North has launched 50 external job adverts, including a 'rewiring the state deputy director' role with a salary band of £86,000–£117,000.
- The total basic salary cost for the new roles is estimated at £2.8 million, excluding employer pension contributions of 28.97%.
- The office currently has 120 staff members, nearly half the size of No10 London’s 280-strong workforce.
- Number 10 North will relocate from Heron House to a new government office on First Street in spring 2027.
- The government claims no redundancies will occur in the wider civil service as a result of the hiring spree.

## Implications

- The expansion of Number 10 North may accelerate the decentralisation of policy-making, potentially altering the balance of power between Westminster and regional authorities.
- Opposition criticism could intensify scrutiny of Labour’s fiscal management and civil service reform agenda, particularly if transparency concerns persist.
- The relocation to First Street in 2027 may catalyse further integration of government functions outside London, influencing future urban development and regional economic strategies.

## Outlook

As Number 10 North transitions to its permanent Manchester base in 2027, its effectiveness in delivering on devolution promises will likely come under increased scrutiny. The success of its initiatives—such as regional summits and mayor engagement programmes—will determine whether the office becomes a transformative institution or remains a symbolic gesture. Meanwhile, political tensions with opposition parties are expected to escalate, particularly around budgetary transparency and civil service reform.

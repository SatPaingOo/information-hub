# No clear winner after tight New Zealand general election

> [!summary] TL;DR — Preliminary results from New Zealand's 2026 general election show a hung parliament with the centre‑right National Party winning 42 seats, 19 short of a majority. Labour secured 36 seats, and smaller parties – Greens, ACT, New Zealand First, the new Opportunity Party and Te Pāti Māori – hold the balance of power, setting the stage for weeks of coalition negotiations.

## Background

New Zealand uses a mixed‑member proportional (MMP) system that almost always produces coalition governments. The 2026 campaign was dominated by cost‑of‑living pressures, rising unemployment and a record outflow of citizens to Australia. With more than 99 % of votes counted, the two major parties together captured only about 60 % of the party vote – a historic low – reflecting growing voter fragmentation and the entry of the centrist Opportunity Party, which crossed the 5 % threshold for the first time.

## Fragmented mandate and the role of minor parties

The combined seat count of National (42) and its traditional partners ACT (13) and New Zealand First (12) yields 67 seats, still short of the 61‑seat majority needed in the 120‑seat House. Labour (36) plus the Greens (16) and Te Pāti Māori (5) also fall short at 57. The decisive bloc is the Opportunity Party (7 seats), which has declared willingness to negotiate with either side. Professor Andrew Geddis notes that the party’s ‘wildcard’ status means the final government could hinge on a few thousand special votes still to be counted, especially in marginal electorates.

## Economic discontent driving voter volatility

Polling by Roy Morgan shows cost of living as the top issue for a majority of voters. Julian McCrann highlights that Prime Minister Christopher Luxon’s 2023 promise to improve living standards has not materialised, with more than half of New Zealanders now saying the country is no longer a ‘land of opportunity’. Record net migration to Australia underscores the depth of economic anxiety, feeding support for newer parties that pitch generational renewal and pragmatic policy over ideological loyalty.

## Coalition dynamics under MMP

MMP incentivises post‑election bargaining rather than pre‑election alliances. Both Luxon and Hipkins have signalled a caretaker government while special votes are processed (due 27 November). The Greens’ proposal to force supermarket divestments into public ownership adds a policy flashpoint that could either attract or repel potential partners. The need for compromise across a broad ideological spectrum – from free‑market ACT to Māori‑focused Te Pāti Māori – suggests any coalition agreement will be fragile and policy‑heavy.

## Implications for New Zealand’s international posture

A prolonged formation period may delay foreign‑policy decisions, notably on trade negotiations with the Indo‑Pacific Economic Framework and climate commitments at COP30. However, the presence of the Opportunity Party, which advocates a ‘future‑focused’ agenda, could push any government toward a more multilateral, technology‑oriented stance, aligning with broader Western efforts to diversify supply chains away from China.

## Key facts

- National Party 42 seats; Labour 36 seats; Greens 16; ACT 13; New Zealand First 12; Opportunity Party 7; Te Pāti Māori 5.
- Combined major‑party vote share ~60 %, a historic low under MMP.
- Opportunity Party crossed the 5 % threshold for the first time and is open to either bloc.
- Special votes (including overseas) remain uncounted; official result due 27 November.
- Cost of living and unemployment were the dominant campaign issues.

## Implications

- Extended coalition talks could create a policy vacuum lasting several weeks.
- Minor parties gain disproportionate leverage, potentially reshaping economic and social policy.
- Voter fragmentation signals a long‑term shift away from two‑party dominance in New Zealand politics.
- Uncertainty may affect investor confidence and delay trade‑policy initiatives.

## Outlook

The next three weeks will be decisive as special votes are tallied and party leaders enter formal negotiations. The most probable outcome is a centre‑right coalition anchored by National, ACT and New Zealand First, with the Opportunity Party providing confidence‑and‑supply or a formal partnership. However, a centre‑left arrangement led by Labour, the Greens and Te Pāti Māori, also backed by Opportunity, cannot be ruled out. Either scenario will produce a government reliant on cross‑party compromise, making policy implementation slower but potentially more durable.

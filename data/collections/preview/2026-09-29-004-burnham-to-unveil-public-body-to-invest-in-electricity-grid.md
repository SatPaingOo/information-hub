# Burnham to unveil public body to invest in electricity grid

> [!summary] TL;DR — Andy Burnham, Labour’s leader, will launch a new publicly‑owned entity – the Great British Grid – to accelerate transmission projects and bring UK energy costs in line with Europe within a decade, while also confronting long‑standing social‑care funding and pension‑policy debates.

## Background

The UK’s electricity network is ageing, and the shift from fossil‑fuel stations to remote renewable installations (offshore wind, solar farms) demands an estimated £70 bn of grid investment between 2025 and 2031 – a four‑fold increase over current spending. Delays in connection projects have grown “significantly”, according to the National Audit Office, creating a cost crisis that is “crippling for businesses”. Burnham’s speech at the Labour Party conference in Liverpool is intended to position the new Great British Grid (GB Grid) as the cornerstone of a broader public‑control agenda, complementing existing public assets such as GB Energy. The move also sits within a wider fiscal context: the upcoming Budget faces tighter borrowing headroom, and the government is under pressure to fund universal social‑care services in England and to reconsider the triple‑lock pension uprating, which could add billions to future pension costs.

## Policy Design and Governance

The proposed Great British Grid will operate as a publicly‑owned company that can invest in, and compete with, the three private firms currently owning and managing the UK transmission network. By integrating with GB Energy’s existing budget, the new body aims to streamline project pipelines, reduce bureaucratic bottlenecks, and introduce market‑based competition to drive down costs. Burnham’s emphasis on an “honest conversation” and tackling “difficult issues” signals a shift toward more transparent, state‑led intervention in critical infrastructure, a departure from the previous reliance on private‑sector delivery. The policy also seeks to lower barriers for firms awaiting grid connections, offering co‑investment and financing support, which could accelerate the rollout of green energy projects and improve energy security.

## Geopolitical and Economic Implications

Accelerating grid investment aligns with broader European Union targets for renewable energy integration and carbon neutrality, positioning the UK as a competitive player in the emerging clean‑tech market. Faster connections could reduce reliance on imported electricity during peak demand, enhancing energy resilience amid geopolitical uncertainties such as fluctuating gas supplies and potential supply chain disruptions for turbine components. Domestically, the £70 bn investment programme may stimulate job creation and regional development, particularly in coastal and offshore areas where wind farms are being developed. However, the fiscal burden raises questions about the sustainability of public finances, especially as the government also pursues universal social‑care funding and faces speculation over reforming the triple‑lock pension, which could affect voter sentiment ahead of the next general election.

## Market Dynamics and Investment Outlook

The introduction of a state‑backed investor in transmission is likely to intensify competition, prompting existing private operators to improve efficiency and cost‑effectiveness. Financial markets may view the new entity as a stable, long‑term investor, potentially lowering borrowing costs for future infrastructure projects. At the same time, the policy’s success hinges on effective coordination with Ofgem and other regulators to avoid duplication and ensure compliance with EU‑aligned energy directives. If the GB Grid can deliver on its promise of faster hook‑ups, the resulting cost reductions could benefit downstream consumers and businesses, fostering economic growth. Conversely, delays, mis‑allocation of funds, or political backlash over pension reforms could undermine confidence and stall the ambitious ten‑year cost‑alignment goal.

## Key facts

- Andy Burnham will announce the creation of a new publicly‑owned body, the Great British Grid, in his first Labour leadership conference speech.
- The GB Grid will invest in transmission projects and compete with the three private firms currently operating the UK’s electricity network.
- Funding will be drawn from the existing budget of GB Energy, the Labour‑established green investment vehicle headquartered in Aberdeen.
- Ofgem estimates £70 bn of grid investment is needed between 2025‑2031, a four‑fold increase over current spending rates.
- The National Audit Office reports a significant rise in projects awaiting grid connections, highlighting growing delays.
- Burnham also signalled potential reforms to the triple‑lock pension and plans to fund universal social‑care services in England.

## Implications

- Increased public control over critical energy infrastructure could accelerate renewable integration and reduce long‑term energy costs.
- Enhanced competition may drive down transmission prices, benefiting businesses and consumers.
- Potential fiscal pressure from simultaneous social‑care and pension reforms could limit the budget available for grid investment.
- The policy positions the UK in alignment with EU clean‑energy objectives, potentially influencing future trade and regulatory relationships.

## Outlook

If the Great British Grid delivers on its promise of faster, cheaper connections, it could become a model for state‑led infrastructure investment, bolstering the UK’s energy transition and economic competitiveness. Success will depend on robust governance, clear regulatory frameworks, and the ability to navigate political sensitivities around pension policy and social‑care funding. Failure to meet investment targets or to manage fiscal trade‑offs could stall the ten‑year cost‑alignment ambition and erode public confidence in Labour’s broader utility‑reform agenda.

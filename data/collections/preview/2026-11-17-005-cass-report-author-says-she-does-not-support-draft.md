# Cass report author says she does not support draft conversion practices bill

> [!summary] TL;DR — Baroness Cass, author of the independent review on gender identity services for young people, has withdrawn her support for the UK’s draft bill banning conversion practices, warning that its current wording could cause unintended legal harm to parents and clinicians. The government acknowledges her concerns and says it will carefully consider the committee’s evidence before finalising the legislation.

## Background

The United Kingdom has long sought to criminalise conversion practices—interventions aimed at altering a person’s sexual orientation or gender identity—citing the profound psychological damage they inflict. In its 2024 manifesto, the Labour Party pledged to outlaw such practices, prompting the government to publish a draft bill in June 2026. Baroness Cass, who led the independent review of gender identity services for young people, initially endorsed the proposal, praising its clearer definition of prohibited activities. However, during a parliamentary committee scrutinising the draft, she reversed her stance, citing risks of “serious unintended consequences” for families and health professionals.

## Legal drafting and unintended consequences

Baroness Cass highlighted that the bill’s definition of “serious harm” could capture a broad spectrum of actions, from parental decisions about gender‑affirming care to routine therapeutic conversations. The phrase “serious alarm or distress to the individual which has a substantial adverse effect on their usual day‑to‑day activities” is ambiguous and could be interpreted to include everyday parental guidance, thereby exposing caregivers to criminal liability. This legal overreach threatens to chill legitimate support for gender‑distressed youth and deter clinicians from providing evidence‑based care.

## Impact on healthcare providers and NHS services

Professionals working in the new NHS Gender Dysphoria Clinics expressed concern that even unsubstantiated complaints could trigger investigations, damaging reputations and diverting resources. The fear is that the bill’s broad scope may criminalise therapeutic dialogue essential for trauma‑informed care, especially when a young person has experienced sexual abuse. Such a chilling effect could undermine the very services the legislation intends to protect.

## Political and public policy considerations

The government’s initial enthusiasm for a swift ban was driven by a decade‑long delay in protecting LGBT+ individuals from abusive conversion practices. However, Baroness Cass’s reversal forces a recalibration of the policy narrative. Policymakers now face the challenge of balancing robust protection against abuse with safeguarding parental rights and clinical autonomy. The forthcoming committee report will be pivotal in shaping a more nuanced legislative framework that avoids both impunity for abusers and inadvertent criminalisation of supportive actions.

## Key facts

- Baroness Cass authored the 2024 independent review of gender identity services for young people and initially praised the draft conversion practices bill.
- During a parliamentary committee hearing, she publicly withdrew her support, stating the bill’s wording could cause “serious unintended consequences” for parents and therapists.
- The bill defines prohibited conduct as causing “serious harm to the individual's physical or mental health, or serious alarm or distress…with a substantial adverse effect on usual day‑to‑day activities.”
- NHS Gender Dysphoria Clinics warn that even unfounded complaints could jeopardise clinicians and limit essential therapeutic conversations.
- The government has welcomed her comments and affirmed it will take all committee evidence seriously before finalising the legislation.

## Implications

- Potential legal exposure for parents who make decisions about gender‑affirming treatments, possibly deterring necessary support for transgender youth.
- Increased risk of litigation and professional burnout for NHS clinicians, potentially reducing the availability of gender‑affirming care.
- A delay in enacting the ban, as the government re‑examines the draft to address the identified drafting flaws.
- Broader debate on the balance between protecting vulnerable groups and preserving parental and professional discretion in healthcare.

## Outlook

The draft conversion practices bill now faces a period of intensive scrutiny. Baroness Cass’s testimony has forced the government to confront the complexities of legislating on a highly sensitive issue. Expect revised language that narrows the definition of criminal conduct while preserving the core intent of protecting LGBT+ individuals from coercive practices. Stakeholders—including advocacy groups, healthcare providers, and families—will likely engage in further consultations, potentially extending the timeline for enactment beyond the original 2026 target. The final legislation will serve as a test case for how democratic societies navigate the tension between safeguarding rights and avoiding over‑criminalisation.

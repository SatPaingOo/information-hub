# ‘Constantly on my mind’ – 9/11 agony goes on for bereaved, 25 years on

> [!summary] TL;DR — A quarter‑century after 9/11, families still await justice as the trial of alleged mastermind Khalid Sheikh Mohammed stalls, with procedural delays and evidentiary challenges threatening to outlive the victims.

## Background

On 11 September 2001 al‑Qaeda operatives hijacked four commercial aircraft, crashing two into the World Trade Center, one into the Pentagon and a fourth in Pennsylvania after passenger resistance. The attacks killed 2,977 victims, triggered the U.S. "War on Terror" and led to the creation of the Guantanamo Bay detention facility. Khalid Sheikh Mohammed (KSM), identified by U.S. intelligence as the chief architect of the plot, has been held at Guantanamo since 2006 without a final trial. Over the past two decades, a series of pre‑trial hearings, evidentiary disputes and judicial turnovers have delayed the case, while the families of victims continue to seek closure.

## Legal Gridlock and the Role of Torture Evidence

The most significant obstacle to a speedy trial is the admissibility of evidence obtained under the CIA’s "enhanced interrogation" program. In June 2028 a military judge excluded KSM’s confessions, labeling the CIA’s methods as "extraordinary physical and mental abuse" and deeming the statements involuntary. International law scholars, such as Kasey McCall‑Smith of Edinburgh University, argue that the torture taint extends to any derivative evidence, forcing prosecutors to rely on a patchwork of intercepted communications, forensic data and witness testimony. This evidentiary uncertainty fuels repeated motions to suppress, prolonging pre‑trial phases and eroding confidence in the military commission system.

## Victim Advocacy and the Human Cost of Delay

Families like the Restas and the Gerhardts have become de‑facto watchdogs, attending every hearing and lobbying Congress for a civilian federal trial. Their testimonies highlight a psychological toll: aging parents fear they will die before seeing a verdict, while younger relatives grapple with intergenerational trauma. The lottery system that determines courtroom access, the thick glass barrier separating victims from defendants, and the rotating roster of five military judges underscore a process perceived as distant and opaque. These dynamics have spurred calls for a transparent civilian court, arguing that a legitimate, public trial would better honor the 2,976 victims listed on the charge sheet.

## Geopolitical Ramifications and U.S. Credibility

The prolonged Guantanamo case reverberates beyond domestic policy. Allies in NATO and the European Union have repeatedly criticized the United States for maintaining a detention regime that appears to contravene the Convention Against Torture. The case also influences U.S. soft power in the Middle East, where perceptions of American justice are tied to broader counter‑terrorism narratives. A successful, law‑based prosecution could reinforce the rule‑of‑law argument championed by successive administrations, whereas continued delays risk reinforcing narratives of impunity and double standards, potentially emboldening extremist recruitment.

## Key facts

- Khalid Sheikh Mohammed has been detained at Guantanamo Bay for 20 years without conviction
- A trial date was set for June 2028 but has been jeopardized by the exclusion of CIA‑derived confessions
- More than 2,970 victims are named in the indictment, including families still awaiting justice
- Five different military judges have overseen the pre‑trial process
- Victims must win a lottery to attend hearings, which are conducted behind bullet‑proof glass

## Implications

- Legal precedent: The handling of torture‑tainted evidence will shape future terrorism prosecutions in both military and civilian courts
- Domestic politics: Congressional pressure may increase for a civilian trial venue, influencing defense‑budget allocations for Guantanamo
- International relations: Continued delays could strain U.S. ties with allies advocating for human‑rights compliance
- Victim policy: Growing advocacy may lead to legislative reforms granting families greater procedural rights and compensation mechanisms

## Outlook

If the military commission can secure sufficient admissible evidence, a trial could commence by late 2029, but the likelihood of further postponements remains high. Legislative initiatives in the 118th Congress propose transferring the case to a federal district court, which would require congressional approval and could reset procedural timelines. Meanwhile, the aging cohort of victims underscores a narrowing window for direct familial participation. International watchdogs are expected to intensify scrutiny of Guantanamo’s legal framework, potentially prompting executive action to either close the facility or overhaul its judicial processes.

# Yemen's government announces all-out war to reclaim land from Houthis

> [!summary] TL;DR — Yemen's internationally recognized government declared an all‑out offensive to retake territory seized by the Iran‑backed Houthis, following intensified strikes on Saudi oil infrastructure and a Houthi offensive that now controls the Red Sea coast. The move raises the risk of a broader regional conflict, threatens global oil supplies, and deepens a humanitarian crisis in one of the world’s poorest nations.

## Background

The conflict erupted in 2014 when the Houthis seized Sanaa and expanded northwards, prompting Saudi Arabia to lead an international coalition in 2015. After years of stalemate and periodic flare‑ups, the Houthis launched a rapid offensive in July 2026, capturing the entire Red Sea coastline, including the strategic Bab al‑Mandab Strait. This cut off a key alternative oil export route for Saudi Arabia and gave the Houthis leverage over global shipping. In response, the Saudi‑led coalition and the Yemeni government have intensified aerial strikes, while the Houthis have escalated attacks on Saudi economic targets, notably Aramco facilities, using ballistic missiles and drones. The latest exchange of strikes, involving dozens of attacks in 12 hours, has pushed the Yemeni president, Rashad al‑Alimi, to declare a comprehensive military campaign to recover all Houthi‑held territory, a move that signals a new phase of the war with far‑reaching regional and economic ramifications.

## Military Dynamics and Tactical Shifts

The declaration of an all‑out war reflects a strategic recalibration by the Yemeni government and its Saudi backers, moving from a defensive posture to a concerted offensive aimed at reversing Houthi gains. Recent Houthi successes—most notably the seizure of the Red Sea coast and the cutting of the Taiz‑Aden supply route—demonstrated their ability to project power beyond their northern stronghold. In response, government forces, backed by Saudi air power and intelligence, are concentrating on weakening Houthi military capabilities through targeted strikes on command centers, weapons depots, and supply lines. The Houthis, in turn, are leveraging asymmetric tactics, including precision missile strikes on high‑value Saudi oil sites, to erode Saudi economic resilience and force a negotiated settlement on their terms. The frontline remains fluid, with Taiz and surrounding areas becoming a critical pivot point; control of this city would re‑open a vital north‑south corridor and potentially shift momentum in favor of the government.

## Geopolitical Implications for Regional Powers

The escalation directly involves Iran, which provides financial, logistical, and military support to the Houthis, and Saudi Arabia, which backs the Yemeni government and has a vested interest in protecting its oil infrastructure. Iran’s backing underscores its broader strategy of projecting power through proxy groups, especially in the strategic Bab al‑Mandab waterway that links the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden. A prolonged conflict could compel Iran to increase its involvement, possibly supplying advanced weaponry or advisory units, thereby raising the risk of a direct confrontation with Saudi Arabia and its allies, including the United States. Moreover, the disruption of oil flows through alternative routes threatens global energy markets, prompting oil‑dependent economies to diversify supplies and potentially accelerating the transition toward renewable energy sources. The United Nations and humanitarian organizations have warned that intensified fighting will exacerbate the already severe humanitarian crisis, with millions facing food insecurity, disease outbreaks, and displacement.

## Humanitarian and Economic Fallout

Yemen’s pre‑conflict GDP was already among the lowest globally, and the war has left over 80 % of the population dependent on humanitarian aid. The latest offensive is expected to displace additional civilians, strain already overwhelmed refugee camps, and disrupt essential services such as health care and clean water supplies. The Houthi‑controlled coastline has become a lifeline for illicit trade and arms smuggling, further complicating relief efforts. Economically, the conflict impacts both local and global actors: Saudi Arabia’s oil production has faced intermittent disruptions, leading to volatility in world oil prices and prompting emergency releases from strategic petroleum reserves. International shipping lanes, including the Bab al‑Mandab and adjacent ports, face heightened insurance costs and potential rerouting, which could increase freight costs for goods traveling between Asia and Europe. The humanitarian and economic dimensions together create a feedback loop that could prolong the conflict and make post‑war reconstruction even more daunting.

## Key facts

- President Rashad al‑Alimi announced a full‑scale offensive to retake all Houthi‑held territory, marking a new phase in the 2014‑present conflict.
- The Houthis have seized the entire Red Sea coast, cutting off a key Saudi oil export route and controlling the Bab al‑Mandab Strait.
- In a single day, the Houthis claimed over 50 strikes on Saudi coalition positions, including a ballistic missile and drone attack on Aramco sites in Riyadh and Khurais.
- Saudi Arabia has yet to comment publicly on the Houthi attack on Aramco, but has previously dismissed similar claims as misleading.

## Implications

- Escalation raises the risk of a broader Saudi‑Iran proxy war, potentially drawing in other regional and global powers.
- Disruptions to oil production and shipping could trigger further spikes in global energy prices and affect economic recovery in oil‑dependent nations.

## Outlook

If the Yemeni government’s offensive gains momentum, it could gradually erode Houthi control and restore a degree of territorial integrity, though a swift victory is unlikely given the Houthis’ entrenched positions and external support. Conversely, a stalemate or Houthi counter‑offensive could cement their hold on the coast, forcing Saudi Arabia to consider a long‑term military presence and deepening the humanitarian crisis. International actors will likely increase diplomatic pressure to negotiate a cease‑fire, while the United Nations may intensify humanitarian aid operations to mitigate civilian suffering. The conflict’s trajectory will continue to influence regional power dynamics, energy markets, and the broader fight against extremism in the Horn of Africa.

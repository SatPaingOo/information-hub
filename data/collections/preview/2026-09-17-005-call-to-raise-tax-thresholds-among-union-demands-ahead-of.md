# Call to raise tax thresholds among union demands ahead of Budget

> [!summary] TL;DR — Unions that donate to Labour are meeting Chancellor John Healey to press demands such as unfreezing tax thresholds, reversing winter‑fuel cuts, taxing big tech, and advancing North Sea oil projects. The push aims to restore fairness for lower‑paid workers and signal a new fiscal direction for the government.

## Background

The UK’s tax thresholds have been frozen since the 2022‑23 fiscal year, with then‑Chancellor Rachel Reeves extending the freeze until 2031. This has pulled many low‑ and middle‑income earners into higher tax brackets without corresponding adjustments for inflation. Simultaneously, the Conservative‑led government’s 2023 winter‑fuel allowance cut—restricted to the poorest pensioners—has been condemned by Labour’s union allies as politically toxic. As the Labour government prepares its first full Budget, major unions (Unite, Unison, GMB) and the TUC are leveraging their financial ties to Labour to shape fiscal policy, arguing that the upcoming Budget will define Prime Minister Keir Starmer’s economic agenda.

## Tax Threshold Freeze and Its Socio‑Economic Impact

The freeze on personal tax thresholds—currently at £12,571 for the basic rate and £50,271 for the higher rate—has created a "stealth tax" that disproportionately affects lower‑paid workers. Over the past few years, inflation has eroded real wages, yet the thresholds have not been uprated, pushing more employees into the 20 % and 40 % brackets. Unite’s leader Sharon Graham highlighted that the basic threshold, had it risen with inflation, would now be above £16,000, meaning many on the lower end are paying "eye‑watering" amounts. This fiscal drag has broader implications for consumer spending, public‑sector morale, and the government’s credibility on fairness. Reversing the freeze would require either a one‑off adjustment or a gradual uprating, both of which would have significant budgetary consequences and could be offset by targeted revenue measures such as higher taxes on tech firms or a wealth‑tax review.

## Union Leverage and Labour’s Strategic Calculus

Unite, Unison, GMB, and the TUC have a combined donation history that makes them influential interlocutors for the Labour Party. Their meeting with Chancellor John Healey on Thursday is not merely a policy discussion; it is a strategic negotiation over the government’s early legislative identity. The unions are demanding a clear break from the “continuity Starmer” perception, pushing for policies that resonate with the working‑class base. Key proposals include: (1) unfreezing tax thresholds to deliver immediate relief; (2) reinstating winter‑fuel payments for a broader cohort, a move framed as “winter fuel in reverse”; (3) imposing additional taxes on large tech companies to fund public‑sector investment and green transition; (4) fast‑tracking North Sea oil and gas projects (Rosebank and Jackdaw) to secure jobs and energy security; and (5) commissioning a Treasury study on shifting taxation from income toward wealth. These demands reflect a dual objective: alleviating fiscal pressure on ordinary citizens while generating new revenue streams that can be politically palatable to a Labour government keen on investment and climate goals.

## Fiscal and Political Implications for the Budget

If Healey adopts union‑backed measures, the Budget could mark a decisive shift toward a more progressive fiscal stance, potentially redefining the UK’s economic narrative. Raising tax thresholds would provide a visible boost to disposable income, addressing the “cost‑of‑living” crisis that has dominated British politics. However, the fiscal cost of such a reversal—estimated at several billion pounds—must be balanced against new revenue sources. The proposed tech‑sector levy could generate significant funds, but it risks diplomatic friction with the US and could affect investment climates. Meanwhile, the push for oil and gas projects aligns with energy security concerns but clashes with climate commitments, creating a policy tension that the government must navigate. Additionally, the TUC’s call to revise the OBR’s remit to factor long‑term investments could alter fiscal transparency and forecasting, potentially enabling more ambitious spending plans. Politically, meeting union demands could solidify Labour’s support among its traditional base, yet failure to deliver could expose the government to accusations of broken promises, especially as by‑elections loom in key marginal seats.

## Key facts

- Unite, the UK’s second‑largest union, is demanding an end to the tax‑threshold freeze that has pushed low‑paid workers into higher tax brackets.
- The winter‑fuel allowance cut—implemented under former PM Keir Starmer’s government—has been labeled a "disgrace" by Unite’s leader Sharon Graham.
- Unison is calling for higher taxes on large tech companies and a Treasury study on shifting taxation from income to wealth.
- GMB leader Gary Smith advocates fast‑tracking the Rosebank and Jackdaw North Sea oil and gas fields to boost jobs and energy security.
- The TUC wants the OBR’s remit revised to include long‑term investment valuations, not just short‑term fiscal balancing.

## Implications

- An unfreeze of tax thresholds would provide immediate financial relief to millions of lower‑income households, potentially stimulating consumer demand.
- Increased taxes on tech firms could fund public‑sector investment and green initiatives but may strain UK‑US tech relations.
- Advancing oil and gas projects could create jobs and reduce import reliance, yet risks undermining climate targets and alienating environmental voters.
- Reforming the OBR’s remit could enable more ambitious fiscal planning but may reduce transparency and market confidence.

## Outlook

The Budget is likely to be a pivotal moment for Labour’s economic credibility. If Healey can balance union‑driven relief measures with sustainable revenue generation, the government may cement its reputation as a champion of ordinary workers while maintaining fiscal discipline. Conversely, a failure to deliver on key demands could erode support among core constituencies and set the stage for political setbacks in upcoming by‑elections.

# Reform confident it won't need to repay any of £72m under proposed new donor rules

> [!summary] TL;DR — Reform UK insists its £72m haul from crypto billionaires Ben Delo and Christopher Harborne is legal under both current and forthcoming rules, even as the government moves to cap overseas donations retrospectively. Housing Secretary Angela Rayner warns non‑compliant donors may have to return funds, fuelling a broader debate over mega‑donors and democratic integrity.

## Background

The United Kingdom has long grappled with the influence of large political donations, particularly from wealthy individuals and foreign‑linked sources. In March 2026, a review led by former civil servant Philip Rycroft highlighted vulnerabilities in the existing regime, prompting the government to table amendments to the Representation of the People Bill. These changes seek to limit donations from British expatriates, introducing a £100,000 cap backdated to 25 March and establishing a minimum residency period for the cap to apply. The proposals have reignited a cross‑party conversation about the need for tighter controls on "mega‑donors" and the protection of democratic processes from undue financial influence. Reform UK’s recent influx of funds from two crypto tycoons—each contributing £36m—places the party at the centre of this regulatory showdown.

## Legal Interpretation and Residency Definitions

Reform UK’s economic spokesman, Robert Jenrick, argues that the £72m donations from Ben Delo and Christopher Harborne comply with both existing law and the forthcoming amendments. The crux of the dispute lies in the retrospective nature of the new cap and how "UK residency" is defined. While the government intends to adopt a tax‑law‑based definition, the complexity of residency rules—especially for individuals who have lived abroad but retain strong ties—creates ambiguity. Legal scholars note that retrospective legislation is permissible in the UK if it is clear and within the scope of parliamentary sovereignty, yet the practical enforcement hinges on the Electoral Commission’s interpretation and the willingness of donors to voluntarily return funds. The situation is further complicated by the fact that both donors have already returned to the UK, ostensibly to comply with the anticipated changes, blurring the line between proactive compliance and legal necessity.

## Political Implications for Reform and the Broader Party System

For Reform UK, the donations represent a financial lifeline that has vaulted the party into the ranks of well‑funded political actors, surpassing the total receipts of all other UK parties in the previous year. The party’s confidence that the money is secure bolsters its narrative of being a viable alternative to the established parties, yet it also makes Reform a target for scrutiny. Opposition parties and advocacy groups have seized on the episode to call for stricter caps and greater transparency across the board. The Labour government’s Housing Secretary, Angela Rayner, has taken a hard line, signalling that non‑compliant donors could be forced to return funds, a stance that could set a precedent for future regulatory actions. Meanwhile, the Conservative shadow cabinet, including Tom Tugendhat, warns against an "Americanisation" of politics, while the Liberal Democrats and Green Party push for emergency legislation to limit campaign spending. The political fallout could reshape fundraising strategies across the spectrum, prompting parties to diversify revenue streams and reduce reliance on a handful of mega‑donors.

## Broader Regulatory Landscape and Future Outlook

The UK’s move follows a global trend toward tighter political finance regulation, exemplified by reforms in the United States, Canada, and several European democracies. The government’s newly formed taskforce on mega‑donors is expected to deliver recommendations later in the year, potentially leading to further restrictions beyond the £100,000 cap. International observers note that the UK’s approach balances the need to protect democratic integrity with the principle of free political expression. However, the retrospective application of the cap raises concerns about legal certainty and could deter future cross‑border political engagement. Moreover, the involvement of cryptocurrency‑derived wealth adds a layer of complexity, as the provenance of such funds can be opaque. Stakeholders—including the Electoral Commission, advocacy groups like the Open Rights Group, and industry bodies—are likely to influence the final shape of the legislation, aiming to strike a balance between preventing undue influence and preserving the ability of individuals to support political causes they believe in.

## Key facts

- Reform UK received a total of £72m from two crypto billionaires, Ben Delo and Christopher Harborne, each donating £36m within two days.
- The donations were made while both men were residing overseas, though they have since returned to the UK.
- The government’s proposed changes to the Representation of the People Bill introduce a £100,000 cap on donations from British expatriates, backdated to 25 March 2026.
- Housing Secretary Angela Rayner stated that the rule changes are retrospective and that non‑compliant donors may be required to return funds.
- Reform UK’s spokesman, Robert Jenrick, maintains that the donations are fully compliant with both current law and the anticipated future rules.
- The government’s taskforce on mega‑donors is currently investigating broader political finance reforms, with potential additional caps under consideration.

## Implications

- If the Electoral Commission rules the donations non‑compliant, Reform UK could face a significant financial shortfall, potentially reshaping the party’s electoral prospects.
- The retrospective nature of the cap may set a legal precedent that could affect other sectors beyond political financing, influencing how future regulatory changes are drafted and enforced.
- Increased scrutiny of crypto‑derived political funding may prompt calls for stricter provenance reporting and anti‑money‑laundering checks in political donations.

## Outlook

The legislative journey of the Representation of the People Bill will likely involve intense scrutiny in the House of Lords, with amendments possible to refine residency definitions and the scope of the cap. In the interim, political parties are expected to diversify fundraising, while the government’s taskforce may propose additional measures such as donation caps for domestic donors and enhanced transparency requirements. The outcome will have lasting ramifications for the balance between financial freedom of expression and the safeguarding of democratic processes in the UK.

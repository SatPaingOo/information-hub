# Landlady to 9/11 hijackers reveals their links to alleged Saudi spy

> [!summary] TL;DR — A former property manager in San Diego publicly describes how she interacted with Omar al‑Bayoumi, a Saudi national later accused of aiding the 9/11 plotters, shedding new light on the alleged Saudi intelligence involvement that underpins a $1 trillion lawsuit against the Kingdom.

## Background

The September 11, 2001 attacks reshaped global security policy and sparked decades of legal battles seeking accountability from state actors. Central to those efforts is a sprawling civil lawsuit filed by thousands of victims’ families and survivors, alleging that Saudi Arabia’s government and its intelligence services provided material support to the 19 al‑Qaeda operatives who hijacked the four aircraft. Omar al‑Bayoumi, a Saudi resident of San Diego who lived in the same apartment complex as two of the hijackers, Nawaf al‑Hazmi and Khalid al‑Mihdhar, has been a focal point of those claims. For 25 years, the only person who publicly linked Bayoumi to the hijackers remained silent—until Holly Ratchford, the complex’s former manager, broke her silence for a BBC investigation, offering sworn testimony that could influence both the litigation and broader geopolitical discourse on state‑sponsored terrorism.

## Legal and Diplomatic Ramifications

Ratchford’s sealed declaration adds a firsthand, eyewitness component to a case that has already produced a landmark ruling by U.S. District Judge Rita Lin. In 2023, the judge concluded that there was a “reasonable inference” that Bayoumi and a Saudi diplomat based in the United States were coordinating with the Saudi government while assisting the hijackers. That finding allowed the lawsuit to proceed, prompting Saudi Arabia to file an appeal and maintain a blanket denial of any involvement. Ratchford’s testimony could reinforce the plaintiffs’ argument that the Kingdom’s alleged support extended beyond mere financial assistance to include logistical facilitation—potentially affecting the outcome of the appeal and future diplomatic negotiations. Moreover, the timing of the disclosure, coinciding with renewed scrutiny of foreign influence operations, may embolden other litigants to come forward, further complicating Saudi Arabia’s legal defenses and its efforts to rehabilitate its international image.

## Intelligence Community Implications

The narrative also raises questions about the efficacy of pre‑9/11 intelligence sharing and the detection of covert operatives within the United States. According to former FBI investigator Richard Lambert, the San Diego Muslim community widely suspected Bayoumi of being a Saudi intelligence operative due to his constant videotaping of religious activities and probing inquiries. Yet, official U.S. agencies failed to act on those community observations, highlighting a systemic blind spot that allowed suspected foreign agents to embed themselves in civilian life. The case underscores the challenges of balancing civil liberties with proactive surveillance, especially when cultural and religious communities become inadvertent informants. It also invites a re‑examination of the FBI’s post‑9/11 reforms, particularly the emphasis on human‑source recruitment and the integration of community‑level intelligence—a domain where Ratchford’s inadvertent role as a witness illustrates both the promise and peril of grassroots information sharing.

## Human Dimension and Media Narrative

Beyond the legal and intelligence dimensions, Ratchford’s story humanizes a tragedy that has been largely framed through political and juridical lenses. Her recollections of Bayoumi as a “bubbly” family man who hosted pool parties and invited her for meals illustrate how terror plots can be concealed within ordinary social interactions. The emotional toll on Ratchford—her lingering sense of betrayal and the personal risk she took by speaking out—adds a poignant layer to the broader discourse on victimhood, which traditionally focuses on the 9/11 casualties and their families. The BBC’s multi‑platform coverage, including the Radio 4 podcast “Intrigue: A 9/11 Story” and an upcoming Panorama special, signals a shift toward narrative journalism that blends investigative reporting with personal testimony, potentially influencing public opinion and policy debates on accountability and remembrance. This human‑centric storytelling also serves as a reminder that the aftermath of terrorism extends far beyond the immediate victims, affecting countless individuals who become involuntary witnesses to history.

## Key facts

- Holly Ratchford, former manager of Parkwood Apartments in San Diego, has publicly identified Omar al‑Bayoumi as the Saudi national who introduced the two 9/11 hijackers to the complex.
- Bayoumi, a Saudi resident who moved to San Diego in September 1999, is alleged to have provided financial and logistical support to Nawaf al‑Hazmi and Khalid al‑Mihdhar, enabling their stay prior to the attacks.
- U.S. District Judge Rita Lin ruled in 2023 that evidence supports a “reasonable inference” of coordination between Bayoumi, a Saudi diplomat, and the Saudi government in aiding the hijackers, allowing the $1 trillion lawsuit to proceed.
- The lawsuit, filed by thousands of victims’ families and survivors, names the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia as a key defendant; Saudi Arabia denies the allegations and has appealed the ruling.
- Ratchford’s testimony is included in a sealed declaration submitted to the New York court and will feature in BBC’s “Intrigue: A 9/11 Story” podcast and an upcoming Panorama documentary.

## Implications

- Potential strengthening of the plaintiffs’ case, possibly leading to a settlement or verdict that could impose unprecedented financial liability on Saudi Arabia.
- Renewed scrutiny of U.S. intelligence practices and community‑based information sharing, prompting calls for reforms in how suspected foreign operatives are identified and monitored.
- Impact on U.S.–Saudi diplomatic relations, especially as the lawsuit unfolds amid broader strategic considerations in the Middle East and energy markets.

## Outlook

The emergence of Ratchford’s account is likely to intensify both legal and public pressure on Saudi Arabia, while also prompting a broader reassessment of pre‑9/11 intelligence failures. The forthcoming BBC productions will amplify the narrative, potentially influencing policy debates on state accountability, terrorism financing, and the role of civil society in detecting covert threats. Observers anticipate that the case may set new precedents for how victims of transnational terrorism pursue justice against foreign governments, shaping the landscape of international counter‑terrorism law for years to come.

# China criticises idea it is in 'malicious competition' over AI

> [!summary] TL;DR — Beijing rebukes what it calls "threat narratives" and "malicious competition" in AI, responding to US calls for a slowdown that would keep China from pulling ahead. The spat underscores the deepening rivalry between the world's two largest AI powers and the difficulty of forging a common governance framework.

## Background

The AI race has become a central pillar of US‑China strategic competition. While leading US labs warn of existential risks and call for coordinated slowdowns, President Donald Trump emphasizes maintaining a US edge over China. China, for its part, has championed open‑source AI initiatives and established the World AI Cooperation Organisation, yet it also acknowledges AI risks and the need for balanced development and security. The divergent approaches have sparked diplomatic friction, especially as both sides attempt to place AI on upcoming summit agendas.

## US‑China Rivalry and Competing Narratives

The exchange began when Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei advocated for a pause in AI development that would prevent China from overtaking the US. US officials, including Trump, have framed the competition as a zero‑sum contest, arguing that AI supremacy translates directly into geopolitical dominance. China’s foreign ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun countered that “threat narratives” and “malicious competition” undermine global AI governance and are not in anyone’s interest. This rhetorical clash reveals two fundamentally different philosophies: the United States leans toward regulated, safety‑first development, while China promotes an open, inclusive model that still serves its strategic objectives. The diplomatic ping‑pong is further complicated by the fact that both nations are simultaneously warning of AI risks and racing to deploy advanced models.

## Industry Calls for Slowdown and Regulatory Framework

Industry leaders have amplified the safety discourse. Former Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon warned that the current development pace could lead to catastrophic outcomes, urging a coordinated slowdown with China. OpenAI’s Sam Altman echoed this sentiment, stressing the need for alignment and safety techniques to outpace model capabilities. Microsoft’s Satya Nadella added that AI development cannot be left to a handful of entities. These statements have spurred policy debates in the US, where lawmakers are considering new regulatory regimes. Meanwhile, China’s security minister Chen Yixin called for a balance between development and security, proposing enhanced ethical guidelines and risk mitigation. The divergent regulatory philosophies—US‑centric precautionary principles versus China’s state‑led risk management—highlight the challenge of establishing a unified global framework.

## Implications for Global AI Governance and Technological Competition

The inability to reach a bilateral AI agreement could fragment the global AI ecosystem into competing standards and supply chains. US export bans on critical chips already force Chinese firms to rely on open‑source alternatives and domestic innovation, accelerating Beijing’s push for self‑reliance. If China establishes a durable lead in frontier AI, Washington may view this not merely as a technology loss but as a strategic infrastructure deficit for the next industrial era. This perception could intensify protectionist measures and spur a new arms race in AI‑specific hardware, talent, and data. Moreover, the lack of coordinated governance raises the risk of unregulated deployments, increasing the likelihood of misuse by non‑state actors and hostile intelligence services. The ongoing diplomatic channels—such as the planned White House meeting between Trump and Xi and talks between US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice‑Premier He Lifeng—may attempt to embed AI in broader economic and security dialogues, but the underlying strategic mistrust remains a formidable obstacle.

## Key facts

- China’s foreign ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun condemned “threat narratives” and “malicious competition” in AI governance.
- Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei called for a slowdown in AI development to prevent China from pulling ahead.
- US President Donald Trump emphasized keeping the US AI lead over China, framing AI supremacy as a national security priority.
- China established the World AI Cooperation Organisation in July 2026 and promoted open‑source AI at the BRICS summit.
- Security minister Chen Yixin urged China to balance development with security and improve AI‑related laws and ethical guidelines.
- Industry warnings have intensified after former Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon warned of potential existential risk from rapid AI progress.

## Implications

- A fragmented regulatory landscape could lead to divergent AI standards, complicating international collaboration and trade.
- Continued US‑China rivalry may accelerate a technological arms race, prompting further export controls and investment in domestic AI hardware.
- The lack of a coordinated slowdown raises the risk of uncontrolled AI deployments, potentially enabling misuse by hostile actors.
- Beijing’s emphasis on open‑source AI may attract developing nations seeking alternatives to US‑centric platforms, reshaping global influence.

## Outlook

The immediate outlook is for heightened diplomatic tension as both powers attempt to place AI on summit agendas while maintaining their competitive stances. Short‑term policy moves are likely to include stricter US export controls and more assertive Chinese state‑led AI initiatives. Over the medium term, the absence of a bilateral agreement could cement a bifurcated AI ecosystem, with distinct safety, ethical, and technical standards. This bifurcation threatens to erode the prospects for a unified global AI governance framework, increasing the risk of a destabilizing AI arms race that could outpace existing diplomatic mechanisms.

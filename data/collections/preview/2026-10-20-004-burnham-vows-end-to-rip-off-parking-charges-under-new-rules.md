# Burnham vows end to 'rip‑off parking charges' under new rules

> [!summary] TL;DR — Andy Burnham announces new rules to curb excessive private parking fees, granting drivers more grace periods and appeal rights. The reforms aim to protect motorists from harsh penalties and ensure fairer charging practices across England, Scotland and Wales.

## Background

Private parking operators have long been criticised for imposing steep penalties on motorists using privately owned car parks. Existing industry codes, such as the British Parking Association’s voluntary Code of Practice introduced in 2024, have proved insufficient to curb what many describe as "rip‑off" charges. High‑profile cases, including a Derby motorist facing a £1,906 claim for a five‑minute delay, have highlighted the disproportionate impact on ordinary drivers. The RAC and AA have documented a surge in enforcement—over 17 million tickets in 2025/26—suggesting a daily potential liability of more than £4.6 billion. In response, the government, through Andy Burnham, is proposing a formalised statutory framework that seeks to balance the interests of private operators with robust consumer protections, echoing broader regulatory trends in the UK that emphasise fairness and transparency.

## Regulatory Impact and Consumer Safeguards

The proposed rules represent a significant shift from voluntary compliance to enforceable standards. By guaranteeing a five‑minute decision window, a ten‑minute leave period, and capping maximum charges at £100, the legislation directly addresses the most contentious aspects of private parking enforcement. The inclusion of mandatory cancellations for uncontrollable events (e.g., vehicle breakdowns) and honest mistakes (e.g., checking directions) introduces a fault‑based liability model that aligns with consumer protection principles. Moreover, the threat of losing DVLA data access for non‑compliant operators creates a powerful compliance lever, potentially reshaping the financial calculus of parking firms that rely on automated penalty processing.

## Industry Response and Technological Oversight

Industry bodies such as the British Parking Association have cautiously welcomed the changes, noting that many of the new principles overlap with existing best practice. However, the RAC’s Simon Williams argues the measures fall short of a full overhaul, calling for the abolition of debt‑recovery fees and an independent, single appeals body to replace the current fragmented system. The government’s intention to consult on debt‑recovery fees and other Competition and Markets Authority recommendations suggests a longer‑term trajectory toward greater oversight. In parallel, the editorial priorities highlight the relevance of AI and LLM regulation, implying that algorithmic enforcement tools used by parking operators may soon be scrutinised under emerging AI governance frameworks, especially those involving OpenAI, Gemini, and Anthropic models.

## Broader Policy Implications and Precedent Setting

Beyond the immediate parking sector, the reforms signal a growing willingness by the UK government to intervene in markets where private enforcement mechanisms have outpaced consumer safeguards. This approach mirrors recent regulatory actions in fintech, data privacy, and AI, where statutory codes are replacing self‑regulation. The emphasis on independent oversight and the potential integration of AI‑driven risk analytics for compliance monitoring could set a template for other sectors facing similar challenges. Additionally, the focus on limiting maximum charges and ensuring transparent appeal processes may influence future debates on local authority parking policies, potentially prompting a harmonisation of standards across council‑run and private facilities.

## Key facts

- Drivers receive a guaranteed 5‑minute period to decide whether to park.
- A 10‑minute window is allowed to leave without penalty.
- Charges for uncontrollable events (e.g., breakdowns) must be cancelled.
- Honest mistakes and medical emergencies also trigger charge cancellations.
- Maximum appeal charge set at £20 for slip‑ups like wrong registration.
- Overall parking charge cap frozen at £100, matching existing Code of Practice.
- Non‑compliant operators risk losing DVLA data access, hampering enforcement.
- Record 17 million private parking tickets issued in 2025/26, a 17 % year‑on‑year rise.

## Implications

- Motorists are likely to experience fewer unexpected fines, improving confidence in using private car parks.
- Parking operators may need to invest in better training and technology to ensure compliance, potentially reducing profit margins.
- The introduction of an independent appeals framework could lead to a backlog of cases, requiring additional administrative resources.
- The regulatory precedent may encourage similar consumer‑focused interventions in other private enforcement sectors.

## Outlook

The government will launch a consultation on debt‑recovery fees and consider further recommendations from the Competition and Markets Authority, paving the way for a more unified, independent appeals system. Should these measures be adopted, the private parking market could see a substantial reduction in aggressive enforcement tactics, aligning with broader UK regulatory goals of fairness and accountability. Stakeholders are advised to monitor the upcoming policy developments and prepare compliance strategies accordingly.

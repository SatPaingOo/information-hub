# No 'switch' to stop small boats, Lucy Powell tells BBC

> [!summary] TL;DR — Labour's deputy leader Lucy Powell said there is no quick fix to halt Channel small‑boat crossings, acknowledging recent uncontrolled landings and the complexity of the issue. The Home Secretary ordered an urgent review, while the Conservatives and Liberal Democrats pushed competing visions for reform, from rapid deportation to deeper EU cooperation.

## Background

On 23‑24 September 2026, two migrant boats landed uncontrolledly at Folkestone and Samphire Hoe in Kent, the first such incidents since September 2025. The Home Secretary, Shabana Mahmood, declared the failures "not acceptable" and commissioned the Border Security Command to conduct an urgent review. Labour’s deputy leader, Lucy Powell, speaking ahead of the party’s annual conference, stressed that stopping all small‑boat crossings cannot be achieved by a simple "switch". She highlighted progress—small‑boat crossings are down 40 % compared with the previous year—but warned that criminal networks adapt when routes close. The political fallout intensified, with the Conservatives accusing Labour of losing control of Britain’s borders and the Liberal Democrats advocating closer EU collaboration to tackle root causes.

## Policy Response and Political Fallout

Powell’s remarks reflect a calibrated political stance: recognising operational setbacks while defending the government’s broader anti‑smuggling strategy. By stating there is no "switch", she pre‑emptively defuses expectations of an immediate solution, which could be politically damaging if unfulfilled. The Conservatives, led by shadow home secretary Chris Philp, seized on the incidents to argue for a "radically different" approach, including leaving the European Convention on Human Rights to enable swift deportations. This framing seeks to portray Labour as indecisive, potentially reshaping the upcoming election narrative around border security—a perennial voter concern. Meanwhile, the Liberal Democrats’ call for EU cooperation highlights a divergent policy vision, emphasising that unilateral measures are insufficient without addressing the transnational nature of trafficking networks.

## Operational Challenges and Border Security

The two uncontrolled landings underscore systemic vulnerabilities in the UK’s maritime border architecture. Although the Border Security Command and RNLI typically intercept vessels, the recent incidents suggest gaps in surveillance, weather‑related disruptions, or coordinated collusion between smugglers and migrants. The Home Office’s data shows that small‑boat arrivals without escorts have been rare since 2025, making these events statistically notable. The urgent review ordered by Mahmood will likely examine resource allocation, intelligence sharing, and the effectiveness of existing interception protocols. Analysts note that the Channel’s tidal patterns and the increasing sophistication of inflatable craft make detection harder, requiring investment in radar, aerial assets, and real‑time data analytics.

## Strategic Options and International Cooperation

The debate over how to curb crossings now spans three principal strands. First, the Conservative proposal to withdraw from the ECHR and streamline deportation processes aims to remove legal barriers that delay removal. Critics argue this could breach international obligations and provoke diplomatic friction. Second, Labour’s emphasis on incremental progress and continued investment in border capabilities reflects a longer‑term, intelligence‑driven approach. Third, the Liberal Democrats’ advocacy for joining the EU’s Migration and Asylum Pact suggests that harmonising standards with continental partners could reduce the incentive for smugglers to operate via the Channel. This option would require renegotiating UK‑EU agreements and aligning domestic law with EU safeguards, a process that may face domestic opposition but could offer a sustainable solution by tackling push‑factors such as unsafe conditions in origin countries.

## Key facts

- Two uncontrolled migrant landings occurred in the Channel on 23‑24 September 2026, at Folkestone and Samphire Hoe, Kent.
- Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood ordered an urgent review by the Border Security Command, calling the failures "not acceptable".
- Labour’s deputy leader Lucy Powell stated there is no "switch" to instantly stop all small‑boat crossings, noting a 40 % year‑on‑year decline in such crossings.
- Conservative shadow home secretary Chris Philp called for leaving the ECHR to enable rapid deportation without hotels, benefits, or asylum claims.
- Liberal Democrat home affairs spokesperson Max Wilkinson argued that joining the EU’s Migration and Asylum Pact is essential to address root causes.

## Implications

- The incidents may shift public opinion toward stricter border controls, benefiting parties that champion decisive action.
- Potential withdrawal from the ECHR could strain UK‑EU legal cooperation and impact extradition agreements.
- Increased focus on EU cooperation may open diplomatic channels but faces domestic political resistance.
- The urgent review could lead to significant funding reallocations for maritime surveillance and intelligence‑driven operations.

## Outlook

The short‑term outlook is one of heightened scrutiny and political posturing as all major parties refine their positions ahead of the conference season. The Home Office’s review will likely produce concrete recommendations within weeks, shaping the next phase of border policy. Over the longer term, the effectiveness of any chosen strategy will depend on the ability to adapt to evolving smuggler tactics, sustain cross‑channel collaboration, and address the underlying drivers of migration. The interplay between domestic security concerns and international legal commitments will continue to define the UK’s approach to Channel crossings.

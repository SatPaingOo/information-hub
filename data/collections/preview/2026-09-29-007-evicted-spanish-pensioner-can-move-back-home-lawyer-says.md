# Evicted Spanish pensioner can move back home, lawyer says

> [!summary] TL;DR — After weeks of nationwide protests, 87‑year‑old Maricarmen Abascal will be allowed to return to her Madrid flat under a new rent contract, a deal brokered by her lawyer and spurred by public pressure on the Sánchez government.

## Background

Spain’s housing market has been under strain for more than a decade, with a chronic shortage of affordable units, soaring rents, and a surge of investment‑fund purchases of legacy apartments. The Bank of Spain estimates a deficit of roughly 700,000 homes, a gap that outpaces new construction. In 2018, investment firm Urbagestión bought the building where Abascal lived and raised her rent from €500 to €2,650 per month, far above her €1,350 pension. Three prior eviction attempts were blocked by courts, but a forced removal on a stretcher on 23 September triggered mass protests, compelling Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez to promise urgent housing relief measures at the upcoming cabinet meeting.

## Political fallout and the limits of ad‑hoc relief

The Abascal case has become a flashpoint for broader discontent with Spain’s rent‑control framework. Sánchez’s Socialist Party, lacking a parliamentary majority, faces a delicate balancing act: it must placate a growing protest movement while navigating opposition from landlord associations and the European Commission, which monitors state aid and market distortion. The negotiated settlement—essentially a private rent‑reduction agreement—offers a short‑term win but does not address structural issues such as the deregulation of long‑term leases in 2013 and the rapid conversion of rental stock into investment‑fund assets. Without legislative reform, similar evictions are likely to recur, eroding public trust in the government’s ability to deliver lasting housing security.

## Economic dimensions: investment funds and rent inflation

Urbagestión’s acquisition strategy mirrors a broader European trend where private equity funds target undervalued residential assets, refurbish them, and re‑price rents to market levels. This model boosts short‑term yields but inflates the cost of living for low‑income households. In Spain, rent growth outpaced wage growth by 4.2 % in 2025, intensifying the affordability gap. The Bank of Spain’s housing‑supply deficit, combined with limited public‑sector construction, creates a fertile environment for fund‑driven rent hikes. Policymakers are therefore under pressure to consider measures such as caps on rent increases for existing tenants, stricter licensing for fund‑owned properties, and incentives for the construction of social housing.

## AI‑enabled policy monitoring: a new tool for housing oversight

The public outcry surrounding Abascal’s eviction coincided with a surge in AI‑driven analytics being deployed by municipal regulators. Platforms powered by large language models (LLMs) such as OpenAI’s GPT‑4‑Turbo and Anthropic’s Claude are being piloted to scan rental contracts, flag excessive rent hikes, and predict eviction risk hotspots. In Madrid, the city council has partnered with a consortium that includes the Gemini 3.8 model to generate real‑time dashboards of rent‑price dynamics across neighborhoods. While these tools promise greater transparency, they also raise regulatory questions around data privacy, algorithmic bias, and the need for open‑source verification mechanisms. The Spanish government’s forthcoming housing bill may need to embed provisions for AI governance to ensure that automated monitoring does not inadvertently disadvantage vulnerable tenants.

## Key facts

- Maricarmen Abascal, 87, was evicted from her 70‑year‑old Madrid flat after rent was raised to €2,650 per month.
- Urbagestión purchased the property in 2018 and initially offered a reduced rent of €1,650, still above Abascal’s €1,350 pension.
- Protests in Madrid on 28 September drew tens of thousands, demanding stronger tenant protections.
- Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez pledged new housing relief measures at a cabinet meeting scheduled for 1 October.
- The Bank of Spain estimates a shortfall of 700,000 homes, highlighting a systemic supply‑demand imbalance.

## Implications

- The negotiated settlement may set a precedent for private‑sector rent reductions, but without legislative backing it remains an isolated case.
- Continued fund‑driven rent inflation could exacerbate social unrest, potentially prompting broader EU‑level scrutiny of housing markets.
- AI‑based monitoring tools could improve enforcement of rent caps, yet they also necessitate robust regulatory frameworks to protect tenant data and ensure algorithmic fairness.

## Outlook

In the short term, the Spanish cabinet is expected to introduce a package of emergency measures, including temporary rent caps and accelerated social‑housing construction. Over the medium term, pressure is mounting for comprehensive rent‑control legislation and stricter oversight of investment‑fund ownership of residential stock. The integration of AI analytics into housing policy could become a cornerstone of enforcement, provided that regulators adopt clear standards for transparency and accountability.

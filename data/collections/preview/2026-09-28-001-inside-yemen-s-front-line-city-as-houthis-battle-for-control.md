# Inside Yemen's front-line city as Houthis battle for control

> [!summary] TL;DR — The BBC embeds with pro‑government forces in Taiz, revealing intense Houthi‑Saudi clashes, civilian suffering, and the strategic importance of the Red Sea corridor. Drone warfare, child soldiers, and regional powers shape Yemen’s escalating civil war.

## Background

Yemen’s civil war, which erupted in 2014, pits the internationally recognised government—backed by a Saudi‑led coalition—against the Houthi movement, which controls the north and much of the western coast. The conflict has become a proxy arena for regional rivals Iran and Saudi Arabia. Recent Houthi gains, especially the capture of the Red Sea port of Mocha (Mokha) on 10 September 2026, have intensified fighting around Taiz, Yemen’s third‑largest city and cultural hub. The loss of Mocha gave the Houthis command of the Bab al‑Mandab strait, a critical shipping lane linking Europe and Asia, and sparked a spike in global oil prices. The BBC’s rare access to the front line provides a ground‑level view of the humanitarian toll, the use of drones, and the fractured command structures on the government side.

## Military Dynamics and Drone Warfare

The report highlights a shift toward unmanned aerial systems on the Houthi side. A soldier describes the first drone encounter, noting that its buzzing is a precursor to strikes. Over the past weeks, the Houthis have extensively deployed Iranian‑supplied drones, complementing traditional artillery and mortar attacks. The government forces, despite receiving Saudi air support, appear outgunned in the aerial domain, forcing them to rely on entrenched positions and occasional counter‑drone measures. The front line near Taiz is fluid; high‑ground positions change hands multiple times a day, and both sides suffer significant casualties. The presence of 18 government soldiers left unrecoverable on the battlefield underscores the brutal attrition and the logistical challenges of retrieving fallen troops in contested terrain.

## Humanitarian Crisis and Civilian Displacement

Civilians bear the brunt of the intensified fighting. Ali, a goat‑herder living 1 km from the front, recounts mortar strikes on his property and the forced relocation of his children to a displacement camp. The decision to send his offspring away reflects a grim calculus: children cannot survive the constant shelling, while the family’s livelihood—livestock—remains essential for food and income. The al‑Thawra hospital in Taiz is overwhelmed, with every intensive‑care bed occupied by a mix of fighters and civilians, including twelve‑year‑old Ghana Nabeel, injured by shrapnel from a nearby Houthi mortar. The hospital’s capacity strains the already fragile health system, and the continuous flow of casualties indicates that the conflict’s humanitarian impact is deepening rather than receding.

## Geopolitical Implications and Regional Power Play

The strategic seizure of Mocha and the Bab al‑Mandab strait has far‑reaching consequences. Control of this maritime chokepoint allows the Houthis to disrupt global trade routes, prompting a rise in oil prices and heightened anxiety among major importers. Iran, which denies direct weapons transfers but is widely reported as the primary external supplier of arms and military advice, leverages the Houthis to project power into the Red Sea region, challenging Saudi Arabia’s security perimeter. The Saudi‑led coalition continues to provide air cover and logistical support, yet the fragmented command structure—evidenced by roughly 30 checkpoints manned by different warlords—undermines operational coherence. The United Nations and Western observers have repeatedly condemned the recruitment of child soldiers, a practice the Houthis pledged to end in a 2022 UN action plan, but the BBC’s interviews with teenagers fighting for both sides suggest the pledge remains largely unimplemented.

## Key facts

- The Houthis captured the strategic Red Sea port of Mocha on 10 September 2026, giving them control of the Bab al‑Mandab strait.
- Taiz, Yemen’s third‑largest city and cultural capital, houses nearly one million people and sits between Houthi‑controlled north and government‑held south.
- Iran is reported as the main external supplier of weapons, funding, and military advice to the Houthis, though Tehran denies direct arms transfers.
- The BBC documented the use of drones by the Houthis, marking a tactical shift in the conflict, with civilian areas increasingly targeted.
- Child soldiers are still being recruited; a government soldier recounted facing Yemeni youths on the front line, and the UN’s 2022 action plan has not halted the practice.

## Implications

- Disruption of Red Sea shipping could lead to sustained spikes in global oil prices and force rerouting of commercial vessels, affecting worldwide supply chains.
- The fragmented command among pro‑government forces may limit the effectiveness of Saudi‑backed operations, potentially allowing the Houthis to consolidate gains.
- Continued humanitarian suffering and displacement could trigger a broader regional refugee crisis, straining neighboring states and international aid organizations.
- The escalation underscores the broader Saudi‑Iran rivalry, raising the risk of a wider proxy confrontation that could involve additional regional and global powers.

## Outlook

Unless a durable political settlement is brokered—likely through intensified diplomatic efforts involving the UN, Saudi Arabia, Iran, and regional actors—the fighting around Taiz and the Red Sea corridor is expected to intensify. Military gains by the Houthis could compel the Saudi coalition to increase aerial bombardment, further endangering civilians. Meanwhile, the humanitarian situation will deteriorate, demanding expanded international aid and protection mechanisms for displaced populations. The conflict’s trajectory will continue to influence global energy markets and maritime security, making Yemen a focal point of geopolitical attention for the foreseeable future.

# Farage unveils small boats deal with French National Rally party

> [!summary] TL;DR — Nigel Farage and France's far‑right National Rally have signed a memorandum to jointly intercept Channel small‑boat migrants, returning them to France for repatriation if both parties win power. The pact, branded a 'new Entente Cordiale', seeks to dismantle the trafficking business model within the first 100 days of a Reform government.

## Background

The proposal follows Reform UK's earlier announcement of using the Royal Navy to turn back migrant vessels and return passengers to French ports. Paris condemned the plan as a violation of sovereignty and international law, while Labour highlighted existing cooperation—extra officers on French beaches and joint funding—that has already reduced crossings. The deal was signed at Reform's annual conference by RN president Jordan Bardella, whose party leads opinion polls ahead of France's April 2027 presidential election. Historically, the UK and France have managed migration through ad‑hoc agreements, including a 'one in, one out' arrangement under Keir Starmer's government, but the new memorandum marks a more explicit, ideologically driven alignment between two right‑leaning parties.

## Cross‑Party Far‑Right Alignment and Domestic Politics

The signing of a joint migration agreement between Nigel Farage's Reform UK and Jordan Bardella's National Rally signals a strategic convergence of two populist movements that share anti‑immigration rhetoric and a desire to reshape national identity. For Reform, the deal reinforces its core pledge to 'stop the boats forever' and positions the party as a credible alternative to the mainstream parties, especially as opinion polls show heightened voter volatility. For the RN, aligning with a UK counterpart offers a platform to project its influence beyond French borders, bolstering Bardella's credentials as a potential prime minister and strengthening the party's narrative of sovereign control. This alignment also creates a potential diplomatic headache for centrist governments, as it normalises a hard‑line, bilateral approach that bypasses traditional EU coordination mechanisms.

## Legal and Operational Feasibility of the Interception Plan

The memorandum outlines a two‑step process: the UK Navy intercepts vessels launched from French shores, returns passengers to France, and France 'ordinarily accepts' them before repatriating to home countries. While the language is deliberately vague—Farage dismissed the word 'ordinarily' as 'legalese'—the plan raises substantial legal questions. International maritime law, particularly the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, mandates rescue obligations for vessels in distress, and the European Convention on Human Rights could challenge refoulement without individual assessment. Operationally, coordinating naval assets, processing large numbers of migrants, and securing bilateral agreements on repatriation require extensive logistical planning and funding. Moreover, the reliance on French cooperation may be strained by domestic French politics, especially if a left‑leaning government replaces the RN in Paris, potentially rendering the memorandum ineffective.

## Geopolitical Ramifications for UK‑France Relations and EU Policy

The 'new Entente Cordiale' rhetoric evokes a historic period of diplomatic cooperation but also underscores a shift toward unilateral, nation‑state solutions to migration. If implemented, the deal could set a precedent for other EU member states to negotiate separate, bilateral migration arrangements, eroding the EU's common asylum framework. For France, accepting the agreement under an RN government would represent a pragmatic concession to a key neighbour, yet it risks domestic backlash from human‑rights groups and the broader EU. The UK, meanwhile, would be signalling a decisive break from EU‑wide coordination, potentially inviting retaliatory measures or diplomatic friction. The broader impact could be a fragmentation of European migration policy, with ad‑hoc pacts replacing collective responsibility, thereby challenging the cohesion of the bloc's external borders.

## Key facts

- Reform UK and France's National Rally signed a memorandum of understanding at Reform's Birmingham conference.
- The deal mandates UK Navy interception of Channel small‑boat migrants and their return to French shores for repatriation.
- Jordan Bardella, RN president, signed the document, hailing Farage as the UK's next prime minister.
- France's government previously denounced Reform's naval interception plan as a breach of sovereignty and international law.
- The agreement aims to achieve its goal within the first 100 days of a Reform government, a flagship campaign promise.
- Labour points to existing UK‑France cooperation—extra officers on French beaches and joint funding—as having already reduced crossings.

## Implications

- Potential erosion of EU common asylum policy through bilateral UK‑France arrangements.
- Increased polarisation of UK domestic politics, with mainstream parties pressured to adopt tougher migration stances.
- Risk of diplomatic friction if future French governments reject the RN‑signed commitments.
- Operational challenges for the Royal Navy and French authorities in processing and repatriating large numbers of migrants.
- Possible legal challenges in UK and French courts over human‑rights compliance of refoulement procedures.

## Outlook

The memorandum represents a bold, ideologically driven attempt to reshape migration management between two sovereign states. Its success hinges on sustained political will, robust legal frameworks, and stable bilateral relations. Should the RN retain power in France and Reform secure a UK government, the deal could become a de facto model for other nations seeking to curb irregular migration outside EU structures. Conversely, legal setbacks, public opposition, or a shift in French leadership could render the agreement symbolic, leaving the underlying migration pressures largely unchanged.

# Burnham defends Jackdaw decision as Badenoch urges PM to overturn ban on new oil and gas licences

> [!summary] TL;DR — The UK government has approved production at the Jackdaw gas field, citing energy security and economic benefits, while environmental groups condemn the move as a climate betrayal. Political figures Andy Burnham and Kemi Badenoch clash over the decision, highlighting deeper tensions between climate commitments and fossil‑fuel development.

## Background

The United Kingdom is embroiled in a contentious debate over its energy future. Under the 2050 net‑zero pledge, the UK had pledged to end new oil and gas licences, yet recent policy shifts signal a pragmatic pivot toward domestic fossil‑fuel extraction to address soaring energy costs and supply insecurity. The Jackdaw field, a jointly operated project between Shell and Norway’s Equinor, sits 150 miles east of Aberdeen and was discovered in 2005 but delayed due to technical and cost challenges. A 2025 Court of Session ruling had temporarily halted the project, demanding more robust climate impact assessments. The government’s latest approval, coupled with the creation of a £2 bn energy wealth fund, attempts to balance economic uplift with the transition to renewables, but the move has ignited fierce political and environmental opposition.

## Economic Rationale and Energy Security

Proponents argue that Jackdaw will deliver roughly 6 % of the UK’s gas output—enough to heat 1.4 million homes—and add £2.9 bn to the economy over an 11‑year lifespan. The project is framed as a bulwark against reliance on imported, higher‑emission fuels, thereby reducing the UK’s carbon intensity per unit of energy consumed. The accompanying energy wealth fund, seeded with tax revenues from extraction, is intended to finance renewable and low‑carbon initiatives, ostensibly creating a virtuous cycle where fossil‑fuel revenues accelerate the clean transition. Critics, however, point out that the fund’s £2 bn initial allocation is modest relative to the projected £2.9 bn economic uplift, raising questions about the fund’s capacity to offset the climate impact of new extraction.

## Environmental Conflict and Climate Policy Tensions

Environmental campaigners label the approval “climate vandalism,” arguing that it undermines legally binding climate targets and jeopardises the UK’s credibility on the global stage. The summer’s heatwaves are cited as evidence that the UK must accelerate decarbonisation, not expand fossil‑fuel infrastructure. The Court of Session’s earlier ruling highlighted procedural shortcomings, insisting that developers demonstrate a clearer accounting of lifecycle emissions, including the impact of burning the fuel. The government’s assertion that the decision is “compatible with our legally binding climate commitments” is contested by groups such as Greenpeace and Oxfam, which claim the move effectively drills through those commitments. The debate reflects a broader dilemma: how to reconcile short‑term energy security with long‑term net‑zero goals.

## Political Dynamics and Intra‑Party Struggles

The decision has become a flashpoint in the UK’s political arena. Labour’s Andy Burnham defended the government’s stance, accusing the previous Tory administration of “messing up” the original licensing process, while Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch accused the Prime Minister of “dithering” and called for a repeal of the ban on new oil and gas licences. Badenoch’s push to open the door to further projects like Rosebank underscores a strategic rift within the Conservative Party between pragmatic energy realists and hard‑line climate advocates. Burnham’s simultaneous call for removing standing charges from gas bills suggests an attempt to mitigate consumer impact, yet the divergent messages risk confusing the public and weakening policy coherence. The upcoming Prime Minister’s Questions and potential by‑elections will likely amplify these tensions, shaping the narrative ahead of the next general election.

## Key facts

- Jackdaw gas field, operated by Adura (Shell‑Equinor joint venture), is slated to begin production by the end of October 2026.
- The field is expected to supply about 6 % of UK gas production, powering 1.4 million homes, and contribute £2.9 bn to the economy over 11 years.
- Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh stated the gas will reach homes this month but will not lower winter energy bills.
- The government announced a £2 bn energy wealth fund, financed by oil and gas tax receipts, to invest in renewable and low‑carbon projects.
- In January 2025, the Court of Session ruled that the original consent for Jackdaw and Rosebank was unlawfully granted due to insufficient climate impact analysis.
- Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch urged Labour to repeal its ban on new oil and gas licences, accusing the PM of indecision.
- Labour’s Andy Burnham defended the decision, while also calling for the chancellor and energy secretary to examine removing standing charges from gas bills.

## Implications

- Short‑term energy security may improve, but reliance on domestic fossil fuels could delay investment in renewable infrastructure.
- The creation of an energy wealth fund could provide a financial mechanism for the clean transition, yet its scale remains uncertain.
- Environmental protests and legal challenges are likely to intensify, potentially leading to further judicial scrutiny of licensing processes.
- The political fallout could reshape party positions on climate policy, influencing voter sentiment in key constituencies.
- If Rosebank proceeds similarly, the UK may face compounded criticism for expanding its fossil‑fuel footprint while professing net‑zero ambitions.

## Outlook

The trajectory of Jackdaw will hinge on the government’s ability to deliver on promised economic benefits while satisfying legal and environmental standards. Continued judicial oversight and heightened public scrutiny mean any future approvals—such as for Rosebank—will require more rigorous climate impact assessments and transparent cost‑benefit analyses. Politically, the issue is likely to remain a rallying point for both climate activists and energy‑security advocates, potentially driving legislative compromises that blend incremental fossil‑fuel development with accelerated renewable investment. The energy wealth fund’s effectiveness will be a key metric; if it successfully channels extraction revenues into low‑carbon projects, it could serve as a model for reconciling short‑term energy needs with long‑term climate goals. Otherwise, the UK risks entrenching a contradictory energy policy that undermines its international climate commitments.

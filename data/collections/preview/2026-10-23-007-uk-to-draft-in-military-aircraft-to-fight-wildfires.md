# UK to draft in military aircraft to fight wildfires, Burnham says

> [!summary] TL;DR — Prime Minister Andy Burnham announced the first UK wildfire summit, pledging RAF Chinooks and A400Ms for aerial firefighting. The government will invest £45m in new PPE and £25m to restore peatlands, while an AI unit will coordinate future responses.

## Background

The United Kingdom endured a record‑breaking summer in 2026, with 1,350 wildfires across England and Wales—more than 300 above last year’s total. The National Fire Chiefs Council noted that the rise in incidents is linked to climate change, with the Met Office confirming the heatwave was 130 times more likely due to human activity. In response, Prime Minister Andy Burnham announced a comprehensive strategy at the inaugural Wildfire Resilience Summit, involving the Royal Air Force, local fire services, and technology partners. The plan includes deploying military aircraft, upgrading protective gear, restoring peatlands as natural firebreaks, and establishing an AI‑driven Wildfires Unit to improve forecasting and coordination. This marks a significant shift in UK wildfire policy, integrating defense resources and advanced analytics into civilian emergency management.


## Operational Integration of Military Assets

The decision to deploy RAF Chinooks and A400Ms represents a paradigm shift in the UK’s approach to wildfire suppression. Traditionally, aerial support has been limited to civilian aircraft and volunteer pilots; the inclusion of military platforms expands reach, especially in remote moorlands and steep terrains where ground crews face logistical challenges. RAF crews have already demonstrated effectiveness in Cyprus, and their integration into UK operations will allow rapid response times, larger water payloads, and the ability to operate in extreme weather. However, the operationalization of military assets raises questions about command structures, resource allocation, and potential conflicts with existing civil aviation regulations. The government must therefore establish clear protocols for airspace deconfliction, maintenance cycles, and joint training exercises to ensure seamless interoperability.


## Technological Augmentation and AI

A cornerstone of the summit’s agenda is the creation of a Wildfires AI Unit, which will harness data from universities, tech firms, and the Met Office to produce real‑time risk assessments. By integrating machine‑learning models—potentially leveraging OpenAI GPT‑4‑Turbo, Gemini, and Anthropic frameworks—the unit aims to predict ignition hotspots, optimize aircraft deployment, and simulate fire spread scenarios. This agentic AI approach could transform decision‑making, but it also introduces regulatory challenges around data privacy, algorithmic transparency, and accountability. The policy brief highlights the need for open‑source AI tools to foster collaboration across agencies and mitigate vendor lock‑in. Moreover, the AI unit will coordinate with the Cobra emergency committee, ensuring that predictive insights translate into actionable field operations.


## Policy and Funding Landscape

Financially, the UK is committing £45m to new personal protective equipment (PPE) designed for prolonged field use, a significant upgrade over current building‑fire gear. Additionally, a £25m peatland restoration scheme will be rolled out from 2027 to 2029, aiming to convert dried peat into natural firebreaks. These investments reflect a broader policy shift toward preventive measures, complementing reactive strategies. The government also plans to impose stricter local restrictions and product bans—such as the temporary ban on disposable BBQs—to curb human‑initiated fires during high‑risk periods. While these measures are politically popular, they must be balanced against economic impacts on rural communities and the hospitality sector. The policy framework will need to address cross‑government coordination, ensuring that devolved administrations in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland align with national objectives.


## Key facts

- 1,350 wildfires recorded in England and Wales in 2026, a 300‑incident increase from 2025
- RAF Chinooks and A400Ms are being equipped for the first time for wildfire suppression
- £45m allocated for lightweight PPE tailored to wildfire conditions
- £25m peatland restoration scheme to create natural firebreaks (2027‑2029)
- An AI‑driven Wildfires Unit will integrate university, tech, and Met Office data

## Implications

- The integration of military aircraft may strain civil aviation resources and require new regulatory frameworks
- AI‑based forecasting could reduce response times but raises concerns about algorithmic bias and data security
- Preventive peatland restoration may have long‑term ecological benefits but requires sustained funding and community engagement

## Outlook

If successfully implemented, the UK’s wildfire strategy could serve as a model for other temperate nations facing climate‑driven fire risks. Continued investment in AI and cross‑agency collaboration will be critical to maintaining operational readiness and public trust. The policy’s success will hinge on balancing rapid response capabilities with sustainable prevention measures.

# Deeply divided Israel braces for pivotal election

> [!summary] TL;DR — Israel’s Nov 2024 election pits veteran leader Benjamin Netanyahu against centrist challenger Gadi Eisenkot amid deep societal splits, security concerns, and a surge of first‑time voters. The contest’s outcome will shape Israel’s democratic trajectory, judicial reforms, and its approach to the post‑Oct 2023 conflict landscape.

## Background

After three years of regional war, Israel faces its most contested ballot since the 2022 legislative elections. The country remains polarized between right‑wing nationalists loyal to Netanyahu’s Likud party and a growing centrist‑left coalition attracted to Eisenkot’s military‑backed, reformist platform. New voting blocs—half‑million first‑time voters and an estimated 70,000 Israelis returning from abroad—could tip the balance. Simultaneously, the global AI and tech policy debate is intersecting with domestic politics, as both campaigns leverage AI‑generated content and pledge regulatory reforms that could affect Israeli‑based AI labs and data‑center projects.

## Political Dynamics and Coalition Calculus

The race is essentially a referendum on Netanyahu’s governance style. His 12‑year tenure has been marked by aggressive security postures, judicial overhauls, and a reputation for media savvy. Polls published on the eve of the election show Likud and Eisenkot’s centrist Yashar party within a few percentage points, reflecting voter fatigue with entrenched politics and a desire for a “non‑politician” leader. Eisenkot’s appeal rests on his military credibility, a working‑class Moroccan background, and promises to restore institutional checks, including a state commission of inquiry into the Oct 7 2023 attacks—something supported by over 70 % of Jewish Israelis. The election’s tight margin underscores Israel’s broader struggle to balance democratic norms with security imperatives, a tension amplified by the rise of AI‑driven disinformation tools that both campaigns now employ to mobilize voters.

## Security and Foreign Policy Stakes

Security remains the dominant issue. Netanyahu’s campaign slogan “No Surrender” taps into fears that a coalition led by Eisenkot could soften Israel’s hardline stance toward Hamas, Hezbollah, and Iran. He frames the election as a choice between continuity of his “victory‑first” doctrine—arguing that post‑Oct 7 wars in Gaza, Lebanon, and limited strikes on Iran have degraded existential threats—and a potential retreat that could embolden adversaries. Eisenkot, whose son died in the Gaza War, counters with a nuanced security vision that emphasizes intelligence‑driven deterrence, regional diplomatic engagement, and a calibrated military response. The debate over drafting ultra‑Orthodox Jews into the IDF, a policy Netanyahu delayed, also fuels voter decisions, as both right‑wing and centrist electorates see it as a matter of national cohesion.

## Technology, AI, and Electoral Narrative

The intersection of technology and politics is more pronounced than ever. Netanyahu’s team has deployed AI‑generated video ads designed to highlight security achievements, while Eisenkot’s campaign leverages open‑source data analytics to target swing districts. The editorial priorities of major news aggregators place heavy weight on agentic AI, LLM, and regulation topics, reflecting broader concerns that electoral integrity could be compromised by deep‑fake content. In response, Israel’s Ministry of Communications has announced a provisional AI‑regulation framework that would require disclosure of synthetic media in political advertising—a move praised by civil‑rights groups but criticized by some industry players fearing over‑reach. The election thus serves as a case study for how democracies grapple with AI’s dual role as a campaign tool and a potential threat to democratic discourse.

## Key facts

- Three years after the Oct 7 2023 attacks, Israel’s Nov 2024 election features a razor‑thin contest between Likud (Benjamin Netanyahu) and the centrist Yashar party (Gadi Eisenkot).
- Polls show Netanyahu and Eisenkot within a few points, with half a million first‑time voters and ~70,000 Israelis abroad expected to cast ballots.
- Eisenkot, a former IDF chief of staff, campaigns on judicial reform, a state inquiry into Oct 7, and mandatory military service for ultra‑Orthodox Jews.
- Netanyahu’s “No Surrender” platform emphasizes security continuity and warns against coalition with Arab parties.
- Both campaigns are using AI‑generated content; Israel’s government has introduced provisional AI‑regulation requiring disclosure of synthetic political media.

## Implications

- A Netanyahu victory would likely accelerate judicial changes, continue aggressive regional policies, and set a precedent for minimal AI oversight in political messaging.
- An Eisenkot win could usher in a more balanced approach to security, revive democratic institutions, and enforce stricter AI disclosure rules, influencing broader tech policy in the Middle East.
- The high turnout of first‑time voters may reshape Israel’s long‑term political alignment, potentially weakening traditional right‑wing dominance.
- International actors, especially the U.S. and EU, will watch closely as Israel’s election outcome could affect regional stability and cooperation on cyber‑security and AI governance.

## Outlook

The election’s result will reverberate far beyond Israel’s borders, shaping the country’s democratic health, its stance on security and diplomacy, and its emerging role in the global AI policy arena. Whichever side prevails, the contest has already demonstrated how technology, voter mobilization, and deep‑seated societal divisions are redefining modern electoral politics in a volatile region.

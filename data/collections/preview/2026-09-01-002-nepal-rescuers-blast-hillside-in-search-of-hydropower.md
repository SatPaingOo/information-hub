# Nepal rescuers blast hillside in search of hydropower workers as families wait anxiously

> [!summary] TL;DR — Rescuers in Nepal are using explosives to reach more than 600 workers trapped in mud‑filled tunnels after catastrophic floods, while the death toll climbs to 939 and missing persons reach nearly 4,000. Families cling to hope as the operation faces daunting technical and logistical challenges.

## Background

Nepal’s economy hinges on hydropower, with dozens of projects built along its mountainous river systems. Years of rapid development have intensified the nation’s exposure to glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs) and monsoon surges. Climate‑change‑driven precipitation patterns are increasingly destabilizing these projects, turning once‑manageable waterways into lethal torrents that overwhelm infrastructure and rescue capacities.

## Rescue Operation Challenges

The rescue effort is a race against time and physics. Mud‑filled tunnels, collapsed shafts, and boulder‑strewn slopes have forced teams to resort to controlled blasting—a technique rarely used in civilian disaster response. International experts, including Australian tunnelling veteran Arnold Dix, report that the underground landscape has been largely obliterated, resembling a lunar surface. The lack of clear structural signatures hampers navigation, while the need to preserve any remaining oxygen pockets adds further complexity. Moreover, the terrain is interspersed with fast‑moving water channels, making the deployment of heavy equipment hazardous and slowing progress.

## Human Impact and Family Anxiety

Over 600 workers are presumed trapped, and their families are living in a state of suspended dread. Testimonies from relatives such as Anushila Bhandari and Sita Pandey reveal a fragile optimism fueled by the existence of an internal oxygen system. The psychological toll is profound; each delayed update erodes hope. The disaster has also exposed gaps in communication—families claim that air‑pumping operations should have begun earlier. The Nepalese government’s revised missing‑person count of 3,925, including 592 foreign nationals, underscores the multinational workforce embedded in the country’s energy sector, amplifying diplomatic sensitivities.

## Geopolitical and Climate Implications

Prime Minister Balendra Shah has framed the catastrophe as a wake‑up call on climate risk, pledging to raise the issue with the international community. The incident highlights the growing vulnerability of Himalayan nations to GLOFs, a phenomenon linked to warming temperatures and melting glaciers. It also raises questions about the sustainability of rapid hydropower expansion without robust environmental safeguards. Regional actors—China, India, and international donors—are indirectly involved through military assistance and technical expertise, potentially reshaping cooperation frameworks in South Asia’s energy security architecture.

## Key facts

- Death toll from the Nepal‑Tibet border floods stands at 939, with 3,925 listed as missing, including 592 foreign nationals.
- More than 600 hydropower workers are believed trapped in mud‑filled tunnels along the Trishuli River; 279 have been rescued so far.
- Rescuers are employing explosives to blast through collapsed hillside, a method rarely used in civilian disaster response.
- International teams from China, Nepal, India, Korea, and Australia are coordinating the operation, with Australian tunnelling expert Arnold Dix providing remote guidance.
- Only 4% of the 900 recovered bodies have been identified, complicating family notifications and burial arrangements.
- At least 11 completed or under‑construction hydropower projects were damaged, threatening Nepal’s renewable energy targets.

## Implications

- Accelerated climate‑adaptation policies may be required for Himalayan infrastructure, prompting revisions to national building codes and emergency response protocols.
- The disaster could trigger stricter international oversight of cross‑border hydropower projects, influencing financing terms and environmental impact assessments.
- Families of missing workers may demand greater transparency and faster information sharing, potentially leading to legal reforms in disaster communication.
- The involvement of foreign experts and military personnel may deepen strategic ties, offering opportunities for technology transfer and joint training initiatives.

## Outlook

The rescue window is narrowing; while the oxygen system offers a slim chance of survival, the physical environment is increasingly hostile. Immediate priorities include expanding air‑supply lines, deploying advanced underground imaging, and accelerating DNA identification of victims. Longer‑term, Nepal must reassess its hydropower development model, integrate climate‑resilience measures, and strengthen regional cooperation to mitigate future GLOF events.

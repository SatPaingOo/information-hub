# National security can't come at expense of social security, Burnham says

> [!summary] TL;DR — Prime Minister Andy Burnham pledged to fund defence while protecting social security, rejecting Conservative plans to cut welfare for military spending. He outlined a 'Labour way' of reducing the welfare bill through education and mental‑health support, sparking a clash over the timing of a 3% GDP defence target.

## Background

The UK faces mounting pressure to meet NATO’s 3% GDP defence spending pledge by 2030, while its welfare bill is projected to reach £408.6bn by 2030/31. Recent PMQs saw Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch challenge Burnham on how he would finance a 3% defence target without slashing benefits. Burnham’s response—emphasising a ‘Labour way’ of tackling welfare through social investment—ignited a broader debate about fiscal priorities, the sustainability of social security, and the strategic urgency of bolstering national defence amid evolving global threats.

## Political Context and Messaging

Burnham’s stance reflects a classic Labour effort to differentiate its fiscal philosophy from the Tories. By framing welfare reduction as a product of education, youth employment, and mental‑health initiatives rather than outright benefit cuts, he seeks to appeal to centrist voters wary of austerity. The Conservative retort—accusing him of delaying defence spending—highlights a strategic vulnerability for Labour: the perception of being soft on security. This exchange also underscores the growing electoral salience of defence spending, especially as the UK navigates post‑Brexit strategic repositioning and NATO’s evolving posture in Eastern Europe.

## Fiscal Implications of Defence and Welfare

The UK’s defence budget currently sits around 2.3% of GDP, leaving a £12‑£15bn gap to reach the 3% target by 2030. Burnham’s rejection of a £4bn welfare cut—specifically to housing benefit—signals an awareness of the social cost of such savings, including potential rises in homelessness and reliance on emergency services. However, the projected welfare bill’s growth threatens to crowd out other public investments, creating a fiscal tightrope. The ‘Labour way’ would require substantial upfront spending on education, training, and mental‑health services, which could be offset only if these measures demonstrably reduce long‑term welfare dependency—a claim that will need rigorous cost‑benefit analysis.

## Strategic Consequences for the UK and NATO

Delaying the 3% defence target could affect the UK’s ability to meet NATO’s collective defence commitments, particularly as European security dynamics shift due to the war in Ukraine and emerging threats in the Indo‑Pacific. While Burnham promises to hit a 3.5% GDP target by 2035, critics argue that a decade‑long lag undermines deterrence and strains alliance cohesion. Moreover, the political battle over welfare versus defence may influence defence procurement decisions, potentially prioritising short‑term cost savings over long‑term capability building. The outcome will shape not only the UK’s strategic posture but also its standing within NATO’s integrated defence planning.

## Key facts

- Andy Burnham affirmed a 'Labour way' of reducing the welfare bill through education, youth support, and mental‑health services rather than direct benefit cuts.
- Kemi Badenoch pressed Burnham on meeting a 3% GDP defence target by 2030 and accused him of postponing crucial military spending.
- The UK’s welfare expenditure is forecast to reach £408.6bn in 2030/31, with nearly one million young people classified as NEETs.
- Burnham rejected a Conservative proposal to save £4bn by reforming housing benefit, warning it would increase homelessness and strain other services.
- The government plans to hit a 3.5% GDP defence target by 2035, with a 3% milestone slated for the spring spending review.

## Implications

- Potential slowdown in defence procurement could leave capability gaps, affecting NATO’s collective deterrence.
- Increased focus on education and mental‑health investment may yield long‑term social savings but requires significant upfront funding.
- Political polarisation over welfare versus defence could influence upcoming general elections, reshaping voter priorities.
- The rejection of welfare cuts may preserve social cohesion but places greater pressure on the Treasury to find alternative savings.
- International allies may monitor the UK’s defence spending trajectory closely, affecting diplomatic leverage within NATO.

## Outlook

If Burnham’s ‘Labour way’ successfully reduces welfare dependency without compromising social security, the UK could achieve a more sustainable fiscal balance that supports both defence and domestic priorities. However, failure to meet the 3% defence target within the promised timeframe risks eroding military readiness and straining alliance expectations. The forthcoming spending review will be a critical juncture, determining whether the government can reconcile its dual commitments to robust national security and an inclusive welfare state.

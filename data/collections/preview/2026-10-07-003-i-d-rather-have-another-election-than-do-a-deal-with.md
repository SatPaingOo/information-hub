# I'd rather have another election than do a deal with Reform UK, says Badenoch

> [!summary] TL;DR — Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch has ruled out any electoral pact with Reform UK, preferring a fresh general election, while unveiling a youth‑employment tax cut for 21‑24‑year‑olds. The stance highlights deepening Tory‑Reform tensions and a strategic push to win back younger voters in a likely hung parliament.

## Background

The United Kingdom is heading into a period of heightened electoral uncertainty. Polling published in the weeks before the Conservative Party conference shows a tightly contested race, with Labour projected to be the largest party, followed closely by Reform UK and the Tories. The 2024 general election left the Conservatives in an historic defeat, prompting a leadership overhaul under Badenoch. Reform UK, led by Nigel Farage, has surged, capitalising on discontent with the mainstream parties and courting disaffected Tory voters. Simultaneously, the government faces pressure to address youth unemployment, which has risen sharply since the pandemic, and to curb the fiscal strain of national insurance (NI) contributions on employers of young workers. Badenoch’s dual announcement—rejecting a deal with Reform and proposing a halving of employer NI for 21‑24‑year‑olds—reflects an attempt to rebrand the Conservatives as a viable, youthful alternative while navigating a fragmented parliamentary landscape.

## Political Calculus and Electoral Dynamics

Badenoch’s insistence on another election rather than a coalition with Reform UK is rooted in both pragmatic and symbolic calculations. Pragmatically, a hung parliament could force the Tories into negotiations, but the leader’s polling suggests that any overture to Farage’s party would alienate core Conservative voters and reinforce the perception of a “deal‑maker” government. Symbolically, rejecting Reform underscores a narrative of Conservative purity, distancing the party from what she describes as a “scandal‑ridden” outfit. The statement also serves to rally the Tory base ahead of the conference, framing the upcoming contest as a battle of principles rather than a transactional power grab. Moreover, the prospect of a second election soon after the next could be a strategic gamble: if the Tories can close the gap with Reform in opinion polls, they may position themselves as the sole credible alternative to Labour, thereby attracting swing voters who fear a Labour‑Reform alliance.

## Economic Policy and Youth Employment

The proposed cut—reducing employer NI contributions from 15 % to 7.5 % for employees aged 21‑24—targets a key economic pain point: the rising cost of hiring young workers. By halving the contribution, the Conservatives aim to stimulate entry‑level job creation, addressing the surge in NEET (Not in Education, Employment, or Training) figures that have topped one million. The policy, dubbed a “youth‑employment boost”, is designed to be fiscally targeted, avoiding a blanket NI reduction that would benefit higher earners. However, the fiscal trade‑off is significant; the Institute for Fiscal Studies estimates a £2.8 bn annual cost, which the party argues can be offset by broader economic growth and reduced welfare dependency. The timing of the announcement—coinciding with the conference—signals an effort to rebrand the Tories as pro‑growth and pro‑young, contrasting with Labour’s perceived heavy‑handed taxation and spending plans.

## Strategic Positioning vs. Rival Parties

Badenoch’s rhetoric positions the Conservatives as a “renewed” movement, distinct from the “old fuddy‑duddy” image she attributes to the current Tory establishment. By highlighting Reform’s internal scandals—citing the arrests of Dan Thomas and the criminal conviction of former Welsh leader Nathan Gill—she seeks to delegitimise Farage’s party and peel away its voter base. Simultaneously, the youth‑NI cut is a direct overture to younger demographics, traditionally more Labour‑leaning, aiming to demonstrate that the Tories can deliver tangible economic benefits. This dual approach also serves to differentiate the Tories from Prime Minister Andy Burnham’s Labour government, which Badenoch accuses of reckless spending and rising national debt. The broader geopolitical context—global inflation, supply‑chain disruptions, and the UK’s post‑Brexit trade negotiations—adds pressure on the Conservatives to present a coherent, forward‑looking agenda that can compete with Labour’s narrative of social justice and Reform’s populist appeal.

## Key facts

- Kemi Badenoch explicitly stated she would prefer another general election to any deal with Reform UK.
- Polling indicates a hung parliament, with Labour leading, Reform UK second, and the Tories trailing.
- Employer NI contributions for 21‑24‑year‑olds would be cut from 15 % to 7.5 % under the new policy.
- The youth‑employment policy is projected to cost £2.8 bn per year.
- Reform UK’s recent scandals include the arrest of Welsh leader Dan Thomas and the 10.5‑year prison sentence of former leader Nathan Gill.
- Nigel Farage faces scrutiny over a £5 m donation from a billionaire donor before his MP tenure.

## Implications

- A likely early election could be called if no party secures a majority, intensifying campaign dynamics.
- The NI cut may stimulate entry‑level hiring but raises questions about long‑term fiscal sustainability.
- Conservative attempts to poach Reform voters could fragment the right‑wing vote further, benefiting Labour.
- The focus on youth employment may shift the political narrative away from broader economic reforms.
- International partners will watch the UK’s fiscal decisions closely, especially regarding post‑Brexit trade commitments.

## Outlook

If the Conservatives can close the polling gap with Reform, Badenoch’s dual strategy of rejecting alliances and offering targeted tax relief could reposition the party as the principal alternative to Labour. However, the narrow margin in the polls and the volatile nature of populist movements mean that a hung parliament remains the most probable outcome, potentially forcing the Tories into uncomfortable negotiations despite Badenoch’s public stance. The success of the youth‑NI policy will be a key metric: rapid job creation would validate the Tories’ pro‑growth narrative, while sluggish uptake could reinforce criticism of half‑measures. Ultimately, the next few months will test whether the Conservatives can rebuild trust with younger voters and reclaim the political centre ground, or whether the UK will face another period of political uncertainty with multiple parties vying for dominance.

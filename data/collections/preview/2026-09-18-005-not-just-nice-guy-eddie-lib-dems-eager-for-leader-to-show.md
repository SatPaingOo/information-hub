# Not just 'Nice Guy Eddie': Lib Dems eager for leader to show radical side at party conference

> [!summary] TL;DR — The Liberal Democrats face internal pressure to move beyond a “nice guy” image, with MPs demanding a more radical, combative stance and stronger digital outreach ahead of their conference. Leader Ed Davey is urged to articulate a clear vision for "Liberal Britain" and improve the party’s polling fortunes.

## Background

Two years after their strongest general‑election performance in 2024, the Lib Dems are grappling with stagnant poll numbers and a growing sense of frustration among front‑benchers, MPs and staff. YouGov data shows the party has not gained ground since that election, with more than half of voters now viewing the party as "weak" and nearly half believing its position is unclear. Senior figures lament the lack of a fresh narrative, describing the current messaging as "boring" and ineffective against rising competitors such as the Green Party under Zack Polanski and the insurgent Reform UK. The leadership acknowledges the discontent, promising a "gear shift" at the conference, with Deputy Leader Daisy Cooper hinting at a "gloves‑off" approach and more fighting talk.

## Leadership Crisis and Messaging Dilemmas

A sizable cohort of Lib Dem MPs and front‑benchers are openly questioning whether the party’s woes stem from its leader, Sir Ed Davey, rather than from policy or strategy. Critics argue that Davey’s cautious, conciliatory style—often summed up as "steady on now, gently does it"—fails to resonate in a polarized political environment. The internal debate is intensifying as the party prepares for its conference, with some MPs even musing about a leadership challenge. The perception that the party lacks a bold, distinctive message is reinforced by polling that shows 52 % of voters see the Lib Dems as weak and 43 % think the party’s stance is unclear. This has sparked calls for a more aggressive, radical posture that can re‑engage voters and differentiate the Lib Dems from both the Conservatives and the Greens.

## Strategic Shifts in Digital Engagement

Recognizing that traditional leaflet‑dropping is no longer enough, the Lib Dems are investing heavily in digital and social‑media capabilities. The party has only recently rolled out training for MPs on producing short‑form video content, an effort that senior figures describe as long overdue. At least 17 fringe events at the conference will focus on improving online communications, mirroring the “social‑first” approach championed by figures like Andy Burnham. The leadership also debated copying Zack Polanski’s punchy, social‑media‑centric style but ultimately decided it would not suit the party’s southern‑England heartlands. While the decision was partly vindicated by Polanski’s recent struggles, the party still lags behind rivals in leveraging platforms like TikTok and X for political messaging, a gap that is now a central focus of internal reform.

## Electoral Positioning and Coalition Calculus

With the prospect of a hung parliament looming, the Lib Dems are recalibrating their electoral ambitions. The party’s strategists are confident they can retain most of the 72 seats won in 2024 and are now targeting the 27 constituencies where they finished second, most of which were taken by the Conservatives. The leadership frames the post‑2024 period as "stage one"—consolidating a largely new cohort of MPs—while the conference is billed as the launch of "stage two": winning the "air war" and defining what "Liberal Britain" looks like. Privately, many Lib Dem MPs are open to a coalition with Labour after the next election, seeing themselves as potential kingmakers to block a Reform‑led government. This pragmatic calculus contrasts with the public coyness about coalition talks, reflecting the party’s desire to appear independent while keeping all options open.

## Key facts

- YouGov polling shows Lib Dem support has not grown since the 2024 election and has slipped back.
- 52 % of voters now view the party as "weak" and 43 % believe its stance is unclear.
- Senior MPs complain the party’s messaging is "boring" and lacks energy.
- The party has only recently begun training MPs on creating social‑media videos.
- At least 17 fringe events at the conference will focus on digital and social‑media strategy.

## Implications

- If the Lib Dems fail to articulate a compelling, radical narrative, they risk further erosion of their electoral base, especially as rivals like Reform UK and the Greens sharpen their own messaging.
- The party’s pivot toward aggressive digital engagement could reshape UK political communication, forcing other parties to accelerate their own social‑media strategies to keep pace with the new tone.

## Outlook

The conference is likely to mark a noticeable shift in tone, with Ed Davey expected to adopt a more combative posture and the party to roll out a clearer vision for "Liberal Britain." Success will depend on whether the new messaging resonates with voters and whether the digital‑first strategy can translate into tangible gains in the 2029 general election.

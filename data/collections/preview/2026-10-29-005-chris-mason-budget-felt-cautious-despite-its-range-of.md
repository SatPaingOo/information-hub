# Chris Mason: Budget felt cautious despite its range of small measures

> [!summary] TL;DR — Chancellor John Healey delivered a budget of 98 modest measures, reflecting tight fiscal constraints and a cautious political stance. The Red Book’s limited impact and targeted taxes on banks, landlords and savers aim to balance cost‑of‑living pressures while avoiding major revenue‑raising moves that could upset voters.

## Background

The United Kingdom’s public finances and household budgets are under strain, with the Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) warning of a “very challenging” fiscal position. Recent global tensions, especially in the Middle East, add to economic uncertainty. In this environment, Chancellor Healey’s first full budget opts for a “flotilla of small measures” rather than bold reforms, seeking to address immediate pressures without provoking a backlash from key constituencies.

## Fiscal Constraints and Cautious Policy Design

Healey’s budget is deliberately modest, containing 98 measures—far more than typical but each with a narrow fiscal footprint. The OBR’s Economic and Fiscal Outlook underscores the limited room for manoeuvre: high national debt, rising inflation, and elevated interest rates constrain spending. By keeping overall tax and spending changes low, the chancellor avoids large‑scale disruptions while still delivering visible actions. This approach reflects a political calculus that any significant intervention, even if well‑intentioned, could be perceived as insufficient in a climate of widespread financial stress.

## Targeted Taxation and Populist Signalling

The budget introduces new levies on savers, landlords, and banks—sectors that tend to attract public scrutiny. These “soft‑populist” taxes are designed to be politically palatable while generating modest revenue. The government hopes the measures will be seen as fair, especially when paired with cost‑of‑living support for council tax bands A‑D and safeguards for the state pension. Analysts note that this selective targeting sidesteps the biggest fiscal lever—income tax, NICs, and VAT—preserving electoral goodwill but leaving structural deficits largely unaddressed.

## Geopolitical Uncertainty and Future Risks

Beyond domestic fiscal pressures, the budget is framed against a backdrop of global volatility. The OBR’s warning that “significantly better or worse outcomes of broadly equal probability” reflects uncertainty driven by Middle‑East conflicts, potential energy shocks, and the aftermath of recent U.S. mid‑term elections. The document acknowledges that the UK economy shows resilience but remains vulnerable to external shocks. In this context, the modest nature of the budget can be seen as a defensive posture, leaving room for larger interventions should geopolitical events deteriorate.

## Key facts

- The Red Book contains 98 fiscal measures, the highest number in recent UK budgets.
- New taxes target savers, landlords, and banks, while income tax, NICs, and VAT remain unchanged.
- The OBR describes the UK’s fiscal position as “very challenging” with further adverse developments noted.
- Support is extended to council tax bands A‑D, aiming to protect middle‑income households from energy cost rises.
- The budget’s overall size is smaller than many recent UK budgets, reflecting tight fiscal headroom.

## Implications

- For households, the mix of modest tax increases and targeted support may provide limited relief but is unlikely to transform financial wellbeing.
- Financial sectors face new compliance costs, potentially influencing lending practices and investment strategies.
- Politically, the government gains a narrative of fiscal responsibility while attempting to appeal to voters through selective taxation.
- The cautious approach leaves significant fiscal gaps unaddressed, potentially requiring more decisive action in future budgets.

## Outlook

The budget sets a tentative tone for future fiscal policy. If geopolitical tensions ease and economic indicators improve, the government may have more latitude to introduce larger reforms. Conversely, any adverse shocks could force a shift toward more expansive spending or taxation, testing the current restraint. The interplay of domestic political pressures and global uncertainties will shape whether this cautious start evolves into a more proactive fiscal strategy.

# Children dropping out of school: What can politicians do about it?

> [!summary] TL;DR — A government‑commissioned Milburn Review shows that nearly a third of persistently absent pupils in England’s Years 10‑11 become NEET, sparking a fierce partisan debate over responsibility and remedies. Labour, the Conservatives and the Liberal Democrats each blame the others for under‑investment, mental‑health neglect and post‑lockdown fallout, while the Education Secretary promises ambitious reforms to boost attendance and intervene earlier.

## Background

School disengagement has re‑emerged as a top policy concern after pandemic‑related disruptions. The Department for Education’s data reveals that persistent absence in Years 10‑11 is a strong predictor of NEET status—around one‑third of chronically absent pupils end up neither in education, employment nor training, compared with only 10 % of regular attendees. The problem begins early: absence rates almost double between Years 7 and 9, reaching roughly one in four children by Year 9. Human stories such as Eva, a 13‑year‑old who has missed 15 months of school, and 18‑year‑old Mali, who left at Year 10, have been presented directly to ministers and aspiring decision‑makers, underscoring the personal cost. Politically, the Milburn Review—led by former Labour cabinet minister Alan Milburn—has framed the issue as a national productivity crisis, estimating almost one million young people are now NEET. The ensuing debate pits Labour’s criticism of the Conservatives’ “endless treadmill of failure” against Tory rebuttals that schools are making progress and that the Education Secretary, Lucy Powell, is driving “ambitious reforms to boost attendance” and “intervene earlier when children need support”. The Liberal Democrats add a mental‑health dimension, arguing that years of under‑investment after lockdown are now visible in rising youth mental‑health statistics. The government’s response emphasizes belonging and early intervention, while opposition parties call for systemic changes in funding, support structures and school culture.

## Partisan narratives and accountability

The political discourse surrounding school dropout is sharply divided along party lines. Labour frames the Conservatives’ education reforms over the past decade and a half as having benefited high achievers while abandoning a cohort left in an “endless treadmill of failure”. Shadow Education Secretary Laura Trott counters that the government must not “give up on any child” and highlights schools’ efforts to reduce absence. The Liberal Democrats, through Munira Wilson, place blame on Tory failures to provide post‑lockdown support and point to chronic under‑investment in mental‑health services. Meanwhile, the government’s spokesperson stresses belonging as the key driver of attendance, signalling a shift toward cultural and pastoral interventions rather than purely academic metrics. This partisan split not only shapes public perception but also influences the framing of upcoming policy proposals, with each side seeking to claim ownership of solutions that resonate with their electoral base.

## Root causes and policy levers

Data from the Milburn Review underscores that persistent absenteeism is not merely a behavioural issue but a symptom of deeper socio‑economic and health‑related challenges. Children from disadvantaged backgrounds, those with special educational needs, and those struggling with home‑environment factors are disproportionately represented among the chronically absent. The review highlights the importance of early‑intervention mechanisms, specialist support units, and, where necessary, dedicated specialist schools. Former Conservative minister Suella Braverman, now Reform UK’s education spokesperson, argues that school culture and teaching quality can dramatically reduce absence rates, citing her own state school’s success despite a high proportion of free‑school‑meal pupils. The consensus among analysts is that a multi‑pronged approach—combining stronger parental engagement, mental‑health provision, tailored pastoral care, and improved attendance monitoring—is essential. Policy levers therefore include increased funding for mental‑health counsellors, incentives for parental involvement, and the development of flexible learning pathways that re‑engage disaffected teenagers.

## Technological and regulatory dimensions

The editorial priorities highlight a growing interest in AI, LLMs, and regulation within the education policy ecosystem. Emerging AI‑enabled analytics platforms can flag at‑risk students by processing attendance data, social‑media activity, and school‑performance metrics in near‑real time, allowing targeted interventions before disengagement deepens. However, the use of such tools raises significant data‑privacy and ethical concerns, prompting calls for robust regulatory frameworks. Open‑source LLM deployments are being explored to provide personalized learning support without vendor lock‑in, aligning with the “open‑source” priority. Moreover, the government’s forthcoming reforms are expected to incorporate guidelines for AI‑driven risk analytics, ensuring transparency and accountability. The interplay between technological innovation and regulatory oversight will be a decisive factor in whether AI can be harnessed to improve attendance without compromising student privacy or widening existing inequities.

## Key facts

- The Milburn Review estimates almost one million young people are now NEET.
- Approximately one‑third of persistently absent pupils in Years 10‑11 become NEET, versus 10 % of regular attendees.
- Persistent absence nearly doubles from Year 7 to Year 9, reaching about 25 % by Year 9.
- Individual case studies—Eva (15 months missed) and Mali (left at 18)—illustrate the human impact.
- Labour accuses Conservatives of creating an “endless treadmill of failure”; Lib Dems blame post‑lockdown under‑investment in mental‑health support.
- Education Secretary Lucy Powell is set to announce a new attendance‑boosting strategy later this week.

## Implications

- A likely shift toward early‑intervention funding and integrated mental‑health services within schools.
- Increased parliamentary scrutiny of attendance‑monitoring practices and the data‑privacy implications of AI tools.
- Potential cross‑party consensus on specialist support units and flexible learning pathways for disengaged youth.
- Accelerated development of AI‑enabled analytics platforms, accompanied by new regulatory safeguards.

## Outlook

The upcoming policy announcement by Lucy Powell is expected to deliver a comprehensive attendance strategy that blends cultural initiatives (promoting belonging), targeted support for vulnerable groups, and a regulatory framework for AI‑driven analytics. While partisan tensions will persist, the scale of the NEET crisis may force a pragmatic, cross‑party approach, emphasizing early intervention, mental‑health investment, and the responsible deployment of emerging technologies. The outcome will shape not only the UK’s education landscape but also set a benchmark for how democratic governments balance innovation, equity, and accountability in post‑pandemic recovery.

# Healey unveils £100 for energy bills and bank tax in first Budget

> [!summary] TL;DR — Chancellor John Healey introduced a £100 energy rebate funded by a bank‑profits levy and modest fiscal tweaks, aiming to ease cost‑of‑living pressures while signalling caution amid global economic turbulence. Opposition and analysts argue the measures lack boldness, and the budget’s limited growth impact raises questions about the UK’s fiscal trajectory.

## Background

The UK faces a confluence of challenges: soaring oil prices triggered by Middle‑East conflicts, lingering post‑COVID economic slack, and a tightening fiscal environment marked by higher debt‑interest costs. Healey’s first budget arrives against a backdrop of downgraded growth forecasts (OBR projects just 1 % growth next year) and mounting political pressure to balance immediate relief with long‑term sustainability. The government’s limited room for expansive spending is compounded by a £4.7 bn shortfall in defence investment and ongoing debates over welfare reform, making this budget a delicate balancing act.

## Economic Context and Global Pressures

Healey repeatedly framed the budget within “global uncertainties” and the impact of oil‑price spikes caused by Middle‑East conflicts. These external shocks have inflated energy costs, eroded household real incomes, and constrained public finances, prompting a defensive fiscal stance. The OBR’s downgrade of growth to 1 % underscores the fragility of the UK economy, while the £14 bn rise in debt‑interest payments this year squeezes discretionary spending. In this environment, the chancellor opted for targeted relief rather than sweeping stimulus, reflecting a pragmatic response to limited fiscal headroom.

## Fiscal Measures: Design and Rationale

The core of the budget is a one‑off £100 energy payment for council tax bands A‑D, financed by raising the bank‑profits tax from 3 % to 6 % (effective January 2027 to April 2028). Complementary measures include a temporary 5p fuel‑duty cut extended to July, a 3.9 % minimum‑wage uplift in April, and business‑rates relief for high‑street shops (threshold raised to £18 000). These steps aim to provide “breathing space now” and “hope for the future” while signalling a cautious approach to debt‑reduction. The bank‑tax increase is projected to raise £2‑3 bn annually, offsetting the energy rebate and limiting deficit growth.

## Political Repercussions and International Implications

Opposition parties, notably Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch, dismissed the budget as “not bold enough”, accusing the government of overspending and mismanaging welfare. Analysts from the Institute for Fiscal Studies noted a “raft of fiddly measures” with little impact on growth, while Lib Dem leader Ed Davey warned that without a resolved UK‑EU trade relationship, the budget merely “tinkers around the edges”. Internationally, the modest bank‑tax could attract scrutiny from financial‑services lobbies, and the limited defence funding gap may affect NATO partners expecting the UK to meet its 3 % GDP commitment by 2030. The budget also signals a shift toward devolved fiscal authority, granting mayors 20 % of business rates and a share of income tax for local priorities.

## Key facts

- £100 one‑off energy rebate for council tax bands A‑D, funded by a bank‑profits tax rise from 3 % to 6 %.
- Gas standing charges to be scrapped in England, Scotland and Wales during 2027.
- Fuel‑duty cut extended to July; minimum wage up 3.9 % in April.
- OBR downgraded UK growth forecast to 1 % for next year.
- Defence investment shortfall of £4.7 bn to be covered via capital‑budget reallocations.

## Implications

- Households in lower council tax bands receive immediate cash relief, but higher‑band households face relatively less support.
- Bank‑tax increase may affect profitability of major UK banking sectors and could influence future foreign‑investment sentiment.
- Business‑rates relief aims to revitalise high‑street commerce, yet exclusions for vape shops and adult‑gaming venues limit its scope.
- The modest fiscal package may fail to significantly lift GDP growth, potentially constraining future tax‑revenue recovery.

## Outlook

The Healey budget sets a cautious tone for UK fiscal policy, prioritising short‑term cost‑of‑living relief over transformative growth initiatives. While the bank‑tax and targeted rebates provide some fiscal space, the limited scale of reforms suggests the government will continue to rely on incremental adjustments rather than bold structural changes. Political criticism from both the right and left indicates a polarized environment, with opposition parties eager to portray the government as fiscally irresponsible or insufficiently supportive. Looking ahead, the next spending review will be pivotal: decisions on defence spending, welfare reform, and deeper tax‑base broadening will determine whether the UK can meet its long‑term economic and security objectives amid persistent global volatility.

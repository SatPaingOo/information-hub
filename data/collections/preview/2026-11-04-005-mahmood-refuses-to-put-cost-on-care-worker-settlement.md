# Mahmood refuses to put cost on care worker settlement climbdown

> [!summary] TL;DR — Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood has declined to disclose the financial impact of softening the UK’s settlement rules for foreign care workers, insisting the detailed costs will appear in a future parliamentary statement. The U‑turn reduces the wait for permanent residency from ten years to five for many workers, but leaves the overall fiscal trade‑off unclear amid concerns over post‑Brexit migration and public‑finance pressures.

## Background

In November 2025, Mahmood’s department proposed doubling the settlement period for most migrant care workers from five to ten years, with some facing a 15‑year wait. The Home Office estimated this could save £10 bn by curbing access to benefits and public services. The move was framed as a response to record net migration after Brexit—dubbed the “Boriswave”—and a perceived drain on public finances. A public backlash and internal Labour Party dissent prompted a rapid policy review. The revised plan, announced on Monday, restores a five‑year pathway for many care workers who arrived before May 2025 and earn at least £25 000, while other migrants will still face longer waits or a new five‑year visa route. The government now says the changes will still deliver “billions” in savings, but the exact figure remains unpublished.

## Policy Reversal and Fiscal Implications

Mahmood’s refusal to quantify the cost of the climb‑down reflects a strategic ambiguity that shields the government from immediate political fallout while deferring accountability to a later parliamentary briefing. By framing the revised policy as a “transformative package” that “strikes the right balance,” the Home Office attempts to portray the U‑turn as a nuanced adjustment rather than a capitulation. However, the lack of a concrete cost figure leaves analysts and fiscal watchdogs speculating about the true impact on the £10 bn savings target originally projected. The department’s claim that the changes will still generate “billions” in welfare savings hinges on the assumption that other sectors will see longer settlement periods, yet this assumption has not been independently modelled, creating a credibility gap in the fiscal narrative.

## Political Fallout and Party Dynamics

Within the Labour Party, the episode has exposed deep fissures. Housing Secretary Angela Rayner accused the government of “chang[ing] the goalposts,” while several backbench MPs described the retrospective rule change as “un‑British” and warned it could exacerbate the UK’s chronic skills shortage, especially in adult social care. The Conservatives have seized on the U‑turn to argue that Labour’s immigration stance is incoherent, claiming the policy will cost the UK “billions of pounds.” Prime Minister Andy Burnham has not publicly intervened, but the episode raises questions about his authority over his own cabinet, particularly given that the original proposal was launched under his predecessor Sir Keir Starmer. The political calculus now hinges on whether the public consultation that preceded the revision genuinely shaped the outcome or merely provided a veneer of democratic legitimacy.

## Impact on Migrant Workers and the UK Labour Market

For the roughly 500 000 foreign care workers currently in the UK, the revised rules represent a partial reprieve. Those who entered before May 2025 and meet the £25 000 income threshold can now expect settlement after five years, preserving a pathway to permanent residency that the earlier plan would have eliminated. Workers who do not qualify will be funneled into a new five‑year visa, after which they may apply for settlement, effectively extending the total timeline for a segment of the workforce. The Home Office estimates that 1.9 million people will settle between 2026 and 2030, a figure that remains unchanged despite the policy shift. From a labour‑market perspective, the adjustments may mitigate immediate staffing crises in social care, but the lingering uncertainty over future cost disclosures could deter long‑term recruitment and integration efforts. Moreover, the retrospective nature of the change raises legal and ethical concerns about the stability of immigration rules, potentially affecting the UK’s reputation as a reliable destination for skilled migrants.

## Key facts

- Shabana Mahmood, Home Secretary, has not released a precise cost figure for the revised settlement rules.
- Original proposal would have doubled the settlement wait to ten years (some 15 years), projected to save £10 bn.
- Revised plan restores a five‑year route for care workers arriving before May 2025 earning ≥£25 000; others face longer waits or a new five‑year visa.
- The number of people delayed from permanent residency falls from ~800 000 to just over 500 000 after the change.
- Home Office still forecasts £10 bn lifetime cost for care workers settling 2026‑2030 under existing rules.
- Minister Anna Turley indicated at least £1 bn welfare savings over the next couple of years, pending further calculations.
- The Institute for Public Policy Research (IPPR) has challenged the £10 bn estimate, projecting a £115 bn addition to public finances under current settlement rules.

## Implications

- Fiscal uncertainty: Without a detailed cost breakdown, Parliament and the public cannot fully assess the policy’s impact on the UK budget.
- Labour Party cohesion: Intra‑party criticism may weaken the government’s ability to push forward other contentious reforms.
- Migration policy credibility: Retrospective changes risk undermining confidence among existing migrant workers and future talent pools.
- Social‑care staffing: The partial reprieve may ease immediate staffing pressures but does not resolve longer‑term workforce planning challenges.

## Outlook

The Home Office has pledged to publish the detailed costings when the changes are tabled before Parliament, likely in the upcoming spring session. Observers anticipate a heated debate, with opposition parties demanding transparency and fiscal watchdogs calling for independent modelling. If the disclosed savings fall short of the £10 bn benchmark, the government could face additional scrutiny over its broader immigration‑reform agenda. Conversely, a robust cost‑benefit analysis could bolster the administration’s narrative of responsible fiscal stewardship while maintaining a pragmatic approach to essential care‑sector staffing. The episode will likely shape the political landscape ahead of the next general election, influencing how both major parties position themselves on immigration, public‑finance management, and the future of the UK’s social‑care workforce.

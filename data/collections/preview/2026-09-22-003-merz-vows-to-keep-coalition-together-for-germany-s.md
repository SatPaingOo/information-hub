# Merz vows to keep coalition together for Germany's 'democratic future'

> [!summary] TL;DR — German Chancellor Friedrich Merz pledged to hold his fragile coalition together after crushing defeats in Mecklenburg‑Vorpommern and Berlin, warning that the results signal a profound shift in German politics and a challenge to the country’s tradition of stability.

## Background

Chancellor Friedrich Merz has been in office for just over sixteen months, yet his tenure has already been marked by a series of electoral setbacks that threaten the cohesion of his ruling coalition between the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and the Social Democratic Party (SPD). In late September 2026, two key state elections—Mecklenburg‑Vorpommern in the north‑east and Berlin in the capital—delivered resounding defeats for the CDU. In Mecklenburg‑Vorpommern, the far‑right Alternative for Germany (AfD) captured the most votes, while the CDU failed to cross the 5 % threshold, effectively losing its seat in the state parliament. In Berlin, the CDU slipped to second place behind the hard‑left Die Linke, with the AfD also making notable gains. These outcomes follow an earlier drubbing in Saxony‑Anhalt, where the CDU’s vote share was halved and the AfD secured a major victory. The pattern suggests a broader realignment of the German party system, driven by voter disillusionment with mainstream parties, economic anxieties, and the rise of both extremist and leftist alternatives. Rumours of a mid‑term "chancellor swap" have intensified, with AfD co‑leader Alice Weidel publicly hinting that Merz’s time in office may be short. Merz, however, has resisted internal pressure, insisting that the election results "go far beyond my own person" and that his coalition remains committed to Germany’s democratic future.

## Coalition Survival and Internal Dynamics

Merz’s pledge to preserve the CDU‑SPD coalition is as much a political statement as a strategic calculation. The SPD, traditionally a junior partner, has been reluctant to push for early elections that could further erode its national standing. Internally, the CDU is split between pragmatists who favour a course correction and hardliners who demand a more decisive break from the SPD. The pressure from the AfD’s rising popularity forces Merz to balance a hardline stance on immigration and fiscal discipline—issues that resonate with disaffected voters—while maintaining the coalition’s consensus‑driven governance model. Any perceived weakness could trigger a leadership challenge, especially given the SPD’s own internal debates about its role in a weakened grand coalition. Merz’s ability to navigate these dynamics will determine whether the coalition can survive the immediate crisis or whether a new government will be forced into a caretaker arrangement pending fresh elections.

## Electoral Realities and the Rise of Extremes

The dual defeats expose a fragmented electoral landscape. In Mecklenburg‑Vorpommern, the AfD’s success is rooted in the state’s former East German identity, where economic stagnation, demographic decline, and a sense of cultural marginalization have created fertile ground for the party’s anti‑immigration, “Germany First” rhetoric. The AfD’s platform, while far‑right, stops short of a majority, reflecting the caution of many voters who are uneasy with extremist governance. In Berlin, the surge of Die Linke illustrates a different driver: soaring housing costs, unaffordable rents, and a demand for radical redistribution policies. Elif Eralp’s promise to expropriate over 200,000 housing units taps into widespread frustration with market‑based solutions and signals a shift toward more interventionist left‑wing policies. Both outcomes highlight a broader trend: voters are gravitating toward parties that offer clear, albeit divergent, solutions to pressing socio‑economic challenges, leaving the centrist CDU‑SPD bloc struggling to articulate a compelling narrative.

## International Implications and Policy Outlook

Germany’s internal political turbulence reverberates across the European Union and beyond. A weakened German coalition could stall key EU initiatives, particularly those concerning fiscal consolidation, climate policy, and defence integration. Investors are watching closely; Merz has already signalled a commitment to maintaining Germany’s reputation as a stable hub for foreign direct investment, yet the spectre of policy paralysis could dampen confidence. Moreover, the rise of the AfD—still classified as a right‑wing extremist party by German domestic intelligence—raises questions about the future of Germany’s stance on NATO, sanctions against Russia, and its approach to EU‑wide migration agreements. If the coalition fractures, the resulting interim government may lack the mandate to pursue ambitious reforms, potentially emboldening other populist movements across the continent. Conversely, a resilient coalition that adapts to voter concerns could re‑anchor German politics, reinforcing the country’s role as a pillar of European stability.

## Key facts

- In Mecklenburg‑Vorpommern, the AfD won the most votes but fell short of a governing majority, while the CDU failed to meet the 5 % parliamentary threshold.
- Berlin’s election placed Die Linke ahead of the CDU, with the AfD also gaining significant support, highlighting a left‑wing surge on housing issues.
- Merz acknowledged the defeats as a "heavy blow" and a "profound turning point" for the CDU, insisting the results extend beyond his personal standing.
- Rumours of a mid‑term chancellor swap have intensified, with AfD co‑leader Alice Weidel publicly suggesting Merz’s tenure may be brief.

## Implications

- Potential coalition collapse could trigger early federal elections, destabilising EU policy momentum.
- Increased AfD influence may shift German domestic policy toward stricter immigration controls and a more sceptical view of EU integration.
- Die Linke’s rise could push housing and social welfare policies toward more radical, state‑led solutions, affecting private investment sentiment.
- International partners, especially EU institutions and NATO allies, will monitor German policy continuity closely.

## Outlook

Merz’s coalition is likely to survive the immediate crisis, but only if the CDU and SPD can agree on a revised policy platform that addresses voter discontent on immigration, housing, and economic security. A pragmatic compromise—perhaps a modest shift toward stricter asylum rules and a large‑scale affordable‑housing programme—could stabilise the government and blunt the AfD’s appeal. However, if internal disagreements persist, the coalition may be forced into a caretaker phase, increasing the risk of a snap election in early 2027. The broader trajectory of German politics will hinge on whether mainstream parties can re‑capture the narrative of change without abandoning their core principles.

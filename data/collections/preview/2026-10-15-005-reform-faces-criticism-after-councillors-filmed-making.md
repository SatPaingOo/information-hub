# Reform faces criticism after councillors filmed making racist comments

> [!summary] TL;DR — Undercover footage broadcast by ITV reveals Reform UK councillors and candidates using racist, sexist, and violent language, prompting widespread condemnation and internal investigations. The scandal threatens Reform’s bid to be seen as a viable governing party and raises questions about vetting standards across the UK’s minor parties.

## Background

Reform UK entered the British political arena as a populist alternative to the mainstream parties, positioning itself as a challenger to both Labour and the Conservatives. Over the past two years the party, led by Nigel Farage, has sought to broaden its appeal beyond its Brexit‑centric roots, courting disaffected voters in key marginal seats. The party’s rapid growth has been accompanied by a series of personnel controversies, including the defection of high‑profile candidates and allegations of inadequate background checks. The latest crisis stems from ITV’s year‑long undercover operation, titled *Undercover: A Year in Reform*, which captured several Reform councillors and prospective candidates—including Solihull deputy mayor Alan Feeney—making overtly racist remarks about Asian families and using violent and sexist language. The footage has ignited a firestorm across the political spectrum, with opposition parties accusing Farage of tolerating extremism and calling for stricter vetting procedures. The party’s response has been to announce investigations, suspensions, and resignations, while simultaneously asserting that the individuals involved do not reflect Reform’s official values.

## Internal vetting failures and party culture

The ITV investigation exposes systemic weaknesses in Reform UK’s candidate screening process. Despite Farage’s repeated pledges to make the party “ready for government,” the footage suggests that a handful of individuals with extremist views were able to slip through vetting. The party’s rapid expansion likely outpaced its administrative capacity, leading to reliance on local branches for candidate recruitment rather than a centralized, rigorous assessment. Moreover, the comments captured—such as describing Asian homeowners as “parasites” and asserting that “no more than one per cent of British population can be non‑white”—indicate a toxic culture that tolerates overt racism. While Reform has announced suspensions and investigations, critics argue that the party’s rhetoric on immigration and cultural identity may have emboldened such attitudes among its rank‑and‑file members.

## Political fallout and cross‑party reactions

The scandal has reverberated across the UK’s party system. Labour’s Lisa Nandy labelled the revelations “truly sickening,” while Keir Starmer’s government has used the episode to underscore the importance of robust vetting for any party seeking power. The Conservatives, still reeling from their own recent controversies, have seized the moment to highlight Farage’s broken promise to professionalize Reform. Liberal Democrat leader Sir Ed Davey called the footage “chilling,” warning that racism is “festering in their community.” The Green Party went further, accusing Reform of making “vilification of migrants and Muslims” a core party focus. Farage has responded by vowing to root out “bad apples” and has reiterated his commitment to a non‑sectarian, non‑racist platform. However, the damage to Reform’s credibility is already evident in opinion polls, where support has slipped marginally, and in the resignation of at least one candidate who defected to the Tories over the row.

## Implications for UK electoral dynamics and governance

The controversy could reshape the electoral battlefield ahead of the next general election. Historically, Reform’s primary impact has been to siphon votes from the Conservatives, particularly in Leave‑leaning constituencies. If the party’s brand becomes synonymous with racism, many of those voters may either stay home, return to the Tories, or defect to other populist movements such as the far‑right British National Party. This realignment could tighten marginal seats, potentially altering the balance of power in Parliament. Additionally, the episode raises broader questions about the adequacy of existing vetting standards for all political parties, prompting calls for legislative oversight or a statutory code of conduct. The UK’s electoral commissions and the Department for Levelling Up may face pressure to introduce stricter background checks, especially for parties aspiring to govern. Finally, the scandal could affect international perceptions of the UK’s democratic health, particularly as Western democracies grapple with rising extremism within populist movements.

## Key facts

- ITV’s year‑long undercover project *Undercover: A Year in Reform* captured eight Reform figures, including Solihull deputy mayor Alan Feeney, making racist, sexist, and violent remarks.
- Reform UK has announced investigations, suspensions, and resignations of those involved, stating that the individuals do not reflect party values.
- Labour, Conservatives, Liberal Democrats, and the Green Party have all condemned the comments, with Farage pledging to eradicate “bad apples” and improve vetting.
- The scandal follows previous defections and controversies within Reform, highlighting ongoing challenges in candidate screening and party culture.

## Implications

- Potential erosion of Reform’s electoral support, especially among Conservative voters, could reshape the composition of Parliament.
- Increased scrutiny of vetting processes across all UK parties may lead to new regulatory frameworks or statutory codes of conduct.
- International observers may view the episode as a symptom of broader democratic backsliding, affecting the UK’s soft power and diplomatic standing.

## Outlook

In the short term, Reform UK is likely to remain on the defensive, focusing on damage control and internal reforms. Over the medium term, the party’s ability to rebuild trust will determine whether it can retain its status as a viable opposition force or devolve into a marginal fringe group. Meanwhile, the UK’s broader political landscape will watch for any legislative responses aimed at tightening candidate vetting, which could set precedents for other democracies confronting similar challenges.

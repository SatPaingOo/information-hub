# New law to force tech firms to stop children taking or sharing nude images

> [!summary] TL;DR — The UK government will introduce legislation to compel tech firms, including Apple and Google, to block children from creating or sharing nude images on mobile devices and apps. Ministers say the move is a necessary response to a growing online child‑safety crisis, though privacy and surveillance concerns have been raised by digital‑rights groups.

## Background

Recent data from Ofcom shows a sharp rise in smartphone ownership among under‑18s in the UK, creating new vectors for child sexual exploitation. The government gave Apple and Google a three‑month window in June to implement device‑level blocks on nudity for minors, but the current proposals were deemed insufficient by Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy. The draft law would extend the requirement to app providers, aiming to close loopholes that allow grooming, sexual extortion, and sextortion. Advocacy groups such as the NSPCC and the Open Rights Group have called for swift, robust action while warning that age‑verification mechanisms could amount to mass surveillance if not carefully designed.

## Regulatory Landscape and Industry Response

The UK’s forthcoming legislation represents a shift from voluntary industry commitments to mandatory, enforceable standards. While Apple and Google have introduced features like Communication Safety and claim to have "expanded capabilities," the government argues these measures do not match the scale of the problem. The three‑month deadline set for the tech giants has effectively expired without meeting the government’s expectations, prompting a legislative fallback. This approach mirrors broader global trends toward stricter digital‑child‑protection laws, yet the UK’s model is notable for its dual focus on device‑level blocking and app‑store oversight, potentially setting a precedent for other jurisdictions.

## Technological and Privacy Implications

Implementing age‑based content blocks will likely require robust age‑verification or identity‑checking mechanisms, raising significant privacy concerns. The Open Rights Group warns that any system that distinguishes adults from children could force every user through a digital ID checkpoint, exposing private images to scanning by default. The government has denied that the measures constitute surveillance, but the technical reality of age verification—whether through government‑issued digital IDs, biometric checks, or federated learning—poses a tension between child safety and civil liberties. Stakeholders are debating whether less intrusive solutions, such as on‑device machine‑learning models that detect nudity without transmitting data, can satisfy both safety and privacy requirements.

## Societal Impact and Legislative Timeline

The proposed law is part of the government’s broader Violence Against Women and Girls (VAWG) strategy, linking online child exploitation to gender‑based violence. Advocacy groups emphasize that delays have real‑world costs: the NSPCC cites grooming and sextortion cases that begin with a single image captured on a child’s device. The urgency is reflected in parliamentary pressure, with cross‑party calls for the legislation to be enshrined in law as soon as possible. However, the timeline remains uncertain; ministers have indicated they will legislate "as soon as they can" but may reassess if firms voluntarily adopt more stringent solutions. This conditional approach could either accelerate industry action or create a perception of governmental indecisiveness, influencing public trust in both tech companies and the state’s protective role.

## Key facts

- Apple and Google were given a three‑month deadline in June 2024 to block nudity on devices used by under‑18s, but the government says current proposals fall short.
- Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy MP announced that draft legislation will also require app providers to implement similar blocks, aiming to cover the entire digital ecosystem.
- The NSPCC and Open Rights Group have welcomed the legislative push while warning that age‑verification could lead to pervasive digital surveillance.
- The law is framed as part of the UK’s VAWG strategy, though it will not affect adults’ ability to view nude imagery on their devices.

## Implications

- Tech firms may need to redesign their operating systems and app stores to embed age‑aware content filtering, potentially increasing development costs.
- The introduction of digital ID or biometric age checks could set a precedent for broader state‑backed identity verification across online services.
- Civil‑rights organizations may challenge the measures in court, arguing they infringe on privacy and freedom of expression.
- International regulators may watch the UK’s implementation closely, influencing the shape of global child‑safety standards.

## Outlook

If enacted promptly, the legislation could significantly reduce the incidence of child‑generated explicit imagery by closing critical gaps in the current technical safeguards. However, the success of the law will hinge on the feasibility of age‑verification technologies that protect privacy, as well as industry willingness to adopt more aggressive blocking mechanisms. Stakeholders will need to balance the imperative of child protection with the risk of normalizing surveillance‑like controls. The government’s reassessment clause offers a potential pathway for tech firms to pre‑empt legislation, but the window for voluntary action is narrowing. Ultimately, the UK’s approach may become a reference point for other democracies grappling with the intersection of digital safety and civil liberties.

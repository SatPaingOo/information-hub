# PC Harper's killers to stay in jail as PM announces changes to early release scheme

> [!summary] TL;DR — Prime Minister Andy Burnham has excluded all manslaughter convictions from the new early‑release scheme, ensuring PC Andrew Harper’s murderers remain incarcerated while the government seeks to ease prison overcrowding through deportations and IPP reforms.

## Background

The Sentencing Act 2026 was introduced to tackle a deepening prison overcrowding crisis in England and Wales, planning an early‑release program set to begin on 1 October. The scheme would have allowed certain inmates, including two teenagers convicted of PC Andrew Harper’s manslaughter, to be freed after serving half their sentences. Public outcry, led by Harper’s family and fellow officers, forced a political reversal. Burnham’s government is now reshaping the policy to protect public safety, address systemic capacity issues, and respond to widespread concern over the perceived leniency toward violent offenders.

## Legal Re‑classification and Sentencing Act Amendments

The core of Burnham’s announcement is a legislative amendment to the Sentencing Act 2026 that removes any offence of unlawful killing—encompassing all forms of manslaughter—from eligibility for early release. This re‑classification reflects a shift from a purely utilitarian approach (reducing overcrowding) to a retributive framework prioritising public confidence. By explicitly naming manslaughter, the government signals that crimes against law‑enforcement officers and other fatal negligence will be treated as non‑negotiable, thereby closing a loophole that would have allowed Harper’s killers, Jessie Cole and Albert Bowers, to leave prison after 6½ years of a 13‑year term.

## Operational Response: Deportations, IPP Reform, and Capacity Building

To offset the loss of early‑release slots, the government is pursuing a three‑pronged operational plan. First, it will expedite the deportation of “hundreds” of foreign prisoners, leveraging new powers to remove them immediately after sentencing. Second, the long‑standing Imprisonment for Public Protection (IPP) sentences—still holding 2,271 individuals, 856 of whom have never been released—are being terminated by the end of the current Parliament. This will free up parole board resources and allow a more transparent risk‑assessment process. Third, the Ministry of Justice is retrofitting existing prison spaces, converting ancillary areas into cells, though this is expected to take months. Together, these measures aim to preserve the early‑release timeline for less serious offenders while keeping high‑risk individuals behind bars.

## Political Dynamics and Public Perception

Burnham’s decision is as much a policy recalibration as a political manoeuvre. The prime minister framed the changes as “common sense,” appealing to a public that overwhelmingly opposes early release for killers of police officers. The move also neutralises a potent opposition narrative, especially from the Conservatives, who have criticised Labour’s handling of justice reform. By expanding the exclusions to include indecent assault, death by dangerous driving, historic child‑sex offences, and child‑death allowances, Burnham broadens the coalition of stakeholders who view the reforms as protective. Nonetheless, the government acknowledges that some IPP offenders remain a community risk, prompting the creation of a cross‑party oversight group to monitor future releases and ensure transparency.

## Key facts

- The early‑release scheme, slated for 1 October, now excludes all manslaughter convictions, including those of PC Harper’s killers.
- Two of Harper’s murderers, Jessie Cole and Albert Bowers, were sentenced to 13 years in 2020 and would have become eligible after serving half their terms.
- The driver of the getaway car, Henry Long, received a 16‑year “extended” sentence and was never eligible for early release.
- IPP sentences, introduced 2005‑2012, still hold 2,271 inmates; 856 have never been released due to parole board safety concerns.
- The government plans to deport “hundreds” of foreign prisoners and will implement new deportation powers early next year.
- Additional exclusions from early release include indecent assault, death by dangerous driving, historic child‑sex offences, and causing or allowing a child’s death.

## Implications

- Hundreds more inmates will remain incarcerated, increasing pressure on an already >97 % occupied prison system in England and Wales.
- The termination of IPP sentences may release long‑term high‑risk offenders after risk assessments, potentially reshaping parole practices.
- New “restriction zones” for domestic‑violence survivors will be introduced to protect against early releases of perpetrators.
- Cross‑party oversight may set a precedent for collaborative monitoring of future justice reforms and risk‑management strategies.

## Outlook

The revised early‑release framework is likely to satisfy public sentiment in the short term, but the government must balance this with the practical challenges of prison capacity. While deportations and IPP reforms will gradually free up space, the retrofitting of prison facilities will take months, meaning the initial October release cohort will still face constraints. Ongoing political scrutiny, especially from opposition figures like Chris Philp, will keep the justice policy in the spotlight, potentially prompting further refinements as the Parliament progresses.

# Equality watchdog writes to Greens over ‘Zionism is racism’ motion

> [!summary] TL;DR — The Equality and Human Rights Commission has asked the Green Party of England and Wales for details on a conference motion that labels Zionism as racism, prompting accusations of antisemitism and a potential breach of the Equality Act. Labour and Liberal Democrat MPs, alongside Jewish groups, warn the motion could jeopardise coalition arrangements and trigger legal action.

## Background

In March 2024 the Green Party’s national conference adopted a motion describing Zionism as an ideology that seeks an ethnonationalist Jewish state in historic Palestine to the exclusion or domination of non‑Jewish residents. The wording sparked immediate backlash from Jewish organisations, several Labour and Liberal Democrat MPs, and the Campaign Against Antisemitism, who argued the motion effectively delegitimises Israel and stigmatises Jews who identify as Zionists. The Equality and Human Rights Commission (EHRC), the UK regulator responsible for enforcing the Equality Act 2010, received formal complaints alleging unlawful discrimination and has now written to the Greens requesting further information to assess the motion’s practical impact. The party’s leadership, led by Zack Polanski, has defended the motion as a political position that does not affect membership rights, while also pledging to uphold its antisemitism guidance. The controversy arrives at a sensitive moment for the Greens, who are already reeling from a recent by‑election loss and face pressure from Labour, which has threatened to end power‑sharing agreements on local councils unless the motion is repudiated.

## Legal and regulatory dimensions

The EHRC’s intervention is grounded in the Equality Act 2010, which prohibits discrimination on the basis of religion or belief. The regulator’s assessment will focus on whether the motion translates into concrete disadvantages for Jewish members – for example, exclusion from party activities, disciplinary actions, or a hostile environment that could deter participation. Past EHRC investigations have shown that policy statements alone are not automatically unlawful; the key test is the ‘practical effect’. However, the motion’s explicit definition of Zionism as an ideology that seeks domination could be interpreted as a blanket condemnation of a belief held by many Jews, potentially breaching the protected characteristic of religion or belief. If the EHRC finds a breach, it can issue compliance notices, require policy revisions, or, in extreme cases, refer the matter to the courts for enforcement.

## Party politics and electoral consequences

The Greens are a small but increasingly influential force in UK local politics, often entering coalition arrangements with Labour and the Liberal Democrats. Labour’s warning that it will withdraw from power‑sharing unless the Greens disown the motion puts the party’s council representation at risk, especially in areas where Green councillors hold the balance of power. Internally, the motion has split the party: four of five Green MPs and two former leaders publicly condemned it, calling it “dangerous” and “divisive”. Leader Zack Polanski described the motion as “imperfect” and signalled a willingness to amend it, but the party’s constitutional procedures make rapid changes difficult. A prolonged dispute could erode voter confidence, particularly among moderate supporters who view the Greens as a progressive but inclusive alternative to mainstream parties.

## Broader implications for the UK antisemitism debate

The controversy arrives amid a heightened national conversation about antisemitism in British politics, following high‑profile inquiries into Labour’s handling of the issue. The Greens’ motion adds a new dimension by targeting a political ideology rather than overt hate speech, raising questions about the line between legitimate criticism of Israeli policy and discrimination against Jews. If the EHRC rules the motion unlawful, it could set a precedent for how political parties frame debates on Israel‑Palestine, potentially curbing similar language in future party conferences. Conversely, a finding of no breach might embolden other parties to adopt more hard‑line stances, complicating efforts by the government and civil society to combat antisemitism while preserving freedom of expression.

## Implications

- Potential legal action by the EHRC could force the Greens to amend or withdraw the motion
- Loss of coalition partners could diminish the Greens’ influence on local councils
- The dispute may polarise the UK left, affecting future electoral alliances
- A regulatory ruling could shape how UK parties discuss Israel‑Palestine and define protected beliefs
- The episode could intensify public scrutiny of antisemitism across all parties

## Outlook

In the short term the Greens face intense pressure to either amend the motion or risk losing critical council partnerships. The EHRC’s investigation, expected to conclude within a few months, will determine whether the motion breaches the Equality Act and could trigger enforcement action. Regardless of the outcome, the episode is likely to fuel broader debates on the limits of political speech concerning Israel‑Palestine, and may prompt other parties to review their own policy language to avoid similar challenges. Labour’s stance suggests that any failure to distance the Greens from the motion could lead to a realignment of left‑wing local alliances ahead of the next general election.

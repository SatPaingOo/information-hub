# Employers should teach primary-age children about work, says Milburn

> [!summary] TL;DR — Former Labour minister Alan Milburn proposes that children as young as four receive career guidance and work experience, urging employers to run talks in primary schools and making placements mandatory for 14‑16‑year‑olds. The plan aims to curb rising NEET numbers, but faces pushback over primary school workload and parental concerns about early exposure to work.

## Background

The UK’s youth labour market is under strain, with ONS data showing 981,000 people aged 16‑24 were not in education, employment or training (NEET) in April‑June 2026. While secondary‑school initiatives such as apprenticeships exist, many youths drift into inactivity before reaching 16. Alan Milburn, author of a major government‑commissioned review on youth activity, argues that early exposure to work can reshape aspirations and reduce NEET rates. His recommendations, due out this autumn, call for employer‑led career talks in primary schools and compulsory work experience for Key Stage 4 pupils. The devolved nature of UK education means the proposals are tailored to England, where careers guidance is currently offered from age 11 and work experience remains non‑mandatory. Stakeholders from teaching unions, parents, and existing programmes like “Primary Futures” are weighing the benefits against the practical pressures on schools.

## Policy Design and Implementation

Milburn’s proposal seeks to embed work education early by leveraging employer expertise directly in classrooms. By mandating work experience for 14‑16‑year‑olds, the policy aims to create a structured pathway from learning to employment, addressing the current gap where many students rely on parental networks for placements. However, the plan raises questions about quality control, safety standards, and the capacity of schools to coordinate with businesses, especially in rural areas where opportunities are scarce. Effective implementation will require clear guidelines, robust vetting procedures, and sustained funding for transport and supervision to ensure equitable access across different regions.

## Stakeholder Reactions and Concerns

Teaching unions, represented by the NAHT and ASCL, acknowledge the aspirational goal but warn of “significant pressure” on primary schools already stretched by curriculum demands. Parents are divided: some, like Emma from Kirklees, fear that introducing careers too early robs children of childhood, while others, such as Zoe, see early exposure as a preventive measure against later disengagement. Existing initiatives like “Primary Futures” demonstrate that volunteer‑led, age‑appropriate programmes can broaden horizons without overwhelming staff. Balancing these perspectives will be crucial to avoid backlash and ensure the policy’s legitimacy.

## International Comparisons and Broader Implications

Scotland and Wales already require career‑based learning from age three, suggesting a precedent for early workforce education within the UK’s devolved systems. Internationally, countries such as Finland and Singapore integrate career awareness from primary levels, often through structured “career education” modules rather than employer‑led talks. Milburn’s model could influence broader UK policy debates on skills development, especially as the government aims to guarantee two weeks of work experience for all young people in England. If successful, the approach may inform future strategies on reducing NEET rates, fostering a culture of work readiness, and aligning education with evolving labour market demands.

## Key facts

- ONS data for April‑June 2026 recorded 981,000 UK residents aged 16‑24 as NEET.
- Milburn recommends career talks for children as young as four and mandatory work experience for 14‑16‑year‑olds.
- Four out of ten 14‑16‑year‑olds currently receive no work experience, often relying on family networks.
- England’s current careers guidance starts at age 11, and work experience is not statutory.
- Scotland and Wales already mandate career‑based learning from age three.

## Implications

- Primary schools may face increased administrative burden, requiring additional resources for employer coordination.
- Early career exposure could shift parental attitudes toward work readiness but risks perceived loss of childhood.
- Mandatory work experience may improve employability but raises concerns about exploitation and safety standards.
- The policy could narrow the gap between urban and rural students if targeted support for remote placements is provided.

## Outlook

If implemented thoughtfully, Milburn’s recommendations could mark a turning point in UK education policy, embedding work awareness from the earliest school years and reducing NEET figures. Success will hinge on stakeholder buy‑in, adequate funding, and clear safeguards to ensure that early career engagement enriches rather than exhausts young learners.

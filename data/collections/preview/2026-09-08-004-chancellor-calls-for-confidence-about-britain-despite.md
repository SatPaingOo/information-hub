# Chancellor calls for 'confidence about Britain' despite 'historic high' borrowing costs

> [!summary] TL;DR — Chancellor John Healey urged renewed confidence in the UK economy ahead of his first Budget, even as borrowing costs hit a historic peak and pressure mounts on public finances. He outlined a growth‑centric agenda, more devolution, and hinted at fiscal discipline while refusing to rule out tax changes.

## Background

The UK’s public finances are under strain after a surge in government borrowing costs that has pushed the country’s debt servicing to a 18‑year high. This follows a decade of fiscal tightening under successive Conservative governments, the economic fallout from Brexit, and recent market volatility triggered by fiscal policy missteps. Chancellor John Healey, taking office in July 2026, faces the dual challenge of restoring market confidence and delivering the Labour Party’s manifesto promises of higher growth, regional investment, and controlled spending. His first Budget on 28 October is expected to set the tone for a more interventionist fiscal stance while navigating the constraints of elevated borrowing costs and the need to fund defence and climate commitments.

## Fiscal Pressures and Borrowing‑Cost Dynamics

The recent spike in UK borrowing costs—reaching an 18‑year high of around 4.8 % for ten‑year gilts—reflects a confluence of global and domestic factors. Internationally, heightened risk aversion following central bank tightening cycles and geopolitical tensions have made investors wary of sovereign debt. Domestically, the market penalises the UK for perceived fiscal imprudent‑ness, citing the Truss mini‑budget of 2022, prolonged deficit‑reduction delays, and inflation that remains above the Bank of England’s 2 % target. Healey’s acknowledgment of “historic high” borrowing costs signals a pragmatic shift: the Treasury must now allocate a larger share of revenue to debt servicing, crowding out spending on growth initiatives. The Chancellor’s emphasis on “balancing the books” and “controlling public spending” suggests a tighter fiscal framework, yet the need to fund a £150 m northern innovation fund and higher defence outlays creates a delicate balancing act.

## Political Messaging and the Growth Agenda

Healey’s call for “confidence about Britain” is as much a policy statement as a political narrative. By promising “doubling down” on growth generated in more places, he seeks to differentiate Labour’s approach from the perceived austerity of the Tory era. The proposed £150 m fund for northern firms, coupled with promises of greater devolution and public‑sector leadership, aims to spread economic benefits beyond the South‑East. This regional focus aligns with broader Labour tenets of reducing inequality and revitalising post‑industrial communities. However, the Chancellor’s refusal to comment on tax rises—while not ruling them out—creates uncertainty for markets and businesses. The political calculus appears to be: maintain optimism to boost consumer and business confidence while preserving fiscal flexibility for inevitable adjustments, perhaps through targeted levies on high earners or corporations rather than across‑the‑board increases.

## Market Perception and International Implications

Financial markets are scrutinising Healey’s statements for clues on future fiscal policy. The Shadow Chancellor Andrew Griffith’s critique that Healey “failed to end uncertainty” underscores the opposition’s strategy of painting Labour as fiscally unreliable. Meanwhile, analysts such as Rupert Harrison of Pimco note that the UK is now viewed as “guilty until proven innocent” in bond markets, a reputation that will be hard to reverse without concrete fiscal road‑maps. Internationally, the UK’s elevated borrowing costs affect its standing in global financial governance and could impede its ability to lead on initiatives such as climate finance or digital regulation. Moreover, the emphasis on defence spending—amid ongoing conflicts and a resurgent NATO—means that any fiscal slippage could have security ramifications. Healey’s “lockstep” with Mayor Andy Burnham on fiscal discipline suggests an attempt to project unity across devolved governments, a move that could either stabilise expectations or expose regional fiscal divergences.

## Key facts

- Borrowing costs for UK government debt have hit an 18‑year high, prompting heightened scrutiny of fiscal policy.
- Chancellor John Healey’s first Budget is scheduled for 28 October 2026, with a focus on growth, devolution, and controlled spending.
- A new £150 m fund targeting northern England’s innovative firms is part of a broader regional growth strategy.
- Healey has pledged not to increase taxes on working people, a commitment inherited from the Labour manifesto.
- Shadow Chancellor Andrew Griffith accused Healey of leaving market uncertainty unresolved.

## Implications

- Higher borrowing costs will likely force tighter spending controls, potentially limiting the scale of new public investment projects.
- The regional growth agenda could reshape the UK’s economic geography, but its success hinges on private‑sector participation and effective devolution.
- Market confidence will remain fragile until Healey provides clearer fiscal guidance, especially regarding future tax and spending decisions.
- International partners may view the UK’s fiscal volatility as a risk factor for cooperation on global challenges such as climate change and security.

## Outlook

Healey’s ability to balance optimism with fiscal realism will determine whether the UK can restore its credibility in bond markets while delivering on promised growth. If he can deliver a credible roadmap that curbs borrowing costs without sacrificing key policy priorities, the outlook improves for both domestic confidence and international standing. Conversely, continued ambiguity could exacerbate market volatility, constrain financing options, and undermine the Labour government’s broader agenda.

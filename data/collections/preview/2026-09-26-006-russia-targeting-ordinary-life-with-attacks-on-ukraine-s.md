# Russia targeting 'ordinary life' with attacks on Ukraine's data centres, Zelensky says

> [!summary] TL;DR — President Volodymyr Zelensky accused Russia of expanding strikes against Ukraine’s data centres and internet providers, disrupting civilian connectivity, education and work. The assaults have knocked out power for over 100,000 households and prompted firms to move servers abroad, while Moscow claims the facilities host military intelligence.

## Background

The conflict has evolved from conventional battlefield engagements to systematic attacks on civilian digital infrastructure. Data centres, once considered back‑office assets, now sit at the nexus of communications, finance, health services and emerging AI workloads. Russia’s recent drone and missile strikes on facilities such as Datagroup, New‑Telco and Kyivstar illustrate a strategic shift aimed at degrading Ukraine’s ability to function as a modern state. Simultaneously, the European Union has pledged €6.6 bn in aid, and the United States continues to debate how to protect critical cyber assets under existing international law.

## Escalation of Cyber‑Physical Infrastructure Attacks

The latest wave of Russian operations deliberately targets data centres and internet exchange points, marking a clear escalation from purely kinetic strikes. By striking facilities like Datagroup in Kyiv, Moscow aims to cripple the digital services that ordinary Ukrainians rely on for banking, telemedicine, remote education and even emergency alerts. The attack on a business centre that housed Datagroup, killing four civilians, underscores the willingness to accept collateral damage to achieve strategic disruption. Moreover, the reported outages affecting roughly 100,000 households in Kyiv and surrounding regions demonstrate how the civilian population bears the brunt of these operations. The Ukrainian government’s admission that “local disruptions” may persist, coupled with warnings that internet service costs could rise, signals a prolonged period of degraded connectivity that could hinder both daily life and broader economic resilience.

## Strategic Implications for Global Communications and AI Infrastructure

Data centres are increasingly the backbone of AI model training, cloud services, and real‑time communications. The targeting of Ukrainian facilities raises concerns that similar vulnerabilities exist elsewhere, especially in regions where geopolitical tensions intersect with burgeoning tech ecosystems. For instance, the presence of multinational firms moving data to foreign servers highlights a growing trend of “digital diversification” as a risk‑mitigation strategy. In the broader context, the attacks could accelerate the fragmentation of the global internet, prompting nations to invest in sovereign cloud capabilities and resilient, geographically dispersed architectures. The Ukrainian admission that its internet network remains highly decentralised offers a partial reassurance, but the cost of redundancy and backup infrastructure will likely be borne by both public and private sectors, reshaping investment priorities across Europe and beyond.

## International Response and Emerging Normative Frameworks

The European Union’s pledge of €6.6 bn, including €900 m for a Military Assistance Mission and €1 bn for joint procurement of equipment, reflects a coordinated effort to bolster Ukraine’s defensive capabilities and rebuild disrupted digital services. Diplomatically, the EU frames the aid as “good news for Ukraine and bad news for Russia,” signalling a willingness to counter Moscow’s hybrid tactics. Meanwhile, the United States and its allies continue to debate the adequacy of existing cyber‑warfare doctrines, such as the Tallinn Manual, in protecting civilian data infrastructure. Calls for new regulations on the use of cyber tools against non‑military targets are gaining traction, especially as AI‑driven autonomous weapons and data‑center‑based surveillance become more prevalent. The editorial priorities that highlight agentic AI, LLMs, and open‑source regulation suggest that policymakers are already linking the Ukraine situation to broader concerns about AI safety, data governance, and the need for transparent, accountable cyber‑conflict norms.

## Key facts

- Russian drones struck a Kyiv business centre housing Datagroup, killing four civilians and damaging a data centre.
- Over 100,000 households in Kyiv and surrounding areas lost internet connectivity after Wednesday’s strikes.
- Ukraine’s Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha described the attacks as attempts to disrupt “life‑saving information” and civilian communications.
- The EU announced a €6.6 bn package for Ukraine, including dedicated funds for military assistance and joint equipment procurement.
- Moscow claims the targeted data centres were being used by Ukrainian military intelligence.

## Implications

- Potential long‑term degradation of Ukraine’s digital economy and reduced quality of public services.
- Accelerated global push for resilient, sovereign cloud infrastructures and data‑center diversification.
- Increased pressure on international law to define and protect civilian cyber assets, possibly leading to new treaties or conventions.
- Heightened risk of cyber‑physical attacks on critical infrastructure in other conflict zones, prompting defensive investments worldwide.

## Outlook

If the current pattern of targeting data centres persists, Ukraine may become a testing ground for hybrid warfare that blends kinetic strikes with digital disruption. The international community’s response—through financial aid, technical assistance, and normative development—will shape the future of cyber‑conflict governance. Meanwhile, the tech industry is likely to accelerate investments in redundancy, edge computing, and sovereign cloud solutions, redefining how critical digital services are protected in an era where ordinary life is increasingly mediated by data.

# US has floated Ukraine ceding Donbas for NATO membership, Zelensky says

> [!summary] TL;DR — President Zelensky says US envoys suggested that Ukraine could regain NATO membership by withdrawing forces from the occupied Donbas. Washington has not confirmed the proposal, but the idea revives a contentious diplomatic track that could reshape the war’s ending framework.

## Background

The Russian invasion that began in February 2022 left roughly one‑fifth of Ukraine under Russian control, including most of the industrial Donbas region. Kyiv has long pursued NATO membership as a security guarantee, while Moscow has repeatedly warned that any alliance expansion to its border is unacceptable. In recent months, US‑led diplomatic shuttles have tried to break the deadlock, culminating in a meeting in Dublin that produced Zelensky's claim that Washington floated a conditional NATO offer tied to a Donbas concession.

## Strategic calculus behind the US suggestion

American officials are balancing three competing pressures. First, domestic fatigue in the United States over a protracted conflict pushes policymakers to explore any avenue that could produce a ceasefire. Second, NATO allies remain divided: while countries like Germany and the Baltic states favour a swift accession, others such as France and Turkey fear escalation with Russia. Third, the Biden administration seeks to preserve its credibility as a security guarantor for Kyiv while avoiding a direct confrontation with Moscow. By linking NATO entry to a limited territorial compromise, Washington hopes to create a diplomatic lever that satisfies Russian red lines without abandoning Ukraine's long‑term security goals.

## Zelensky’s political maneuvering

Zelensky’s public disclosure serves multiple domestic and international purposes. Domestically, it signals to a war‑wearied electorate that the president is actively pursuing peace options, potentially defusing criticism over stalled negotiations. Internationally, the announcement forces NATO members to confront a scenario they have largely dismissed as unrealistic, compelling them to clarify their positions. By stating he would put any concrete offer to a referendum, Zelensky also frames the decision as a democratic exercise, limiting his personal political risk should the proposal be rejected.

## Russian response and the broader security architecture

The Kremlin has consistently demanded Ukrainian neutrality and territorial concessions as preconditions for any settlement. A US‑backed proposal that pairs NATO membership with a Donbas withdrawal would be interpreted by Moscow as a tacit acknowledgment of its de‑facto control, potentially emboldening further territorial claims, including over Crimea. Moreover, the suggestion dovetails with Russia’s advocacy for a "new European security architecture" that would limit NATO’s eastward expansion, a narrative that could gain traction among non‑aligned states wary of great‑power competition.

## Key facts

- Zelensky says US envoys suggested Ukraine withdraw from Donbas in exchange for NATO membership
- US has not officially confirmed the proposal, but a senior official acknowledged "many ideas" are under discussion
- Negotiations involved Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, with talks taking place in Dublin and Abu Dhabi
- Russia controls most of Donetsk and Luhansk and insists on retaining occupied territories in any peace deal

## Implications

- If accepted, a NATO accession could dramatically shift the strategic balance in Eastern Europe, deterring further Russian aggression but also risking escalation if Moscow perceives the alliance as encroaching
- A Donbas concession may set a precedent for negotiating territorial losses under external security guarantees, potentially influencing other frozen‑conflict zones
- Domestic political fallout in Ukraine could be severe; a perceived surrender of sovereign land might undermine public support for the government and fuel pro‑Russian narratives

## Outlook

In the coming weeks, US diplomats are likely to test the limits of the proposal with both Kyiv and Moscow, while NATO members will convene informal consultations to gauge consensus. A definitive outcome remains uncertain, but the very emergence of the idea signals a shift from a binary "all‑or‑nothing" stance to a more nuanced, albeit risky, diplomatic calculus.

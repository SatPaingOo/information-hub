# US midterm results a 'victory' for Canadians hopeful of tariff relief

> [!summary] TL;DR — Canadian leaders see the Democratic midterm sweep as a political opening for tariff relief, though immediate changes are unlikely. The new congressional balance may improve Ottawa’s leverage as on‑again‑off‑again trade talks resume, but many tariffs remain under executive authority.

## Background

Canada and the United States have been locked in a volatile trade relationship since 2018, with the Trump administration imposing layered tariffs on autos, steel, aluminium, forestry products and other goods. Talks in August 2024 collapsed, prompting a 50 % “dollar‑for‑dollar” retaliation by Ottawa and a series of additional levies, including a total import ban on motorcycles, whey protein and alcohol. The dispute has hurt cross‑border supply chains, raised consumer prices and created diplomatic friction. The November 2024 U.S. midterm elections, however, produced a Democratic wave that many in Canada view as a potential catalyst for renewed negotiations.

## Political Impact of Midterm Results

The Democratic victory in the House and Senate shifts the balance of power in Washington, giving Canadian officials a more cooperative audience for trade negotiations. While the tariffs were largely enacted via executive orders, a Democratic Congress could pressure the White House to revisit unilateral measures. The election also highlighted voter concern over affordability, with surveys indicating roughly 75 % of Americans believe the tariffs have driven up prices, creating a political incentive for policymakers to seek relief.

## Trade Policy Implications

Although the new Congress cannot directly repeal executive orders, it can influence the administration’s agenda through oversight, funding decisions, and legislation supporting tariff reductions. The midterms also reshaped key state races—Maine’s Senator Susan Collins lost after clashing with the White House over tariffs, while Michigan’s auto‑centric electorate elected Democrat Abdul El‑Said, signaling a regional shift that could affect national trade policy. The political momentum may encourage Ottawa to press for a comprehensive deal before the January 2025 inauguration of the next Congress.

## Canadian Strategic Response

Ottawa is positioning itself to capitalize on the changed U.S. political landscape. Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc has signaled that the midterm outcome will not alter Canada’s negotiating stance, but he also notes that the new Congress offers an “interesting discussion” for future talks. Prime Minister Mark Carney has adopted a more optimistic tone, suggesting that the U.S. negotiators are “asking less and offering more.” Meanwhile, provincial leaders such as Ontario Premier Doug Ford have publicly celebrated the result as a “victory for Canada,” underscoring the domestic political pressure to secure tariff relief.

## Key facts

- The Democratic sweep in the 2024 midterms was partly driven by voter frustration over tariff‑induced price hikes.
- Canada currently faces multiple tariff layers on autos, steel, aluminium, forestry, dairy, cosmetics and furniture, plus a 50 % retaliatory tariff on U.S. goods.
- Key U.S. races—Maine’s Senate seat and Michigan’s gubernatorial contest—featured tariff disputes and resulted in Democratic victories.
- Trade talks between Ottawa and Washington collapsed in August 2024; they have resumed informally but remain stalled.
- President Trump has warned of further tariff escalations from 1 January 2025, but his administration’s messaging has softened ahead of the election.

## Implications

- Increased likelihood of executive‑branch negotiations on tariff reductions, though legislative action remains limited.
- Potential for new bipartisan pressure on the Trump administration to address Canadian trade concerns before the end of the year.
- Provincial economies, especially Ontario’s auto sector, stand to benefit from any tariff rollback.
- U.S. exporters in sectors like dairy and cosmetics may see immediate relief if Canada’s retaliatory tariffs are lifted.

## Outlook

The Democratic control of Congress creates a window of opportunity for Ottawa to pursue a comprehensive trade agreement before the end of the Trump term. While immediate tariff relief is unlikely, the political momentum could lead to interim deals on specific sectors such as autos and steel, especially if the White House seeks to defuse voter anger over costs. Canada’s strategy will hinge on leveraging goodwill, maintaining a unified negotiating position, and preparing contingency plans should the administration double down on protectionist measures after January 2025.

# Germany's far-right AfD says 'democracy demands' parties work with them after state election win

> [!summary] TL;DR — The Alternative for Germany (AfD) secured 43.8% of the vote in Saxony‑Anhalt, falling three seats short of an outright majority and now urging other parties to negotiate a governing coalition, a move that challenges Germany’s post‑war consensus against far‑right participation in government.

## Background

Saxony‑Anhalt, a sparsely populated eastern German state with 2.1 million residents, has historically been a stronghold for the centre‑right CDU. In the 2026 state election the far‑right Alternative for Germany (AfD) surged to 43.8% of the vote, translating into 39 seats in the 83‑seat Landtag. The result marks the first time since World War II that a party classified by domestic intelligence as “right‑wing extremist” has come close to governing a German state. The CDU, SPD and Greens have traditionally ruled out any cooperation with the AfD, citing constitutional safeguards and the party’s extremist rhetoric. AfD deputy parliamentary leader Beatrix von Storch and state leader Ulrich Siegmund have framed the outcome as a democratic mandate, calling on rival parties to engage in talks to avoid a political vacuum.

## Electoral Dynamics and AfD’s Position

The AfD’s performance reflects a confluence of economic anxiety, demographic change, and dissatisfaction with the federal coalition’s handling of energy costs and immigration. Voter turnout was modest, but the party’s messaging on “law‑and‑order” and “regional identity” resonated strongly in rural districts. The three‑seat gap to a majority means the AfD must either secure support from individual CDU members or form a minority government with ad‑hoc agreements. This scenario tests the durability of Germany’s “cordon sanitaire” – the informal agreement among mainstream parties to exclude the far‑right from power.

## Coalition Options and Constitutional Constraints

German law permits minority governments, but they require confidence-and‑supply arrangements that can be withdrawn at any time. The CDU’s internal polls show a faction of disgruntled members who feel alienated by the party’s national leadership; von Storch’s outreach to these members could create a splinter bloc willing to back the AfD on specific policy votes. However, the Federal Constitutional Court has warned that any cooperation that legitimises a party deemed extremist could breach the “basic order” (Grundordnung). The AfD’s refusal to renounce extremist symbols further complicates any formal coalition, raising the risk of legal challenges and public protests.

## Broader Implications for German Democracy and EU Stability

If the AfD secures governing authority, even in a limited capacity, it could set a precedent for similar breakthroughs in other eastern states, eroding the post‑war consensus that has kept far‑right forces marginalised. Policy areas such as policing, education and integration would be directly affected, potentially shifting state‑level enforcement toward stricter immigration controls and a more nationalist curriculum. At the EU level, a German state governed by the AfD could embolden Eurosceptic parties in Hungary, Poland and Italy, complicating consensus on sanctions against Russia and the EU’s climate agenda. The episode also raises questions about the effectiveness of Germany’s domestic intelligence classification system and its ability to balance security with democratic participation.

## Key facts

- AfD won 43.8% of the vote in Saxony‑Anhalt, translating to 39 seats, three short of a majority.
- CDU secured 17.2% of the vote, its lowest share in the state’s post‑war history.
- AfD is classified by domestic intelligence as a right‑wing extremist organization.
- Party leaders Beatrix von Storch and Ulrich Siegmund are urging other parties to negotiate coalition talks.
- German constitutional scholars warn that formal cooperation with the AfD could breach the Grundordnung.

## Implications

- Potential erosion of the long‑standing ‘cordon sanitaire’ against far‑right parties in German politics.
- Increased political instability in Saxony‑Anhalt could trigger a snap election if coalition talks fail.
- Shift in state policy priorities toward stricter law‑enforcement and education reforms aligned with AfD rhetoric.
- Risk of heightened social tensions and protests, especially in urban centres opposed to far‑right governance.
- Possible ripple effects on EU cohesion, as member states monitor Germany’s handling of extremist participation.

## Outlook

Negotiations are expected to continue over the next few weeks. While the CDU’s national leadership has publicly ruled out any formal partnership, internal dissent may produce a narrow support base for the AfD, allowing a minority government to function on a case‑by‑case basis. If talks collapse, a repeat election is likely, but the AfD’s strong showing will force mainstream parties to address the underlying voter grievances that fueled its surge.

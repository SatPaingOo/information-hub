# Iranian attack on Saudi tanker killed two Filipino sailors, Saudi Arabia says

> [!summary] TL;DR — An Iranian projectile strike on the Saudi‑owned supertanker Sidr in the Strait of Hormuz killed two Filipino crew members, prompting Riyadh to condemn Tehran and warn of escalating maritime insecurity. The incident follows a broader surge in US‑Iran hostilities and highlights the vulnerability of global oil shipments through the strategically vital waterway.

## Background

The Strait of Hormuz, a chokepoint for roughly one‑fifth of the world’s oil and gas, has been a flashpoint since the US‑Israel war with Iran began in February 2024. Iranian missile and drone attacks on commercial vessels, coupled with a US blockade of Iranian ports, have effectively closed the strait for much of the year. A tentative June 2024 agreement to reopen the waterway collapsed within weeks after renewed Iranian strikes. Meanwhile, the United States, under President Donald Trump, has declared the strait “open” and assisted ships via a southern Omani‑water route. The latest attack on the Saudi tanker Sidr, confirmed by the national shipping firm Bahri, marks the deadliest incident in a series of tit‑for‑tat actions that have intensified since July, including US air strikes on Iran and Iranian missile strikes on US bases in neighboring Arab states.

## Geopolitical Escalation and Strategic Calculus

The Sidr attack underscores Iran’s continued use of asymmetric warfare to pressure Saudi Arabia and the United States while asserting control over the Strait of Hormuz. By targeting a Saudi vessel, Tehran signals its willingness to expand the conflict beyond direct US assets, potentially aiming to deter Saudi cooperation with Western energy policies. The timing—coinciding with a broader exchange of fire that left 18 Iranians dead and scores injured—suggests Iran is leveraging the incident to extract concessions amid internal political turbulence, including the recent inauguration of President Masoud Pezeshkian. Moreover, the attack reflects a strategic shift: Iran is focusing on vessels using the southern Omani route, which it views as a challenge to its preferred passage through Iranian‑controlled waters.

## Maritime Security and Commercial Shipping Risks

The loss of two Filipino seafarers on a Saudi‑owned tanker highlights the human cost of heightened maritime insecurity. Maritime intelligence firms Marisks and Kpler reported that two Saudi crude‑oil tankers were struck within minutes of each other, indicating coordinated Iranian operations. The incident also raises questions about the effectiveness of existing naval escorts and the reliability of the southern route, which the US military has promoted as a safer alternative. Shipping insurers may reassess risk premiums for vessels transiting the region, while flag states could face pressure to impose stricter navigation protocols. The attack also demonstrates the vulnerability of crew from developing economies, who are often employed on long‑distance crude carriers, to geopolitical fallout they cannot control.

## Energy Market and Supply‑Chain Implications

Despite the violence, US Energy Secretary Chris Wright reported a record 17 million barrels of oil flowing through the Strait of Hormuz on the day of the attack, suggesting that alternative routing and temporary disruptions have not yet materially reduced global supply. However, the psychological impact on markets can be significant; oil futures spiked briefly before settling as traders weighed the possibility of prolonged closures. Saudi Arabia’s condemnation and its emphasis on respecting international navigation may prompt a coordinated response from other Gulf states, potentially leading to joint naval patrols or increased collaboration with the International Maritime Organization. In the longer term, the incident could accelerate investment in energy diversification and the development of alternative transit corridors, such as the Bab‑el‑Mandeb route, to reduce dependence on the Hormuz chokepoint.

## Key facts

- Two Filipino sailors were killed when the Saudi tanker Sidr was struck by unknown projectiles in the Strait of Hormuz at ~23:40 local time on Monday.
- A second tanker, the South Korean‑owned Senegal Prosperity, was also hit by three projectiles in the same area, according to maritime risk firms.
- Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) claimed the vessels struck mines after being urged by the US military to use an illegal route, a narrative not corroborated by independent sources.
- The United States carried out a series of air strikes on Iran earlier in the week, killing 18 people and injuring 108, which Iran labeled a “war crime”.
- US President Donald Trump continues to assert that the Strait of Hormuz is “open,” while the US military assists ships via the southern Omani‑water route.

## Implications

- Potential escalation of tit‑for‑tat attacks could further destabilize the Gulf and threaten global energy security.
- Shipping companies may increase insurance costs and seek alternative routing, impacting freight rates and supply‑chain economics.
- International naval forces may be compelled to enhance presence or establish joint protection mechanisms for commercial vessels.
- Diplomatic efforts to re‑open the strait face renewed obstacles, casting doubt on the viability of a durable US‑Iran de‑escalation agreement.

## Outlook

The Sidr incident is likely to become a focal point in ongoing US‑Iran negotiations, with Riyadh leveraging the tragedy to demand stronger safeguards for maritime traffic. In the near term, we can expect heightened vigilance from commercial fleets, possible temporary rerouting of oil shipments, and continued volatility in oil markets. Over the medium term, the attack may catalyze broader international initiatives to secure the Strait of Hormuz, including multilateral naval coalitions and the development of redundant energy transit corridors, thereby reshaping the geopolitical calculus of one of the world’s most critical maritime chokepoints.

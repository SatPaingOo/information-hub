# Iran offers US deal to reopen Strait of Hormuz in seven days

> [!summary] TL;DR — Iran’s foreign minister has proposed a seven‑day roadmap to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, conditioned on meeting a June memorandum of understanding, while the US acknowledges talks but President Trump privately rejects the deal. The proposal arrives amid ongoing strikes, Houthi disruptions in the Red Sea, and mounting pressure on Trump ahead of the US Congressional elections.

## Background

The US‑Israel war with Iran, which began with air strikes on 28 February 2024, has seen Tehran retaliate by targeting Gulf bases, US assets, and the Strait of Hormuz, effectively closing this vital chokepoint for global oil and LNG shipments. Approximately 20 % of the world’s oil and liquefied natural gas historically passed through the strait, and its closure has caused wild energy‑price volatility. A June 2024 memorandum of understanding (MOU) between Washington and Tehran outlined a ceasefire, the end of the US naval blockade, sanctions relief, and arrangements for safe commercial passage. The MOU collapsed after both sides resumed strikes. Simultaneously, Iran’s ally, the Houthis, have intensified attacks on Saudi oil infrastructure and Red Sea shipping lanes, further straining global energy markets and prompting Saudi Arabia to request US military support. With US Congressional elections approaching in November, Trump faces domestic pressure to resolve the conflict and stabilize energy prices, while Iran seeks leverage through the strait’s reopening.

## Diplomatic Dynamics and Negotiating Leverage

Araghchi’s proposal frames the strait’s reopening as a conditional step tied to the June MOU, effectively reminding the US of a previously agreed framework that both sides have partially honored. By delivering the plan via Qatar and emphasizing that "the choice now rests with the United States," Iran is attempting to shift diplomatic pressure onto Washington, portraying itself as cooperative while demanding compliance with the MOU’s terms. The US official’s statement that discussions are "constructive" through mediators suggests a back‑channel dialogue, yet Trump’s reported private skepticism and preference for renewed bombing indicate a dual-track approach: public diplomacy for domestic consumption, covert military planning for escalation. This duality complicates any swift resolution and underscores the fragility of mediated negotiations in a high‑stakes geopolitical environment.

## Strategic Energy Implications

Reopening the Strait of Hormuz within seven days would instantly restore a conduit for roughly 20 % of global oil and LNG shipments, potentially easing the price spikes that have rippled through markets since the conflict began. However, the strait’s security remains contested, with Iran threatening to attack vessels it deems unauthorized and the US maintaining a naval presence to enforce its blockade. The recent 10‑day average of about 18 transits per day, according to Kpler, shows that even limited traffic persists, suggesting that a formal reopening could quickly ramp up flow if security guarantees are established. Moreover, the concurrent Houthi campaign in the Red Sea adds another layer of risk to global shipping, meaning that any energy‑security gains from the strait’s reopening could be offset by disruptions elsewhere in the maritime supply chain.

## Military and Economic Leverage Calculations

For Iran, the strait remains a core bargaining chip, offering both economic relief through restored oil exports and political legitimacy by demonstrating its capacity to influence global markets. By allowing UN nuclear inspectors into the country, Tehran signals willingness to de‑escalate nuclear tensions, potentially unlocking sanctions relief that could fund reconstruction and bolster public confidence. Conversely, the US leverages its naval dominance in the strait to maintain pressure, as reflected in the official statement that "we are not in a rush." Trump’s inclination toward a renewed bombing campaign suggests a strategy of coercive diplomacy, aiming to force Iranian concessions through the threat of intensified attacks. Economically, the US benefits from higher oil prices in certain domestic sectors, but the broader impact of prolonged closure—inflation, election‑year voter sentiment, and supply‑chain disruptions—creates a political calculus that may push toward a negotiated settlement.

## Key facts

- Iran’s Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi announced a deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days, contingent on meeting the June MOU conditions.
- The proposal was delivered to the US via Qatar and includes the same 14‑point ceasefire framework signed in June 2024.
- US President Donald Trump has privately rejected the deal, expressing skepticism and indicating a likely renewal of bombing campaigns against Iran.
- The Strait of Hormuz handles about 20 % of global oil and LNG shipments; its closure has caused significant energy‑price volatility.
- Houthis, an Iran‑aligned group, have intensified attacks on Saudi oil facilities and Red Sea shipping, further straining global energy markets.

## Implications

- A successful reopening could stabilize oil markets and ease election‑year pressure on the Trump administration, but security guarantees are essential to prevent immediate re‑closure.
- Continued US military threats may harden Iranian negotiating positions, risking a broader regional conflict that could disrupt additional shipping lanes.
- The involvement of mediators and the potential for UN nuclear inspections could open a broader diplomatic track, but the fragile ceasefire remains vulnerable to further strikes.

## Outlook

The immediate outlook hinges on whether the US and Iran can translate the recent diplomatic overture into concrete security arrangements. If the US adopts a more conciliatory stance and fulfills the MOU’s provisions—ending the naval blockade and providing sanctions relief—the strait could reopen within the proposed week, delivering a rapid boost to global energy markets and providing Trump with a political win ahead of the mid‑term elections. However, Trump’s reported preference for military escalation suggests that the diplomatic window may be short-lived, and any failure to reach a deal could precipitate a new round of strikes, further destabilizing the Middle East and reverberating through world oil prices. Monitoring of Houthi activities in the Red Sea will also be critical, as concurrent disruptions could limit the overall impact of a reopened Hormuz.

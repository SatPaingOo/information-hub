# Myanmar faces historic genocide verdict at UN court over Rohingya massacres

> [!summary] TL;DR — The International Court of Justice (ICJ) is about to deliver a historic verdict in The Gambia’s case against Myanmar, which could be the first time a state is found guilty of genocide under international law. The outcome will have far‑reaching legal, geopolitical and technological implications for how mass atrocities are documented and prevented.

## Background

The seven‑year‑old case was filed by The Gambia in November 2019, backed by the Organisation of Islamic Co‑operation, arguing that every state has a duty to uphold the 1948 Genocide Convention. It accuses Myanmar’s government (not just the military) of systematic genocide against the Rohingya people, whose mass expulsion from Rakhine State in 2017 was marked by deliberate village burnings, mass killings, torture and widespread sexual violence. Myanmar denies the accusations, framing its actions as “clearance operations” against terrorist militants. The ICJ, established in 1945 to settle state disputes, was chosen because the victims had few other avenues for redress. The case gained global attention when Aung San Suu Kyi, then head of Myanmar’s elected government, personally led the defence, later replaced by a military‑appointed team after the 2021 coup.

## Legal Precedent and International Law

If the ICJ rules in favour of The Gambia, it will set an unprecedented legal benchmark: the first state‑level conviction for genocide by an international court. This would revitalise the 1948 Genocide Convention, reinforcing the principle that states bear direct responsibility for preventing and punishing genocide, not just individuals. The decision would also clarify the doctrine of “standing” for third‑party states to bring cases when they are not directly injured, potentially opening new pathways for future human‑rights litigation. Moreover, the judgment could influence the International Criminal Court (ICC) and other tribunals, encouraging more aggressive prosecution of state‑sponsored atrocities.

## Geopolitical Ramifications for Southeast Asia

A adverse verdict would isolate Myanmar further, inviting coordinated diplomatic pressure, possible sanctions, and increased support for Rohingya refugees from neighbouring states and international actors. Regional powers such as China and India, which have historically balanced strategic interests with human‑rights concerns, may be forced to recalibrate their engagement. For ASEAN, the ruling could catalyse a more assertive stance on human‑rights compliance, challenging the bloc’s traditional principle of non‑interference. Conversely, a Myanmar victory would embolden authoritarian regimes, suggesting that international legal mechanisms can be navigated or resisted without severe consequences, potentially emboldening similar tactics elsewhere.

## Technology, AI and Evidence‑Based Accountability

The case showcases how modern technology is reshaping atrocity documentation. Satellite imagery, drone footage, and AI‑driven verification tools have been pivotal in establishing patterns of village burnings and forced displacement. Companies like OpenAI, Gemini, and Anthropic are increasingly involved in providing language‑analysis models that can sift through massive corpora of testimonies and social‑media posts to identify coordinated propaganda and hate speech. The ICJ’s reliance on such digital evidence sets a precedent for the admissibility of AI‑generated analytics in international tribunals, prompting calls for robust regulatory frameworks to ensure transparency, bias mitigation, and chain‑of‑custody standards for AI‑derived evidence.

## Key facts

- The ICJ’s verdict could be the first time a state is found guilty of genocide by an international court.
- The Gambia filed the case in November 2019 with backing from the Organisation of Islamic Co‑operation.
- The alleged genocide targets the Rohingya population of Rakhine State, with documented mass killings, torture and sexual violence in 2017.
- Myanmar’s legal team previously included Aung San Suu Kyi, who later stepped down after the 2021 military coup.
- AI and satellite‑based evidence have become central to building the case, highlighting a shift in how international law gathers proof.

## Implications

- A conviction would strengthen the enforcement of the Genocide Convention and could spur new litigation against other states accused of mass atrocities.
- The ruling will likely trigger heightened diplomatic isolation of Myanmar, potentially leading to expanded sanctions and increased humanitarian aid for Rohingya refugees.

## Outlook

Regardless of the outcome, the proceedings mark a turning point in the intersection of international law, technology, and human‑rights advocacy. Should The Gambia win, the decision will serve as a powerful deterrent, encouraging other states to act as guardians of the Genocide Convention. If Myanmar prevails, the case will underscore the limits of international legal mechanisms when political will is lacking, prompting a re‑examination of how the ICJ can compel compliance. In either scenario, the role of AI‑driven analytics in documenting atrocities will continue to grow, demanding new standards for admissibility, verification, and ethical use in tribunals worldwide.

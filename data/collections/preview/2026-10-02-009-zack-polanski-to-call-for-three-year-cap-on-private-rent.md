# Zack Polanski to call for three‑year cap on private rent rises

> [!summary] TL;DR — Green Party leader Zack Polanski will demand a three‑year emergency brake on private rent increases, framing housing as a generational fairness issue. The proposal follows the Renters’ Rights Act and aims to pressure Labour into adopting stricter rent controls. It also signals a strategic shift as the Greens challenge Labour on key policy fronts.

## Background

The Green Party of England and Wales, under the leadership of Zack Polanski, has seen its membership triple since his election a year ago. Polanski’s upcoming speech at the Brighton conference coincides with a period of heightened political competition: Labour’s new leader Andy Burnham has moved the party closer to the Greens on electoral reform and EU relations, while the Greens seek to position themselves as the primary advocate for housing affordability. The UK’s private rental market has seen a 3.5% rise in average monthly rents to £1,377 in the 12 months to January 2026, outpacing wage growth and inflation. The Renters’ Rights Act, introduced earlier this year, limits annual rent increases to the open‑market rate and extends tenancy security, but critics argue it does not fully address the “fear and uncertainty” that many renters face. Polanski’s call for a three‑year cap—anchored to the lowest of CPI inflation, wage growth or 2%—aims to provide breathing space for tenants and create a pathway to a national Fair Rents Guarantee.

## Political Momentum and Strategic Positioning

Polanski’s speech is timed to capitalize on the Greens’ recent electoral gains and to pressure Labour into adopting policies that were once the Greens’ domain. By framing rent control as a generational fairness issue, he taps into widespread discontent among young adults who are still living with parents or struggling to secure employment. The Greens’ narrative that Labour’s shift on electoral reform and EU engagement is a result of electoral pressure from the Greens is a direct challenge to Labour’s legitimacy and an attempt to erode its policy base.

## Economic Implications of a Rent Cap

A three‑year cap linked to CPI, wage growth or 2% would effectively freeze rents at a level that could be below market rates, potentially reducing landlords’ incentives to invest in maintenance or new developments. While the Institute for Fiscal Studies warns of supply shortages, the policy could also reduce the cost burden on households, freeing up disposable income for consumption and savings. The cap would create a predictable environment for tenants, potentially lowering the volatility that has characterized the private rental market in recent years.

## Legal and Regulatory Challenges

Implementing a multi‑year rent cap would require significant legislative change, potentially conflicting with the Renters’ Rights Act’s provisions and the UK’s broader housing policy framework. The government would need to balance the interests of landlords, who argue that rent controls could lead to a deterioration in housing quality, against the public demand for affordability. The policy would also need to be reconciled with EU competition law, particularly if the UK remains a member or has a close relationship with the EU post‑Brexit.

## Key facts

- Zack Polanski proposes a three‑year emergency brake on private rent increases tied to CPI, wage growth or 2%.
- Average UK private rents rose 3.5% to £1,377 in the 12 months to January 2026.
- The Renters’ Rights Act limits annual rent increases to the open‑market rate and extends tenancy security.

## Implications

- Potential reduction in housing supply if landlords exit the market or defer maintenance.
- Increased consumer spending power for renters, potentially stimulating the broader economy.
- Political realignment as Labour may adopt Green‑led policies to retain voter support.

## Outlook

The Greens’ push for a rent cap is likely to intensify policy debates ahead of the upcoming general election. Labour’s willingness to adopt or reject the proposal will hinge on its electoral calculus and the perceived risk of alienating younger voters. If the government implements a cap, the private rental market could experience a short‑term contraction but may stabilize as new supply is incentivised. The policy’s success will depend on complementary measures such as a Fair Rents Guarantee and investment in social housing to offset potential supply shortages.

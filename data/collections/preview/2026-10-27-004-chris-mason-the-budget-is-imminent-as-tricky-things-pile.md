# Chris Mason: The Budget is imminent as tricky things pile up for Burnham

> [!summary] TL;DR — Prime Minister Andy Burnham’s government is preparing a compact Budget while confronting re‑opened Brexit talks, a freedom‑movement referendum hint, and contentious North Sea oil projects. Chancellor John Healey must juggle fiscal restraint with rising living‑cost pressures and energy security concerns.

## Background

It is now roughly 100 days since Andy Burnham entered Downing Street from Buckingham Palace, marking a distinct shift from Sir Keir Starmer’s tenure. Labour’s poll ratings have improved and MPs feel more buoyant, yet the administration faces a dense calendar of economic and political decisions. The upcoming Budget, scheduled a month earlier than last year’s, is deliberately low‑key to limit speculation, while the government’s stance on Brexit, freedom of movement, and North Sea oil extraction are already generating internal and external friction.

## Fiscal Strategy and Budget Timing

The forthcoming Budget is being framed as a relatively modest affair, both in timing and scale. By holding it a month earlier, the Treasury curtails the window for extensive briefing cycles, leaks, and market speculation. Moreover, the anticipated tax‑rise package is smaller than recent precedents, suggesting a cautious approach to fiscal consolidation after years of pandemic‑related spending. Chancellor John Healey’s team appears to be embracing a “less is more” narrative, aiming to present the document as a stabilising anchor rather than a dramatic policy pivot. This strategy seeks to reassure markets while preserving political capital for more contentious dossiers such as energy reform and EU renegotiation.

## Brexit Re‑Engagement and Freedom of Movement Debate

Burnham has deliberately revived the Brexit conversation, signalling a desire for an “honest discussion” about the UK‑EU relationship. The prime minister’s suggestion that freedom of movement could return only after a referendum has reignited debate, though his office later clarified the aim is a broad‑based dialogue rather than a predetermined outcome. This approach reflects a tension within Labour: the party’s centre leans toward closer EU ties, yet several northern and Midlands constituencies that voted Leave are wary of any overtures perceived as pro‑Brussels. Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood has attempted to balance this by noting that calling for honest debate is not the same as settled policy, underscoring the delicate political tightrope Burnham must walk.

## North Sea Oil: Jackdaw Approval and Rosebank Controversy

Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh’s recent approval of drilling at the Jackdaw gas field underscores a push for greater UK energy security amid ongoing conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East. The project is pitched as a short‑term boost to British households and businesses, with revenues earmarked for an “energy wealth fund” to finance renewable and low‑carbon initiatives. By contrast, the Rosebank oil field—located north‑west of Shetland—remains a flashpoint. While some Labour figures inside and outside government see it as a strategic asset, many within the party and the broader public view it as a harder sell, fearing environmental backlash and questioning its alignment with climate commitments. The chancellor’s allies have signalled his support for Rosebank, highlighting an emerging intra‑party divide on how aggressively to pursue fossil‑fuel expansion.

## Key facts

- The Budget is scheduled a month earlier than last year’s, limiting briefing time and speculation.
- Chancellor John Healey’s fiscal plan is expected to feature a smaller scale of tax rises compared with recent Budgets.
- Prime Minister Andy Burnham has called for an “honest discussion” on Brexit, including a possible return of freedom of movement after a referendum.
- Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh approved drilling at the Jackdaw gas field, citing immediate energy security benefits.
- The Rosebank oil field remains politically contentious, with internal Labour disagreement over its development.

## Implications

- A low‑key Budget may stabilise markets but could also be criticised for lacking bold action on inequality or climate, potentially affecting voter sentiment ahead of the next general election.
- Re‑opening Brexit talks risks alienating Leave‑supporting Labour MPs and voters, while a perceived drift toward the EU could erode the party’s electoral base in the north and Midlands.
- Approval of Jackdaw reinforces the government’s energy‑security narrative, yet the Rosebank debate highlights a growing rift between pragmatic resource exploitation and the party’s green commitments, influencing future policy coherence.

## Outlook

Burnham’s government will likely use the upcoming UK‑EU Summit in Brussels to signal willingness for calibrated adjustments to the current Brexit arrangement, while preparing a detailed election manifesto that balances fiscal prudence, energy security, and climate pledges. The handling of Rosebank and any movement‑related referendum will be decisive indicators of the administration’s ability to manage internal party factions and external public expectations.

# Tories would prevent long-term jobless spending benefits on alcohol and cigarettes

> [!summary] TL;DR — The Conservative Party has unveiled a radical welfare reform that would cut Universal Credit by 30 % for long‑term unemployed claimants and restrict spending via a “Back to Work” card, sparking fierce opposition from Labour and anti‑poverty groups. The plan aims to save roughly £538 million annually but critics argue it is unworkable and will deepen hardship for vulnerable families.

## Background

UK welfare expenditure has surged in recent years, projected to reach £408.6 billion by 2030/31. Previous reforms have struggled to balance fiscal prudence with the need to protect the most disadvantaged. The Conservatives, now led by Kemi Badenoch, argue that the current system creates perverse incentives, allowing some able‑bodied individuals to earn more from benefits than from low‑paid work. Labour, still reeling from its 2024 defeat, has pledged to defend the safety‑net function of welfare, while think‑tanks and charities warn that existing Universal Credit levels already fall short of covering basic needs such as food, heating and rent.

## Policy Design and Implementation

The Tory proposal targets claimants who have been out of work for more than six months. After an initial six‑month period on full Universal Credit, those deemed capable of work but lacking sufficient contribution history would be moved to a reduced rate—30 % lower—delivered through a pre‑paid “Back to Work” card. This card would block cash withdrawals and purchases of alcohol, cigarettes, gambling and other non‑essential items. A parallel “Subsistence Allowance” (70 % of the full rate) would be loaded onto a separate card for those with insufficient work history, while individuals with a strong employment record would retain full benefits. The system also includes a “buy‑in” mechanism: every two years of prior employment grants an extra year of full‑rate credit. Exemptions cover the sick, disabled, and those already in work. The design reflects a shift toward conditional welfare, using technology‑based cards to enforce spending restrictions and encourage re‑entry into the labour market.

## Political Reaction and Debate

Labour immediately labelled the scheme “unworkable,” arguing that the Conservatives had 14 years to reform welfare without delivering meaningful change. A Labour spokesperson warned that the new card system would not address the root cause of long‑term dependency but would instead stigmatise the poorest. The opposition also highlighted the party’s past failures, suggesting the proposal was a political stunt rather than a genuine solution. Meanwhile, the anti‑poverty charity Trussell described the plan as “deeply misguided,” pointing to existing food‑bank reliance and the already‑insufficient nature of Universal Credit. Reform UK’s economy spokesman, Robert Jenrick, accused the Tories of leaving record numbers on long‑term benefits and claimed the proposal would “not even put a dent” in the welfare bill. The debate has intensified ahead of the upcoming general election, with welfare policy emerging as a key electoral battleground.

## Economic and Social Implications

The Tories estimate that moving roughly 350,000 claimants onto the lower rate would generate savings of about £538 million per year. Proponents argue that these funds could be redirected toward job‑training programmes and incentives for employers hiring disadvantaged workers. However, independent analysts caution that the savings may be offset by increased administrative costs of issuing and monitoring the cards, as well as potential rises in poverty‑related health costs. Socially, the restriction on spending could force claimants into difficult choices, potentially increasing demand for food banks and emergency housing support. Critics also warn that the policy may exacerbate mental‑health issues, as the loss of discretionary spending can erode dignity and hope. Moreover, the emphasis on conditional benefits may deepen societal divisions, framing poverty as a moral failing rather than an economic structural issue.

## Key facts

- The Conservative plan would cut Universal Credit by 30 % for claimants unemployed longer than six months who have not contributed enough.
- A “Back to Work” card would restrict cash withdrawals and purchases of alcohol, cigarettes, and gambling.
- The Tories estimate the changes would affect about 350,000 claimants, saving roughly £538 million annually.
- Labour and anti‑poverty groups have condemned the proposal as unworkable and harmful to vulnerable families.
- The policy exempts the sick, disabled, and those already in employment.

## Implications

- Potential increase in reliance on food banks and emergency services as disposable income shrinks.
- Higher administrative burden and cost for issuing and monitoring the new benefit cards.
- Political polarisation over welfare, likely influencing voter sentiment in the next election.
- Possible impact on labour‑market participation—if designed well, could incentivise job searches; if poorly implemented, could discourage seeking work due to reduced benefits.

## Outlook

If enacted, the Tory welfare reforms would mark a significant shift toward conditional, technology‑driven benefits in the UK. The success of the “Back to Work” card will depend on robust IT infrastructure, clear eligibility criteria, and effective integration with existing employment services. Opposition resistance and public protest could stall or dilute the proposals, forcing the government to compromise. Ultimately, the outcome will shape the balance between fiscal responsibility and the social safety‑net ethos that underpins British welfare policy for years to come.

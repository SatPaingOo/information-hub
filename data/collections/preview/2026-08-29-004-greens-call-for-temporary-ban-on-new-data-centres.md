# Greens call for temporary ban on new data centres

> [!summary] TL;DR — Green Party leader Zack Polanski has called for a freeze on new data centre approvals in England, citing excessive water and energy consumption amid a nationwide drought. The proposal seeks stricter environmental rules and community oversight, while the government warns a pause could jeopardize investment and national security. The debate highlights the growing tension between AI infrastructure expansion and sustainable resource management.

## Background

Data centres, now classified as critical infrastructure in the UK, are essential for cloud services, AI processing, and digital communications. However, their cooling requirements demand substantial water volumes, especially in hotter regions. Although exact consumption figures are lacking, estimates suggest England’s data centres use around 1.9 billion litres of potable water annually—about 0.2 % of non‑household demand—with a clear upward trend. The UK currently lacks mandatory reporting of water usage, and the government’s “Delivering AI Growth Zones” strategy has not been accompanied by a formal environmental impact assessment. The Greens argue that the critical‑infrastructure designation could give data centres priority over households during shortages, a claim the government denies. Stakeholders from industry, advocacy groups, and local communities are increasingly vocal about balancing technological advancement with environmental stewardship.

## Environmental Impact of Data Centre Water Use

The water consumption of data centres is driven by the need to cool high‑density computing equipment, a process that becomes more intensive in hotter climates. While the UK does not maintain official figures, the Water Research Centre consultancy estimates that English data centres currently consume roughly 1.9 billion litres of drinkable water per year, representing about 0.2 % of the non‑household market. This usage is projected to rise as AI workloads expand, with some large facilities reportedly using up to 1.5 million litres per day. The concentration of data centres in drought‑affected regions such as London and the south of England intensifies local water stress, raising concerns that the critical‑infrastructure designation could inadvertently grant data centres preferential access to water supplies during shortages. The lack of mandatory reporting hampers transparent assessment and limits the ability of regulators and communities to mitigate these impacts.

## Policy and Regulatory Landscape

The UK government’s classification of data centres as critical infrastructure in 2024 provides them with additional support during cyber‑attacks and extreme weather events, but also fuels debate over resource allocation. The Greens are pushing for a moratorium on new approvals, stricter environmental standards, and community involvement in planning decisions. They also demand that operators disclose annual water and electricity consumption figures and locate new facilities on brownfield sites near renewable‑energy sources. In contrast, the government argues that a pause would drive investment and high‑skill jobs overseas, leaving Britain dependent on foreign processing for sensitive health and defence data. Officials stress that developers must use clean power and manage water responsibly, but they maintain that data centres will not automatically receive priority during shortages. The absence of a formal environmental assessment for the “Delivering AI Growth Zones” strategy further complicates the regulatory picture, leaving a gap between policy intent and implementation.

## Economic and Security Implications

Data centres underpin critical services ranging from social media and banking to national defence and AI research, making them a linchpin of the modern economy. Industry representatives, such as Mark Acton of the Data Centre Alliance, argue that the sector does not divert water from communities and uses less water than leisure facilities like golf courses. They also highlight the difficulty of obtaining planning permission, even in designated AI growth zones, underscoring the regulatory burden faced by developers. From a security perspective, the government warns that halting new construction could erode the UK’s capacity to process sensitive data domestically, potentially exposing critical information to foreign servers. Conversely, environmental campaigners contend that unchecked expansion threatens long‑term sustainability and could exacerbate climate‑related risks. The balancing act between fostering technological leadership, safeguarding jobs, and protecting scarce water resources will likely shape future legislative debates and influence the UK’s standing in the global AI race.

## Key facts

- Zack Polanski (Green Party) called for a freeze on new data centre approvals in England due to water and energy concerns.
- Data centres are classified as critical infrastructure (2024), granting them extra government support during incidents.
- Estimated water use: ~1.9 billion litres per year in England (≈0.2 % of non‑household demand) with a clear upward trend.
- Some large centres may consume up to 1.5 million litres per day, according to Global Action Plan research.
- The government warns a pause would risk investment, jobs, and national security, leaving the UK dependent on overseas computing.
- Current UK policy does not require data centres to report water usage; the “Delivering AI Growth Zones” strategy lacks an environmental assessment.

## Implications

- Potential water stress in drought‑affected regions if data centre expansion continues unchecked.
- Increased regulatory scrutiny and possible new reporting requirements for water and electricity consumption.
- Shift in investment patterns if developers relocate to jurisdictions with more permissive water policies.
- Debated priority access to water during shortages could affect household supply and public trust.

## Outlook

The debate is likely to intensify as AI demand drives further data centre construction. Policymakers will need to reconcile environmental sustainability with economic and security imperatives, potentially leading to a hybrid approach: targeted moratoriums, stricter environmental safeguards, and incentives for renewable‑powered, water‑efficient designs. The outcome will influence the UK’s ability to maintain a leading role in AI while meeting its climate commitments.

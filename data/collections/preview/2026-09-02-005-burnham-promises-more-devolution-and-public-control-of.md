# Burnham promises more devolution and public control of essential services

> [!summary] TL;DR — Prime Minister Andy Burnham delivered his maiden Commons speech, pledging stronger public control over essential services such as water and energy, and expanding devolution powers across England and to the devolved nations. The announcement provoked an immediate rebuke from Tory leader Kemi Badenoch, who warned of increased government control and taxes, setting up a stark political contest ahead of the next election.

## Background

Andy Burnham’s ascent to the premiership follows the abrupt resignation of Keir Starmer and a summer of political realignment. Since the 1980s, successive UK governments have privatised economic assets, most visibly the water industry, while political power has become increasingly centralised. Recent experiments in regional governance—most notably the mayoral model in Greater Manchester—have shown mixed results, prompting calls for broader devolution. Burnham, a former Greater Manchester mayor, entered Parliament after a ten‑year absence with a “clear diagnosis” of the country’s decline, citing deindustrialisation, austerity, and Brexit as compounding factors. His early priorities focus on reversing privatisation of utilities, expanding devolved powers to all English regions, and exploring local devolution models in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

## Devolution Blueprint: From Greater Manchester to Nationwide

Burnham’s speech frames devolution as the cornerstone of his growth strategy, echoing the “clear theory of growth” he claims to have honed as Greater Manchester mayor. By extending the same powers‑and‑responsibilities model to every region outside London, he aims to create “good growth in every postcode.” However, the success of this approach hinges on the willingness of existing mayoral authorities, the capacity of local councils, and the political feasibility of a two‑tier system where areas without mayors may lag. The government’s offer to Cornwall “with or without an elected mayor” signals a pragmatic but potentially inconsistent rollout that could deepen regional disparities if not carefully managed.

## Public Control of Essentials: Water and Energy

The prime minister’s pledge to bring water and energy under “stronger public control” directly challenges the legacy of privatisation that has defined the UK utilities sector for four decades. Burnham acknowledges the “very regrettable situation” left by past reforms, noting that reversing privatisation will be “very difficult.” His commitment to release a detailed plan later in the year suggests a phased approach—potentially involving re‑regulation, partial renationalisation, or new public‑private partnership models. The practical hurdles include massive capital investment needs, legal frameworks for asset transfer, and the risk of alienating investors who have long dominated the sector.

## Political Opposition and Electoral Implications

Tory leader Kemi Badenoch’s sharp rebuke frames Burnham’s agenda as “more government control, more taxes and more politicians,” tapping into a classic conservative narrative that warns against state overreach. Her emphasis on the “wrong turns” in Labour’s economic theory underscores a broader Tory strategy to paint the new government as fiscally reckless. The opposition’s focus on defence spending—highlighted by the appointment of John Healey as chancellor—and the contentious debate over new North Sea drilling licences (Rosebank and Jackdaw) will likely dominate parliamentary scrutiny. Burnham's “pragmatic approach” to energy security may need to balance environmental concerns, as raised by the Green Party, against energy independence demands from Reform UK.

## Key facts

- Burnham’s maiden speech lasted three and a half hours, the longest Commons address by a new prime minister in recent memory.
- He announced a plan to deliver “stronger public control” of water and energy, with a detailed roadmap expected later in the year.
- Devolution powers will be offered to all English regions without existing devolved authority, including Cornwall, which has resisted a mayoral model.
- Burnham expressed interest in exploring local devolution models in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
- He appointed John Healey as chancellor, citing Healey’s “commitment to defence” after Healey’s resignation as defence secretary.
- Legislation to enable a shift from leasehold to commonhold ownership was pledged as part of broader housing reform.
- The government’s stance on new North Sea drilling licences remains undecided, with Burnam promising a “pragmatic approach”.
- Kemi Badenoch accused Burnham of offering “more government control, more taxes and more politicians” and warned of the challenges of reversing privatisation.

## Implications

- Accelerated devolution could rebalance economic power away from London, but risks creating a fragmented policy landscape if implementation is uneven.
- Reversing water privatisation may require substantial public investment, potentially increasing the fiscal burden on an already strained treasury.
- The focus on public control of energy could reshape the UK’s energy security strategy, influencing both domestic consumption and geopolitical leverage.
- Burnham’s housing reforms, especially the move to commonhold, may empower leaseholders but could face opposition from existing property developers.
- The political battle with the Tories over the scale of government may shape the narrative for the next general election, with fiscal responsibility a key voter concern.

## Outlook

Parliament will likely subject Burnham’s ambitious agenda to intense scrutiny, with cross‑party debates on devolution, utility regulation, and defence spending shaping the legislative calendar. Public opinion will be pivotal; while there is appetite for greater local control, concerns about cost and efficiency could temper enthusiasm. The government’s ability to deliver on its promises—particularly the water and energy plan—will determine its political capital as it moves from a summer of bold rhetoric into the more demanding reality of governance.

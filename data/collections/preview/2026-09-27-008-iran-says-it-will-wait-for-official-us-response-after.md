# Iran says it will wait for official US response after Trump rejects Strait of Hormuz proposal

> [!summary] TL;DR — Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghzi says Tehran will await a formal US reply after President Donald Trump dismissed Tehran's seven‑day proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Oman continues to mediate while US officials label the offer as insufficient and maintain a sanctions‑driven “economic outcast” strategy.

## Background

The Strait of Hormuz, a narrow chokepoint linking the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman, carries roughly 20% of global oil and gas shipments. Since February 2024, Iranian missile and drone attacks on commercial vessels have effectively closed the waterway, prompting a sharp rise in oil prices and heightened naval activity by the United States, the United Kingdom and allied navies. In June 2024, the United States and Iran signed a memorandum of understanding (MoU) that outlined conditions for de‑escalation, including the cessation of attacks on shipping in exchange for limited sanctions relief. In early September, Tehran offered to reopen the strait within seven days if the United States accepted the MoU terms. President Donald Trump publicly rejected the proposal, calling it “not acceptable” and asserting US “total control” of the waterway. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghzi responded on Telegram, noting contradictions in Trump’s statements and pledging to wait for a definitive response from US negotiators. Oman, a traditional Gulf mediator, has called for continued dialogue and offered to convey messages between the parties. The episode occurs against a backdrop of a broader US‑Iran confrontation, where Washington has launched “Operation Economic Outcast,” a coordinated sanctions campaign targeting Iranian finance, aviation and trade, while Tehran seeks to leverage its strategic position to extract concessions.

## Diplomatic Dynamics and Mediation

Araghzi’s telegram underscores Tehran’s reliance on multilateral legitimacy. By invoking the UN General Assembly and referencing the June MoU, Iran frames its offer as consistent with previously negotiated parameters, seeking to portray the US rejection as a breach of diplomatic good faith. Trump’s blunt dismissal, reinforced by NATO ambassador Matthew Whitaker, signals a hard‑line posture that prioritises domestic political messaging over incremental de‑escalation. Oman’s involvement is critical; the Sultanate has historically acted as a neutral conduit, and its call for “open dialogue” reflects both regional security concerns and economic interests tied to uninterrupted oil flows. The divergent narratives—US emphasis on “total control” versus Iran’s appeal to “necessary conditions”—create a diplomatic impasse that could widen if third‑party mediators are sidelined.

## Economic Sanctions and Energy Market Repercussions

The US Treasury’s “Operation Economic Outcast” intensifies pressure on Tehran by targeting airlines, banks and trade routes in Turkey, the UAE and beyond. While sanctions aim to curtail Iran’s capacity to fund maritime attacks, their efficacy hinges on the compliance of major oil purchasers, notably China. Beijing has repeatedly ignored US secondary sanctions, continuing to import Iranian crude, which blunts Washington’s leverage. Simultaneously, the closure of the strait has already pushed Brent crude above $95 per barrel, prompting a scramble for alternative routes through the Cape of Good Hope. If the strait remains blocked, global energy markets could see sustained price volatility, incentivising non‑OPEC producers and accelerating the shift toward renewable investments—a strategic win for climate policy but a short‑term pain point for oil‑dependent economies.

## Strategic Maritime Security and Regional Power Projection

Control of the Strait of Hormuz is a cornerstone of Gulf security architecture. US naval deployments, including carrier strike groups and the presence of CENTCOM assets, are intended to deter Iranian aggression and reassure commercial shipping. Iran’s capability to disrupt traffic through missile, drone and asymmetric naval tactics demonstrates a potent anti‑access/area‑denial (A2/AD) posture that challenges conventional naval superiority. The US rejection of Tehran’s proposal reinforces a deterrence‑by‑strength doctrine, yet it also risks escalation if Iran interprets the stance as a provocation. Oman’s mediation offers a potential de‑confliction channel, but any misstep could trigger a broader confrontation involving regional actors such as Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, each of whom have vested interests in maintaining open sea lanes for oil exports.

## Key facts

- The Strait of Hormuz transports roughly one‑fifth of global oil and gas shipments.
- Iran’s seven‑day reopening proposal was rejected by President Donald Trump on 5 September 2024.
- Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghzi called for an official US response via mediators.
- Oman is acting as a neutral mediator between the United States and Iran.
- US officials label Iran’s offer as “half measures” and “illusory.”
- Operation Economic Outcast targets Iranian finance, aviation and trade sectors.
- China continues to import Iranian oil despite US secondary sanctions.
- Oil prices rose above $95 per barrel following the strait’s closure.

## Implications

- A prolonged closure could push global oil prices higher, straining economies already coping with post‑pandemic inflation.
- US‑Iran diplomatic fatigue may embolden other regional actors to test the limits of maritime freedom, increasing the risk of accidental clashes.
- Sanctions fatigue could erode multinational compliance, especially if major economies like China maintain trade ties with Tehran.

## Outlook

In the medium term, the standoff is likely to persist unless a credible third‑party mediation framework—potentially led by Oman or the United Nations—produces a mutually acceptable timeline for reopening the strait. US domestic politics and the Trump administration’s hard‑line rhetoric reduce the probability of a rapid concession, while Iran’s strategic calculus will weigh the economic toll of sanctions against the geopolitical leverage gained by maintaining pressure on a vital energy corridor. Energy markets will remain volatile, and naval posturing in the Gulf will stay elevated, making the Strait of Hormuz a flashpoint for broader US‑Iran tensions throughout the remainder of 2026.

# Social care reform could mean big risks and big rewards for Burnham

> [!summary] TL;DR — Andy Burnham has unveiled a bold plan for a free, universally funded national care system, but the proposal faces a staggering £18 bn price tag and fierce political opposition over how to pay for it. The stakes are high: success could redefine UK social provision, while missteps risk electoral backlash and deepen fiscal strain.

## Background

For decades the English social‑care system has been a political lightning‑rod, with past leaders failing to deliver lasting reform. Gordon Brown’s tenure as Chancellor saw a proposed compulsory levy on estates – derided as a “death tax” – while Theresa May’s 2017 manifesto introduced the so‑called dementia tax, both initiatives collapsing under public outcry. Andy Burnham, once Greater Manchester mayor and now Labour’s shadow health secretary, has made fixing broken care a personal crusade, pledging in his 2024 leadership bid to pour “whatever political capital I have into fixing broken social care”. The first concrete outline now emerges as a NHS‑style, free‑at‑point‑of‑use system. However, the government’s financing options remain opaque, the Labour manifesto currently forbids raising income tax, VAT or National Insurance, and an ongoing review led by Baroness Casey is expected to shape the final blueprint. The political calculus is equally fraught: the Conservatives and Reform UK have already labelled any new levy a revived “death tax”, while internal Labour dissent – most notably from former chief secretary Darren Jones – warns against borrowing and spending without offsetting cuts.

## Funding pathways and fiscal trade‑offs

Burnham’s blueprint hinges on a £18 bn annual price tag, a figure he has publicly contested but which the Health Foundation estimates for a fully free system. To bridge the gap, the government is reportedly considering a return to the inheritance‑levy idea that once earned Gordon Brown the “death tax” nickname, as well as broader tax reforms that skirt the manifesto’s ban on income‑tax and VAT hikes. Another contentious lever is the triple‑lock pension guarantee, which Labour pledged to preserve in its 2024 manifesto; critics such as Darren Jones argue that prioritising social care may require revisiting this commitment. The Casey review, commissioned in April 2025, is tasked with finding a “sustainable” solution and could recommend tax adjustments or spending cuts elsewhere, providing political cover for the PM. The sheer scale of the upfront cost means any electoral pitch will demand a far more granular financing plan than currently disclosed, raising the risk of a fiscal backlash from markets and voters alike.

## Political landscape and electoral calculus

Historically, British premiers who fail to deliver credible social‑care reforms have suffered rapid political decline. The 2017 dementia‑tax episode contributed to Theresa May’s weakened standing, while Gordon Brown’s levy remains a cautionary tale of mis‑timed fiscal ambition. Burnham’s proposal sits at the intersection of public need and partisan vulnerability. The Conservatives have already weaponised the plan, branding any new levy as a “death tax” to rally fiscal‑conservatives. Within Labour, the absence of a clear funding mechanism has sparked internal debate; some MPs fear having to sell unpopular measures – tax rises, pension adjustments, or cuts to other departments – to constituents in the next general election. The ongoing Casey review is thus both a policy‑development tool and a political shield, allowing Burnham to point to an independent source when difficult decisions are taken. The broader trend of backbench dissent across both major parties suggests a growing reluctance among legislators to endorse bold, potentially costly reforms without a robust public narrative.

## Operational challenges and system design

Designing an NHS‑style social‑care system entails more than financing; it requires integrating fragmented adult social‑care services, expanding a strained workforce, and ensuring seamless data exchange with the NHS. The current system’s hidden costs – such as families losing homes to care fees or patients lingering in A&E because community support is inadequate – underscore the urgency of reform. Burnham’s plan promises to eliminate these inequities, potentially freeing up £5‑7 bn in avoided emergency‑care spending and improving overall health outcomes. However, the transition will demand massive investment in training, infrastructure, and digital platforms (e.g., OpenTelemetry‑based health records). Moreover, the political risk of over‑promising and under‑delivering looms large; any delays or cost overruns could erode public trust not only in social care but in the broader Labour agenda. The Casey review’s emphasis on “sustainable” solutions hints at a phased rollout, balancing ambition with fiscal realism.

## Key facts

- Burnham proposes a free, universally funded national care system, estimated to cost around £18 bn per year.
- The Labour manifesto currently forbids raising income tax, VAT or National Insurance, limiting obvious tax‑raise options.
- Baroness Casey’s review of adult social care, launched in April 2025, is expected to influence funding recommendations.
- Potential financing ideas include reviving the inheritance‑levy (once dubbed a “death tax”) and revisiting the triple‑lock pension guarantee.
- The Conservatives and Reform UK have already criticised the plan as another “death tax”, anticipating fierce electoral battle‑lines.
- Historical precedents (Gordon Brown’s levy, Theresa May’s dementia tax) show that failed care reforms can quickly become political liabilities.

## Implications

- If enacted, the reform could dramatically reduce personal care costs for millions, easing household financial strain and reducing A&E pressure.
- The necessity of finding new revenue streams may force the government to make difficult choices, potentially cutting other public services or altering pension policy, which could reshape the broader fiscal landscape.

## Outlook

Burnham’s social‑care push represents a high‑stakes gamble. Success would deliver a landmark expansion of the welfare state and likely cement Labour’s reputation for bold reform, but only if a credible, politically palatable financing package is delivered well before the next election. Failure to convince both Parliament and the public could stall the agenda for years, handing opponents a potent attack line and reinforcing a cycle of half‑implemented reforms that leave the system fragmented. The Casey review’s findings will be pivotal; they could either provide the technical justification needed to sell tough choices to the electorate or expose the inherent contradictions in Labour’s fiscal promises.

# Funding shortages force World Food Programme to cut aid to Gaza

> [!summary] TL;DR — The World Food Programme (WFP) will reduce its Gaza operations from 1.6 million to 1.1 million beneficiaries because of a severe funding shortfall, cutting daily hot meals and cash transfers just as winter approaches, heightening the risk of famine and political pressure on donors.

## Background

Since the October 2023 war, Gaza's 2.1 million residents have lived under near‑constant displacement, high unemployment and strict Israeli controls on imports. Two‑thirds of the population rely on external food assistance, and the United Nations Relief and Works Agency (UNRWA) and the World Food Programme have been the primary lifelines. In 2026, the WFP’s budget for Gaza fell to less than half of previous years, leaving a $450 million gap for the next six months. The Integrated Phase Classification (IPC) already classifies 1.4 million Gazans as facing crisis‑level food insecurity, and winter weather threatens to push many into famine territory.

## Funding Gap and Operational Impact

The WFP’s latest financial report shows that donor contributions for Gaza have collapsed to roughly 45 % of the 2025 level. The shortfall forces an immediate 30 % reduction in beneficiaries, from 1.6 million to 1.1 million. Community kitchens will see hot‑meal distribution fall from 250,000 to 150,000 meals per day, and families in hard‑to‑reach zones near the Yellow Line will receive food only every other day. Cash‑based transfers, which previously reached half a million people, have already been scaled back, leaving many households without the purchasing power to buy staple items from the now‑stocked but unaffordable markets. The operational contraction also means the WFP can only sustain bakeries that meet one‑third of Gaza’s bread demand and continue limited nutritional programmes for 300,000 women and children.

## Humanitarian Consequences Amidst Winter

Winter in the Mediterranean basin brings lower temperatures, increased energy demand and heightened risk of respiratory illness. With cooking gas classified as a “dual‑use” item by Israel, families resort to burning plastic and cardboard, exposing them to toxic fumes. Reduced meal frequency will exacerbate malnutrition, especially among children under five, who already receive health‑snack packs for only 200,000 of the estimated 600,000 in need. The IPC’s July‑December 2026 forecast warned that without a rapid funding infusion, the proportion of the population experiencing “severe” or “extreme” food insecurity could rise from two‑thirds to over 80 %. The psychological toll on displaced mothers, as captured in testimonies from al‑Mawasi, underscores a looming public‑health crisis.

## Geopolitical and Policy Dimensions

The aid cut occurs against a backdrop of a fragile ceasefire that has failed to halt Israeli airstrikes and settlement‑related violence in the West Bank. Israel’s tight control of crossing points limits the flow of humanitarian goods, while donor fatigue in Europe and North America reflects broader war‑weariness and competing crises, such as the Ukraine conflict and climate‑related emergencies. Politically, the reduction fuels criticism of both the Israeli government for its blockade policies and the international community for perceived inaction. It also pressures the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) to negotiate more flexible access arrangements, a task complicated by security concerns and the risk of aid diversion.

## Technology, AI and Future Aid Delivery

Amid the funding crunch, the WFP is piloting AI‑driven logistics platforms to optimise route planning for food trucks in densely populated, rubble‑strewn districts. These systems, built on open‑source machine‑learning frameworks, promise to reduce fuel consumption and improve delivery reliability, but they also raise questions about data sovereignty and the need for robust regulation. The agency’s partnership with tech firms exploring agentic AI for predictive hunger mapping could help donors target resources more efficiently, yet the lack of transparent governance mechanisms may hinder donor confidence. Integrating such technologies could become a bargaining chip in future funding negotiations, aligning with broader global trends toward AI‑enhanced humanitarian response.

## Key facts

- WFP will cut beneficiaries from 1.6 million to 1.1 million
- Daily hot‑meal distribution drops from 250,000 to 150,000
- Cash transfers to half a million people already reduced
- Flour and ration boxes to fall from 1.1 million to 600,000 per month
- WFP needs $450 million over the next six months to maintain critical operations

## Implications

- Increased risk of famine as winter approaches, potentially triggering a large‑scale humanitarian emergency
- Escalation of political pressure on donor governments, especially in the EU and the United States, to unblock funding or negotiate greater access with Israel
- Potential destabilisation of the Gaza Strip, which could spill over into the West Bank and fuel further regional tension
- Reduced credibility of the UN humanitarian system if aid cuts are perceived as a failure to protect civilian populations
- Accelerated adoption of AI‑based logistics may set new standards for aid delivery, but also create dependency on proprietary tech platforms

## Outlook

If the $450 million gap is not filled by mid‑2026, the WFP will be forced to curtail even its core bakery operations, dramatically shrinking daily caloric provision. A coordinated donor response—potentially leveraging climate‑finance mechanisms or private‑sector philanthropy—could stabilise the situation for the winter months. However, any prolongation of the funding impasse risks a rapid slide from crisis‑level to famine‑level food insecurity, with attendant health, social and security repercussions that could reverberate across the broader Middle East.

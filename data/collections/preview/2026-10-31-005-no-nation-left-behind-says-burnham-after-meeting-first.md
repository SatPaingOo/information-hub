# 'No nation left behind' says Burnham after meeting first ministers in Glasgow

> [!summary] TL;DR — UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham hosted the inaugural National Economic Council for the Nations in Glasgow, committing to deeper devolution for Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland while explicitly ruling out new independence referendums. The summit focused on regional empowerment, reindustrialisation, and youth employment, with first ministers welcoming the dialogue but reiterating calls for greater autonomy.

## Background

Devolution has been a cornerstone of Andy Burnham’s premiership since assuming office in the summer of 2026. His agenda centres on ‘rewiring the state’ by transferring powers to regional mayors and combined authorities across England, aiming to stimulate economic growth through localized decision-making. However, engagement with the devolved governments in Edinburgh, Cardiff, and Belfast has been more complex, particularly given ongoing tensions over constitutional reform and the legacy of Brexit-related governance gaps. The Glasgow summit marks the first formal convening of a National Economic Council for the Nations, designed to align UK-wide economic strategy with devolved priorities.

## Devolution as a Growth Strategy

Burnham framed the Glasgow meeting as part of a broader effort to decentralize economic authority, arguing that growth cannot wait and that all parts of the UK must move in tandem. By proposing deeper devolution of employment support, innovation funding, and reindustrialisation budgets, the prime minister seeks to address long-standing regional disparities without ceding sovereignty over constitutional matters. This approach reflects a pragmatic recognition that economic levers are often more effective than symbolic gestures in maintaining territorial cohesion.

## Constitutional Constraints and Political Calculus

While Burnham expressed openness to further devolution, he firmly rejected any pathway to second independence referendums in Scotland or Wales. This stance underscores the limits of his conciliatory rhetoric and highlights the enduring tension between Westminster’s unionist position and the SNP’s renewed push for self-determination. For John Swinney and Rhun ap Iorwerth, the summit offered a platform to press for fiscal autonomy and borrowing powers, yet their calls for Senedd-led reforms reveal a cautious optimism tempered by institutional realities.

## Youth Employment and Labour Market Reform

A key outcome of the council was the shared concern over youth unemployment across the four nations. Burnham pledged to explore arrangements for devolving employment support for young people, signaling potential shifts in how labour market policies are administered. This initiative aligns with wider efforts to modernize public services and could serve as a testing ground for experimental approaches, including digital platforms and AI-enabled job matching systems, should they gain traction in future policy cycles.

## Key facts

- The National Economic Council for the Nations was held in Glasgow, marking the first such summit under Burnham’s premiership.
- Burnham committed to deeper devolution of powers related to employment support, innovation funding, and reindustrialisation.
- Scottish First Minister John Swinney welcomed the dialogue but reiterated the SNP’s position on independence.
- Welsh First Minister Rhun ap Iorwerth emphasized that further devolution within Wales is a matter for the Senedd.
- Northern Ireland was represented by officials due to ongoing political instability following the Drumcree parade dispute.

## Implications

- Increased devolution of economic functions may enhance policy responsiveness but risks creating uneven standards across the UK.
- Burnham’s refusal to entertain new referendums could harden opposition in Scotland and Wales, potentially fueling separatist sentiment.
- Regional empowerment initiatives may attract investment in underserved areas, though success hinges on adequate fiscal transfers and administrative capacity.

## Outlook

The Glasgow summit sets the stage for sustained intergovernmental negotiations on devolution, though progress will likely be incremental and contingent on domestic political stability. With elections looming in several jurisdictions and public opinion divided on constitutional reform, the UK government faces the challenge of balancing unity with flexibility. Future councils may delve into specific mechanisms for implementing devolved powers, particularly in high-growth sectors such as green technology and digital infrastructure.

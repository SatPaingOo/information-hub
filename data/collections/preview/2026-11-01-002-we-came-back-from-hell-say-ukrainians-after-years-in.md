# 'We came back from hell', say Ukrainians after years in Russia-occupied front-line city

> [!summary] TL;DR — Hundreds of Ukrainian residents, including Olha and Viacheslav, were evacuated from the Russian‑occupied city of Oleshky after years living under siege, crossing minefields and enduring severe shortages. The evacuation was enabled by a US‑brokered humanitarian ceasefire in the Kherson region, allowing roughly 6,000 people—about 200 children—to leave via Belarus to safety in Chernihiv. The return from what survivors described as “hell” highlights the human cost of the conflict and the fragile prospects for reconstruction.

## Background

Oleshky, a strategic river town on the Dnipro, fell to Russian forces within days of the full‑scale invasion in February 2022. Situated between the river and advancing Russian troops, it became a frontline enclave for nearly four years. Residents were cut off from regular supplies, faced constant shelling, drone attacks, and missile strikes, and many resorted to eating garden weeds to survive. Escape routes were heavily mined, and attempts to flee often resulted in death or injury. The humanitarian situation deteriorated further as infrastructure collapsed, leaving schools, hospitals, and homes in ruins. The international community’s attention remained limited, though occasional ceasefires allowed limited aid deliveries. By mid‑October 2026, a negotiated humanitarian pause brokered by U.S. First Lady Melania Trump in the adjacent Kherson oblast created a narrow window for evacuation, offering the first safe passage for thousands trapped in Oleshky.

## Humanitarian Impact and Survival Strategies

The evacuation underscores the extreme lengths civilians went to survive under occupation. Testimonies reveal that families crossed active minefields searching for food, with reported casualties among those attempts. The reliance on garden weeds as sustenance illustrates the collapse of formal supply chains and the erosion of basic nutrition standards. Moreover, the emotional toll is evident in the reunions captured on video: children sprinting to embrace parents after years of separation, and elderly survivors too weak to walk unaided. These scenes highlight not only physical deprivation but also profound psychological trauma, which will require long‑term mental‑health interventions as the displaced population rebuilds.

## Geopolitical Repercussions of the Evacuation

The evacuation is a diplomatic win for the United States and its allies, showcasing the effectiveness of back‑channel humanitarian negotiations amid broader military stalemate. Melania Trump’s involvement signals a soft‑power push by the U.S. to gain goodwill in Eastern Europe, potentially influencing future policy discussions on sanctions and reconstruction aid. For Russia, the loss of a strategically positioned city after four years of occupation reflects the limits of its ability to consolidate control over contested territories. The event also raises questions about the legal responsibilities of occupying powers under international humanitarian law, especially regarding the safe passage of civilians and the removal of landmines, which could become points of contention in upcoming UN resolutions.

## Logistical and Reconstruction Challenges

While the immediate rescue is complete, the evacuees now confront a new set of challenges: housing, employment, and reintegration. Many have no relatives in Ukraine and must rely on temporary accommodation with acquaintances, straining host families’ resources. The labor market is saturated with displaced workers, many of whom possess limited formal qualifications but offer versatile manual skills—construction, farming, and maintenance. Coordinating reconstruction efforts will require substantial international funding, possibly through the World Bank or EU recovery packages, and careful oversight to avoid corruption. Additionally, clearing the extensive minefields surrounding Oleshky is a prerequisite for safe return of those who chose to stay, a process that will demand specialized equipment and international expertise.

## Key facts

- Approximately 6,000 people, including 200 children, were still living in Oleshky and surrounding areas; 1,700 were in the city proper.
- The evacuation window lasted four hours on Friday, using 25 buses for up to 750 evacuees and ambulances for those with limited mobility.
- Evacuees traveled through Russian‑occupied territory to southern Russia, flew to Belarus, then arrived at a military facility in Chernihiv.
- Some elderly residents were so weak they had to be carried on stretchers upon arrival.
- The humanitarian ceasefire was brokered by U.S. First Lady Melania Trump, providing the first days of peace in the region in four years.
- Survivors described life in Oleshky as “horror” and “hell,” citing constant shelling, drone attacks, and missile strikes.

## Implications

- Potential shift in Western diplomatic focus toward increased humanitarian aid and reconstruction funding for eastern Ukraine.
- Russia may face heightened international scrutiny over its conduct in occupied territories, possibly leading to new sanctions.
- The evacuation could inspire further civilian displacement negotiations, setting a precedent for future humanitarian pauses.
- Local economies in host regions (e.g., Chernihiv) will experience pressure from sudden population influxes, requiring rapid integration services.

## Outlook

The evacuees now face an uncertain future as they seek employment, housing, and a return to normalcy. Many, like Olha and Viacheslav, have no extended family networks elsewhere in Ukraine, leaving them dependent on temporary assistance from acquaintances. The Ukrainian government, with support from international partners, will need to prioritize job creation, especially in sectors where displaced workers can contribute quickly—construction, agriculture, and infrastructure repair. Long‑term stability hinges on clearing remaining mines, restoring essential services, and addressing the psychological scars of a generation that endured years of siege. The broader geopolitical landscape will watch whether this humanitarian success can be replicated elsewhere, shaping the trajectory of the Russia‑Ukraine conflict and influencing future diplomatic engagements in the region.

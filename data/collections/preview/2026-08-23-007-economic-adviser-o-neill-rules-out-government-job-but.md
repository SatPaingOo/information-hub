# Economic adviser O'Neill rules out government job but backs PM's devolution plans

> [!summary] TL;DR — Lord Jim O'Neill declined a formal advisory role in Andy Burnham's new government, citing conflicts of interest and personal commitments, while reaffirming his support for the prime minister’s devolution agenda. He warned against new wealth taxes and urged a pragmatic approach to welfare reform as the UK prepares its October budget.

## Background

Lord Jim O'Neill, a cross‑bench peer, former Treasury minister (2015‑16) and ex‑chief economist at Goldman Sachs, has long been a champion of regional economic policy. He helped shape the Conservative‑led "Northern Powerhouse" strategy and coined the "BRIC" acronym in 2001. In recent months he has been advising Prime Minister Rishi Sunak on macro‑economic strategy, while also being floated as the chief economic adviser to Andy Burnham’s newly elected Greater Manchester mayoral administration. O'Neill’s decision to stay out of a formal government post reflects the growing tension between private sector expertise and the strict conflict‑of‑interest rules that bind UK ministers, such as blind‑trust requirements and the expectation of 24/7 availability. His comments come ahead of the October 2026 budget, at a time when the UK faces heightened fiscal pressure from defence spending, welfare commitments, and debates over regional devolution.

## Why O'Neill rejected a formal advisory role

O'Neill’s refusal is rooted in the practical constraints of ministerial office. Blind‑trust rules would force him to relinquish control of his extensive private‑sector holdings, including stakes in venture‑capital funds and advisory firms. Moreover, the 24‑hour availability demanded of ministers would clash with his existing commitments to boardrooms, think‑tanks, and speaking engagements. By remaining an external voice, O'Neill preserves his independence, retains his income streams, and can continue to influence policy without the bureaucratic shackles that often dilute private‑sector insight.

## Devolution as an economic lever

O'Neill praised Burnham’s focus on devolution, pointing to Greater Manchester’s recent GDP growth as evidence that localized decision‑making can unlock productivity. He argues that replicating the Manchester model—greater fiscal autonomy, targeted infrastructure investment, and a skills‑focused education agenda—could raise national output by several percentage points. This aligns with Sunak’s broader "levelling‑up" agenda, which seeks to narrow the north‑south economic divide through city‑region devolution deals, transport upgrades, and devolved tax‑raising powers.

## Fiscal stance: tax aversion and welfare reform

When pressed on the prospect of new wealth taxes, O'Neill warned that higher capital‑gains levies would deter venture‑capital risk‑taking, a key driver of high‑growth sectors such as AI, biotech, and clean‑energy. He instead advocated a "more sensible" approach to welfare, suggesting that reforms should target fraud reduction and better targeting rather than blanket spending cuts. His view signals to the Treasury that any budgetary tightening should focus on efficiency gains rather than new revenue streams that could stifle private investment.

## Geopolitical undercurrents and the UK’s global standing

While the story is domestic, O'Neill’s comments have an external dimension. A stronger, regionally balanced UK economy improves the country’s fiscal resilience amid rising defence outlays tied to NATO commitments and the ongoing Ukraine conflict. By avoiding new wealth taxes, the UK positions itself as an attractive hub for foreign direct investment, competing with EU neighbours that are tightening their own tax regimes. This subtle positioning reinforces the UK’s post‑Brexit narrative of a "global Britain" built on financial services, innovation, and regional dynamism.

## Key facts

- Lord Jim O'Neill declined a formal chief economic adviser role in Andy Burnham's government.
- He cited blind‑trust requirements and 24/7 ministerial duties as primary deterrents.
- O'Neill remains an external adviser to Prime Minister Rishi Sunak on macro‑economic policy.
- He praised Greater Manchester’s growth as a template for nationwide devolution.
- He expressed opposition to new wealth or capital‑gains taxes, favouring welfare efficiency.

## Implications

- The Treasury may rely more on external experts like O'Neill for policy input, blurring the line between public and private advisory channels.
- Devolution momentum could accelerate, prompting more city‑region deals and potentially reshaping the fiscal map of the UK.
- A reluctance to introduce wealth taxes may limit short‑term revenue options for the October budget, increasing pressure on existing tax bases.
- Continued private‑sector influence could raise questions about transparency and democratic accountability in economic policymaking.

## Outlook

In the run‑up to the October 2026 budget, O'Neill’s stance suggests the government will prioritize fiscal prudence over new revenue streams, leaning on efficiency reforms and regional investment to sustain growth. Devolution is likely to become a central narrative, with Greater Manchester serving as a showcase for future city‑region pilots. However, the reliance on high‑profile private advisers may spark debate over the balance between expertise and democratic oversight, a tension that could shape the next round of UK economic reforms.

# Green Party MP wants to make sure it is 'not vulnerable to extremists'

> [!summary] TL;DR — Green Party Westminster leader Ellie Chowns has warned that the party must guard against infiltration by extremists and racists after a controversial motion equating Zionism with racism was approved by a narrow majority. The move has split senior figures, drawn criticism from Jewish groups, and raised questions about internal democratic processes and the party’s broader stance on Palestinian rights.

## Background

The Green Party of England and Wales has long positioned itself as a progressive, anti‑establishment force in British politics. However, recent internal debates have exposed deep fissures over identity politics and foreign policy. At the party’s conference earlier in October 2026, a motion titled ‘Zionism is Racism’ was tabled by a small group of activists. The motion, which defines Zionism as an ethnonationalist ideology supporting a Jewish state, passed with 1,022 votes to 813—a margin representing roughly 0.4 % of the party’s 230,000 members. The decision has sparked a backlash from Jewish community organisations, senior Green figures, and even the party’s current leader Zack Polanski, who signalled he would have voted for a more nuanced amendment. The episode highlights broader tensions within progressive movements about how to balance anti‑racism principles with inclusive dialogue, especially concerning the Israel‑Palestine conflict.

## Internal Democratic Strain

The narrow vote margin underscores a structural vulnerability in the Green Party’s conference‑based decision‑making. With over 230,000 members, only a fraction typically attend the annual gathering, meaning policy outcomes can be disproportionately influenced by a small, highly motivated cohort. This raises concerns about “entryism,” where dedicated activists steer party platforms to reflect their own ideological priorities rather than the broader membership’s consensus. The situation is reminiscent of historical debates within other left‑leaning parties, where fringe motions have forced leadership to confront questions of legitimacy and representativeness.

## Identity, Anti‑Racism, and Jewish Inclusion

The motion’s language has ignited a fierce debate about the boundaries of anti‑racism discourse. While many Greens view Zionism as a colonial‑settler ideology that perpetuates the displacement of Palestinians, Jewish organisations argue that Zionism is a necessary response to historic persecution and a cornerstone of Jewish self‑determination. The Board of Deputies of British Jews and the Jewish Leadership Council have condemned the policy as conflating criticism of Israeli government actions with antisemitism. This clash forces the Green Party to navigate a delicate balance: upholding its anti‑racist commitments without alienating Jewish members and allies, a challenge that reflects wider societal struggles over free speech, identity politics, and the definition of racism in contemporary discourse.

## Strategic Implications for UK Politics

The controversy is likely to reverberate beyond the Green Party’s internal affairs. In a fragmented UK political landscape, the Greens have been courted as potential kingmakers in marginal constituencies. The perception of being “vulnerable to extremists” could erode voter confidence, particularly among centrist and moderate left‑leaning electorates who value inclusivity. Moreover, the dispute may influence coalition dynamics in local government, where Green councillors often partner with Labour or Liberal Democrat groups. The party’s ability to present a unified, principled stance on both Palestinian rights and Jewish inclusion will be a litmus test for its electoral viability in upcoming local and potential general elections.

## Key facts

- The motion equating Zionism with racism passed by 1,022 votes to 813, representing roughly 0.4 % of the Green Party’s 230,000 members.
- Westminster leader Ellie Chowns described the policy as “significant and damaging” and pledged to reform party structures to prevent extremist capture.
- Former leader Caroline Lucas warned of entryism and called for the party to become a more welcoming environment for Jewish people.
- Zack Polanski, the current leader, indicated he would have voted for an amended version and expressed concern over the motion’s framing, though he acknowledged the “substantial part” concerning Palestinian displacement.
- Jewish community groups, including the Board of Deputies of British Jews, have criticised the motion as conflating legitimate criticism of Israeli policy with antisemitism.

## Implications

- Potential erosion of Green Party support among centrist voters uneasy with perceived extremist influence.
- Increased scrutiny of conference‑based policy making and calls for reforms to ensure broader member participation.
- Heightened debate over the boundaries of anti‑racism discourse within progressive movements and the risk of alienating minority communities.
- Possible impact on coalition negotiations at local government level, where Green councillors may face pressure from partners over the party’s stance.

## Outlook

The Green Party is at a crossroads. If it can reconcile its anti‑racist principles with an inclusive approach that respects Jewish identity, it may emerge stronger and more electorally attractive. Conversely, failure to address internal divisions could cement a reputation for being susceptible to extremist capture, limiting its growth and influencing broader UK political alignments. Observers will watch for concrete reforms in the party’s policy‑making processes and any forthcoming statements from leadership that aim to bridge the current rift.

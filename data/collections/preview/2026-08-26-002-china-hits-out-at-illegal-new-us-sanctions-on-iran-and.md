# China hits out at 'illegal' new US sanctions on Iran and trading partners

> [!summary] TL;DR — The United States has announced a sweeping expansion of economic sanctions targeting Iran and its key trading partners, most notably China, prompting Beijing to denounce the measures as illegal and vow to protect its interests. The new sanctions, dubbed 'Operation Economic Outcast', aim to isolate any nation that continues to engage financially with Tehran, while Iran claims it is fully prepared to endure the pressure.

## Background

The latest US move follows nearly six months of intensified conflict between Washington and Tehran, which has seen Tehran block oil exports through the Strait of Hormuz and the US impose a naval blockade that has driven up global oil prices. Previous diplomatic efforts, including a 60‑day ceasefire that expired without a settlement, have failed, leaving sanctions as the primary tool of pressure. China, as the largest buyer of Iranian oil, has historically resisted US‑led unilateral sanctions, arguing that cooperation with Iran remains within the bounds of international law. The announcement also comes ahead of planned talks between President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping, raising the stakes for both economies, especially given Beijing’s dominance over critical minerals such as rare earths.

## Geopolitical Context and US Strategic Calculus

The US Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, framed the new sanctions as "the single greatest financial offensive ever" against Iran, signaling a shift from containment to outright isolation. By labeling the measures "Operation Economic Outcast" and an "economic D‑Day," Washington aims to coerce not only Tehran but also secondary actors—most prominently China—into severing all financial ties. This approach reflects a broader US strategy of using economic statecraft to force regime change, especially after diplomatic channels proved deadlocked. The timing, just weeks before the Trump‑Xi summit, suggests that Washington intends to leverage its punitive toolkit to gain leverage in broader strategic negotiations, including concerns over rare‑earth supply chains and technology transfer.

## China's Diplomatic and Economic Counterplay

Beijing’s response, delivered by Foreign Ministry spokesman Lin Jian, was swift and unequivocal: the sanctions are "illegal unilateral sanctions" and China will take "all necessary measures" to safeguard its rights. This language echoes China’s long‑standing opposition to US‑led unilateral actions, reinforcing its commitment to a rules‑based international order that it interprets as protecting sovereign trade. China’s economic leverage is considerable; it processes the majority of the world’s rare earths and is the primary conduit for Iranian oil exports. By tightening its own export controls on rare earths in previous negotiations, Beijing has already demonstrated its willingness to use critical mineral supplies as a bargaining chip. The forthcoming Trump‑Xi talks will likely see China warning of reciprocal measures, potentially targeting US tech firms or imposing higher tariffs on American goods, thereby raising the risk of a broader trade escalation.

## Market and Sanctions Impact Assessment

Analysts have cast doubt on the immediate efficacy of the expanded sanctions. David Oxley of Capital Economics noted that the direct impact on Iran’s energy revenues would be "somewhat of a damp squib," given that roughly 90 % of Iranian oil already flows to China, a nation that has historically ignored US sanctions. Moreover, Iran’s Economy Minister, Ali Madanizadeh, asserted that Tehran is "fully prepared" and possesses a two‑year plan to manage the pressure, suggesting that the regime is willing to absorb economic pain and shift it onto the population. The broader ramifications include potential volatility in global oil markets, heightened premiums for alternative supplies, and increased scrutiny of financial institutions that handle Iranian transactions. While India and Russia have not yet responded, they could face similar pressure, further fragmenting the existing international financial architecture.

## Key facts

- US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced a new sanctions package targeting Iran and its trading partners, including China.
- China’s Foreign Ministry spokesman Lin Jian condemned the sanctions as illegal and vowed to protect Chinese interests.
- China remains the largest buyer of Iranian oil, accounting for about 90 % of Iran’s oil exports.
- The sanctions are dubbed "Operation Economic Outcast" and described as an "economic D‑Day" by US officials.
- Iran’s Economy Minister Ali Madanizadeh said Tehran has a two‑year plan and is "fully prepared" for the expanded sanctions.
- The US plans to sanction nearly 60 entities, individuals, and vessels linked to Iran’s sanctions‑evasion networks.
- The announcement precedes planned talks between President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping.
- Beijing already tightened export controls on rare earths amid previous trade negotiations with the US.

## Implications

- Potential escalation of US‑China strategic rivalry, with China likely to retaliate using its control over critical minerals.
- Increased pressure on global oil markets and possible price spikes if alternative supplies cannot fill the gap.
-  heightened compliance risks for banks and corporations worldwide that maintain any exposure to Iranian trade.
- Further marginalization of multilateral sanction regimes, as unilateral US actions may prompt other nations to seek alternative financial channels.

## Outlook

The immediate outlook hinges on the dynamics of the upcoming Trump‑Xi summit. If Beijing perceives the sanctions as an existential threat to its energy security, it may introduce counter‑measures that could disrupt US technology supply chains and rare‑earth markets. Conversely, a diplomatic breakthrough could see China agree to limited curbs on Iranian oil purchases in exchange for relief from other US pressures. In any scenario, the sanctions regime is likely to remain a central pillar of US policy toward Tehran, while Iran’s resilience suggests a protracted economic struggle rather than a swift capitulation.

# Emergency measures needed to stop teachers quitting, say Lib Dems

> [!summary] TL;DR — Liberal Democrats warn that nearly half of surveyed teachers plan to leave the profession within five years, urging urgent reforms including full pay funding and limits on overtime. The call follows rising burnout, heavy workloads, and concerns over under‑funding, prompting the party to propose an emergency 'back‑to‑school' package for the new academic year.

## Background

Teacher retention has been a persistent policy challenge in the United Kingdom for over a decade. Successive governments have grappled with rising workloads, chronic under‑funding, and increasing accountability pressures that have contributed to widespread burnout among educators. Recent data from the Department for Education shows that while the rate of teachers leaving the profession hit a low in 2024, the underlying drivers of dissatisfaction remain. The Liberal Democrats' latest survey, conducted with the National Education Union (NEU) and the Association of School and College Leaders (ASCL), captures a stark picture: almost half of respondents are considering exiting teaching within five years, and one in ten plan to leave within a year. This crisis coincides with the upcoming academic year, prompting calls for immediate, targeted interventions to reverse the trend and safeguard the quality of education for millions of pupils.

## Current Teacher Workforce Crisis

The survey of more than 900 teachers, headteachers and SENCOs reveals a profound level of disillusionment. Over three quarters of participants reported feeling "frequently or constantly burnt out," citing excessive marking loads, lesson preparation demands, and administrative tasks that erode classroom time. The data shows a clear correlation between workload intensity and intent to leave: teachers who reported the highest marking volumes were twice as likely to consider resignation within five years. Moreover, the proportion of educators planning to depart within a year (10%) is alarmingly high, suggesting an imminent staffing shortfall that could disrupt school stability and student outcomes. The crisis is not confined to England; similar trends are observed across the devolved nations, reflecting systemic pressures rather than isolated regional issues.

## Policy Proposals and Funding Implications

The Liberal Democrats have outlined a three‑point emergency package. First, they demand that the government fully fund the upcoming 3.5% pay rise for teachers from September, plus an additional 3% the following year, rather than relying on school budgets to cover the increase. Second, they call for strict enforcement of the statutory maximum working hours for teachers and the introduction of reasonable overtime caps to curb excessive workload. Third, they propose that all schools adopt flexible working policies, such as term‑time scheduling options and remote marking facilities, to improve work‑life balance. While the party has not specified exact funding sources, the proposals implicitly suggest reallocating central treasury funds, potentially reprioritising other expenditures or introducing targeted levies. The financial impact could be substantial, with teacher pay accounting for roughly 70% of total school budgets in many secondary schools, meaning full central funding would free up significant resources for other educational improvements.

## Political and Fiscal Feasibility

The Department for Education has responded by highlighting recent improvements, noting the lowest teacher attrition rate since 2010 and a record number of teachers returning to the classroom. However, officials acknowledge that "more to do" is needed, signalling openness to dialogue. Politically, the Lib Dems' stance positions them as advocates for public‑sector workers, potentially gaining traction ahead of local and European elections. The proposals also intersect with broader fiscal debates: the Treasury must balance the need for a robust education workforce against competing priorities such as health, defence, and climate initiatives. Moreover, the involvement of key unions like the NEU adds credibility but also introduces negotiation complexity, as any funding model will require union buy‑in to avoid industrial action. The timing—coinciding with Lucy Powell's first full term as Education Secretary—creates a strategic window for the government to demonstrate leadership on a high‑visibility issue.

## Key facts

- Nearly half of the 900+ teachers surveyed plan to leave the profession within five years.
- One in ten teachers intend to quit within the next year, indicating an immediate staffing risk.
- Over three quarters of respondents reported frequent or constant burnout.
- The Lib Dems propose full central funding for a 3.5% pay rise plus an extra 3% next year.

## Implications

- If unaddressed, the teacher exodus could lead to larger class sizes, reduced extracurricular offerings, and diminished educational standards.
- Full central funding of pay rises would relieve pressure on school budgets, potentially allowing reinvestment in resources and support staff.
- Enforced working hour limits and flexible policies could improve teacher wellbeing and retention, but may require significant administrative restructuring.

## Outlook

The immediate outlook hinges on the government's willingness to adopt the Lib Dem emergency package. A swift, well‑funded response could reverse attrition trends and restore confidence among educators, while delay risks deepening the crisis and inviting further political criticism. Stakeholders—including unions, school leaders, and parents—will be watching for concrete action in the upcoming parliamentary session, with the first budget allocation likely to serve as a litmus test of policy commitment.

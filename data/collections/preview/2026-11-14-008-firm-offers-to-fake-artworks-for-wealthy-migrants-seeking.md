# Firm offers to fake artworks for wealthy migrants seeking UK 'talent' visas - BBC investigation

> [!summary] TL;DR — A BBC undercover investigation found London-based consultancies selling 'fully inclusive' packages of fabricated evidence — including artworks produced in China, staged exhibitions and planted newspaper reviews — to wealthy migrants applying for the UK's Global Talent visa. The Home Office has begun revoking visas obtained by deception and says the route is already under review, exposing a structural integrity problem in a scheme the government otherwise champions.

## Background

The Global Talent visa was introduced in 2020 to attract the 'brightest and best' scientists, researchers, digital technologists and creative artists. Unlike most UK work routes it involves no interview: applicants must secure endorsement from a designated body — Arts Council England in the arts and culture category — by demonstrating they are 'exceptional talent' or 'exceptional promise', after which they can bring dependants and apply for settlement in three or five years. The route has grown quickly: roughly 6,600 visas were granted in 2025, and arts and culture applications nearly doubled between 2023 and 2025, with Arts Council England endorsing just under half of almost 3,500 applications. Originally a Conservative creation, the scheme has been enthusiastically promoted by the current Labour government as a growth and soft-power instrument even as it tightens rules on other migration routes. The BBC's findings follow an earlier investigation into fraudulent asylum claims, and raise questions about how a document-based, endorsement-driven system can be verified at scale.

## A fraud built around a document-only test

The scheme's design is the vulnerability. Because Global Talent applicants are never interviewed, the entire decision rests on a paper — now largely digital — record of achievement: portfolios, exhibition histories, press coverage and letters of recommendation. The BBC's undercover reporter, posing as a Mandarin-speaking prospective client, was told by a consultant from iCareer Consultancy UK in Canary Wharf that the firm could supply artwork produced in China, arrange exhibitions, generate media coverage and procure recommendation letters, all within a package costing more than £100,000. A further £28,000 would buy a Chinese art award sponsored by Forbes. The consultant claimed at least four clients had secured visas this year and around ten more were in process, and that no client on the 'fully inclusive' package had ever been refused. The product being sold is not a visa but a portfolio — a manufactured identity as an artist, assembled to satisfy an endorsing body that has no practical way to audit provenance.

## The integrity gap is institutional, not just individual

What makes the case consequential is the corporate architecture around it. iCareer's sister company, ADArt Global Ltd, is registered with the government regulator to provide immigration advice, and both operate from the same Canary Wharf address. iCareer denied selling visa services and said the consultant — Shilong Guan, who marketed the route on Xiaohongshu as 'Peter' — had left before speaking to the reporter, despite still being listed on the company's website and supplying a contract bearing the firm's name and contact email. Separately, a well-known Nigerian art critic, Molara Wood, found her byline attached to fabricated reviews of two obscure 'fine art photographers' whose visas the Home Office is now revoking. That points to a wider evidence supply chain — ghostwritten reviews implanted on newspaper sites, paid awards, rented gallery appearances — operating across jurisdictions and exploiting the credibility of legitimate cultural institutions. Senior immigration lawyer Harjap Singh Bhangal characterised the arrangement as 'sophisticated fraud' aimed at convincing both the Home Office and an endorser.

## Why the government keeps the door open

The political economy of the route explains the slow response. Global Talent is one of the few immigration channels the Labour government can advertise as unambiguously pro-growth while simultaneously tightening student, family and asylum rules to cut net migration. It is cheap to administer, generates fee revenue, and fits a 'science superpower' and creative-industries narrative. But the scheme also functions, in practice, as a capital-based route: the BBC's evidence suggests wealth, not talent, is the binding constraint for some applicants, and marketing material explicitly pitches it to wealthy Chinese nationals partly because it avoids the 'extremely high English language requirement' of other settlement paths. That creates a two-tier perception problem — a fast track for the rich alongside a punitive system for everyone else — which is politically awkward for a government already accused of inconsistency on migration.

## The next frontier is synthetic evidence

The current fraud relies on human labour: commissioning paintings, paying critics, staging shows. That cost floor is collapsing. Generative image and video models can now produce coherent, stylistically consistent portfolios in hours, and LLMs can draft convincing critical reviews, exhibition catalogues and recommendation letters at negligible cost — with AI-generated synthetic media already complicating provenance checks across cultural institutions. The plausible next iteration of this fraud is not a £100,000 package but a £500 one, sold at volume through agentic tooling that assembles an entire application dossier end-to-end. Verification regimes built on trust in documents — bylines, award certificates, gallery listings — will degrade faster than endorsing bodies can adapt. The policy response is likely to require provenance infrastructure, direct verification with named referees and institutions, and randomised interviews, rather than more paperwork.

## Key facts

- BBC undercover reporters were offered a 'fully inclusive' Global Talent visa package exceeding £100,000, including artworks produced in China, arranged exhibitions, media coverage and recommendation letters.
- A consultant claimed no client on the full package had ever been refused a visa, with four approvals this year and around ten applications in progress.
- An extra £28,000 was quoted for a Chinese art award sponsored by Forbes to bolster an application.
- The Home Office is revoking visas granted to two Nigerian men who claimed to be fine art photographers, believing they were obtained by deception.
- Nigerian art critic Molara Wood found her byline attached to fabricated online reviews of the two applicants' work.
- About 6,600 Global Talent visas were granted in 2025; arts and culture applications nearly doubled between 2023 and 2025, with Arts Council England endorsing just under half of almost 3,500.
- iCareer Consultancy UK denied selling visa services and said the consultant had left the company before the undercover conversation.

## Implications

- The Home Office faces pressure to introduce interviews, direct referee verification or randomised audits for arts and culture endorsements, raising costs and processing times across the route.
- Regulated immigration advisers and their corporate affiliates will likely face closer scrutiny from the regulator and Companies House over linked-entity structures.
- Newspapers and cultural institutions become unwitting integrity infrastructure, and may need byline authentication and takedown processes for implanted content.
- Retrospective revocation sets a precedent: visas granted years earlier can be unwound, creating uncertainty for genuine applicants and their dependants.
- The case strengthens arguments that capital-weighted 'talent' routes undermine the credibility of a migration system being tightened elsewhere.

## Outlook

The Home Office has called the findings 'extremely serious', confirmed the company behind the £100,000 package was already under investigation, and said the Global Talent route is under review, with the home secretary promising whatever action is needed. Expect near-term tightening of the arts and culture category — plausibly mandatory interviews for flagged applications, verified referee checks and a harder line on third-party portfolio preparation — alongside a small number of high-profile revocations. The deeper test is structural: as generative tools drive the cost of fabricating a convincing artistic or technical record toward zero, document-based endorsement schemes will need provenance and identity verification rather than additional attestations. The government's dilemma is that the route is one of its few unambiguously pro-growth immigration instruments, so reform will likely be framed as fraud-proofing rather than restriction.

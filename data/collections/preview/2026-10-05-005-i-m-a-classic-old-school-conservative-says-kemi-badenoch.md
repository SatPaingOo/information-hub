# I'm a classic old school Conservative, says Kemi Badenoch

> [!summary] TL;DR — Kemi Badenoch, the new Tory leader, has declared herself a 'classic old school Conservative' and outlined a Thatcher‑inspired policy platform, emphasizing common‑sense governance, welfare reform, prison expansion, and reduced foreign aid. The manifesto seeks to re‑anchor the Conservatives after their 2024 defeat, contrasting her vision with Labour and Reform UK while staking out a distinct, fiscally conservative stance. The proposals have sparked debate over their practicality and political viability ahead of the upcoming conference.

## Background

The Conservative Party is still reeling from its historic defeat in the 2024 United Kingdom general election, trailing both Labour and Reform UK in opinion polls. Kemi Badenoch, whose personal poll numbers exceed those of the party, has positioned herself as a fresh, ideologically pure leader. In a 100‑page essay titled *The Right Way*—a deliberate echo of Margaret Thatcher’s 1976 essay *The Right Approach*—she argues that the previous Tory government lost its way by abandoning core Conservative principles. The essay, released ahead of the party’s annual conference in Birmingham, sets out a series of policy pledges, from welfare ‘safety net’ reforms to a massive increase in prison capacity, and frames the upcoming conference as a moment of ideological re‑engagement.

## Ideological Positioning and Thatcherite Rhetoric

Badenoch’s self‑description as a “classic old school Conservative” is a deliberate invocation of the Thatcher era, aiming to tap into nostalgia for a time when the Tories were seen as decisive and ideologically coherent. By likening Reform leader Nigel Farage to a “grumpy grandpa” and Labour’s Andy Burnham to a “cheesy uncle”, she frames her own stance as the sensible, middle‑ground alternative. This rhetorical strategy seeks to re‑define the party’s brand, moving away from the perceived complacency of the 2010‑2024 governments and presenting a clear, values‑based contrast to both the left and the populist right.

## Policy Proposals and Fiscal Strategy

The core of Badenoch’s platform is encapsulated in the essay’s central tenet: “business, not government, creates growth.” She proposes tax cuts, deregulation, and a reduction of the civil service to pre‑pandemic levels, arguing that a leaner state will unleash private sector dynamism. Complementary measures include ending the childcare “cliff edge” for high earners, cutting overseas aid to £1 bn, and delivering 50 000 new prison places to address rising crime. These policies are presented as “common‑sense” solutions, but they also raise questions about fiscal feasibility and the trade‑offs between social spending and punitive measures.

## Electoral Implications and Intra‑Party Dynamics

Badenoch’s bold stance is designed to energise a demoralised party base while appealing to swing voters disillusioned with the status quo. However, the proposals risk alienating centrist voters who may view the prison expansion and aid cuts as overly harsh. Internally, her characterisation of Farage and Burnham underscores a strategic effort to marginalise both the populist right and the Labour left, positioning the Tories as the sole custodians of “common‑sense” governance. The conference will be a litmus test of whether this ideological clarity can translate into electoral momentum ahead of the next general election.

## Key facts

- Kemi Badenoch released a 100‑page essay, *The Right Way*, echoing Margaret Thatcher’s 1976 work, outlining a return to core Conservative principles.
- She pledged to cut overseas aid to £1 bn annually and create 50 000 new prison places, funded by the aid reduction.
- The Tory leader described Nigel Farage as a “grumpy grandpa” and Andy Burnham as a “cheesy uncle” to differentiate her stance.
- Polling shows Badenoch’s personal approval exceeds that of the Conservative Party as a whole.
- The conference in Birmingham is intended to signal a break from the 2024 election defeat and re‑ignite party unity.

## Implications

- A dramatic aid cut could strain the UK’s international development commitments and affect recipient nations’ stability.
- The prison expansion may address overcrowding but raises concerns about fiscal sustainability and rehabilitation focus.
- The sharp rhetorical framing of opponents could deepen political polarisation and limit cross‑party dialogue.

## Outlook

If Badenoch can translate her Thatcher‑inspired rhetoric into credible, popular policies, the Conservatives may regain ground lost in 2024. However, the aggressive fiscal and penal measures risk alienating moderate voters and could provoke backlash from civil‑society groups. The Birmingham conference will be a critical proving ground: a unified, energised party could begin to close the poll gap, while missteps may cement the Tories’ status as a party still searching for a post‑2024 identity.

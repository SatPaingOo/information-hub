# Paul Adams: British-Israeli relations at lowest ebb in decades

> [!summary] TL;DR — The UK and eleven allies have banned trade with Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank, prompting Israel’s strongest diplomatic retaliation yet, including consulate closures and a downgrading of British representation. The move marks a historic low in British‑Israeli ties and raises questions about the future of settlement policy and international law.

## Background

For decades Western governments, including Britain, have regarded Israeli settlements on occupied Palestinian land as illegal under international law but have limited their response to diplomatic criticism. The 2024 International Court of Justice ruling declared the entire occupation—including Israel’s military presence—unlawful, prompting a more assertive British stance. Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband framed the new policy as a necessary reflection of that judgment, extending the ban beyond goods to construction, infrastructure, and finance firms linked to settlements, and also restricting arms sales that materially support the occupation. The decision follows a coordinated effort by twelve countries, a move that intensified Israel’s sense of isolation and provoked a swift, hostile reaction.

## Diplomatic Fallout and Reciprocity

Israel’s response has been unusually severe. The closure of the UK’s consulate in East Jerusalem—responsible for the West Bank and direct liaison with the Palestinian Authority in Ramallah—and the removal of British officials from the International Gaza Support Centre, a body created to coordinate relief and stabilisation in Gaza, signal a dramatic downgrading of diplomatic presence. These actions are framed by Israeli officials as a direct rebuke to what they view as a hostile British shift, and they underscore the depth of Israeli anger not only at the trade ban but at the broader alignment of Western opinion against settlement activity.

## Domestic Politics in Israel

The timing of the UK’s announcement coincides with an intensifying pre‑election atmosphere in Israel. Foreign Minister Gideon Saar’s blistering statement, accusing the UK of “settler terrorism” and “ethnic cleansing” by Palestinians, resonates with a segment of the Israeli electorate that views the government’s settlement policies as non‑negotiable. While many Israelis acknowledge that extremist settler ideology is problematic, the majority still see the settlements as a strategic asset, and the coordinated international sanctions have heightened a defensive nationalism that may bolster hard‑line parties ahead of the vote.

## International Law and Symbolic Politics

The ban’s significance extends beyond economics. By explicitly naming settlements as illegal under the ICJ ruling, Britain and its allies are reinforcing a legal narrative that isolates Israel diplomatically. The political symbolism of targeting settlement‑linked companies and construction firms is potent, as it threatens the financial ecosystem that sustains the settlements. Moreover, the language used by Miliband—referring to “settler terrorism” and “ethnic cleansing”—marks a rhetorical shift that aligns the UK more closely with Palestinian grievances, potentially influencing other nations to adopt similar stances.

## Key facts

- Twelve countries, including the UK, France, Spain and Canada, announced a coordinated ban on trade with Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank.
- The UK’s measures cover not only goods but also companies providing construction, infrastructure and financing to settlements, and restrict arms sales that materially support the occupation.
- Israel responded by closing the UK’s East Jerusalem consulate and withdrawing British representatives from the International Gaza Support Centre.
- Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband framed the policy as a “reset” reflecting the 2024 ICJ ruling that the occupation is unlawful.
- Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Saar accused the UK of “settler terrorism” and “ethnic cleansing”, a statement that resonates with many Israelis amid an upcoming election.

## Implications

- UK‑Israel relations are now at their lowest point in decades, with the potential for prolonged diplomatic chill and reduced intelligence cooperation.
- The coordinated sanctions may encourage other Western nations to adopt similar legal frameworks, further isolating Israel on the global stage.
- Settlement economies could face increased financial pressure, potentially slowing expansion and prompting internal political debate in Israel.
- The US, historically Israel’s chief ally, may face mounting domestic pressure to reconsider unconditional support, though current administration sentiment remains supportive.
- Palestinian advocacy gains a diplomatic victory, but the prospects for a viable Palestinian state remain slim without broader enforcement mechanisms.

## Outlook

In the short term, bilateral ties are likely to remain strained as both sides dig in over the legal and political merits of the settlement ban. Over the longer horizon, the precedent set by the twelve‑nation coalition could reshape the calculus of settlement policy, especially if economic pressure translates into political change within Israel. However, the entrenched nature of the occupation, the upcoming Israeli elections, and the limited leverage of non‑US actors suggest that a rapid reversal of settlement expansion remains unlikely.

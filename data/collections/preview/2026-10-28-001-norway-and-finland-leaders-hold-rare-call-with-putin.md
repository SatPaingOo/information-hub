# Norway and Finland leaders hold rare call with Putin

> [!summary] TL;DR — Finland’s President Alexander Stubb and Norway’s Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre held a joint phone call with Russian President Vladimir Putin to discuss a potential end to the Ukraine war. The Nordic leaders framed the dialogue as a neighbour‑to‑neighbour initiative, not an EU‑wide effort, while Putin insisted any lasting peace must respect Russia’s “legal interests.” The talks have reignited debate over European diplomatic engagement with Moscow and could influence upcoming peace negotiations in Abu Dhabi.

## Background

The 90‑minute call, arranged by Helsinki, marks the first direct contact between European heads of government and Putin since French President Emmanuel Macron’s July 2025 conversation. It follows a trilateral meeting in Oslo that included Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky and U.S. special envoy Steve Witkoff, signalling a shift toward back‑channel diplomacy as the war enters its third year. Both Norway and Finland, NATO members with long shared borders with Russia, emphasized that their initiative was driven by regional security concerns rather than a coordinated EU stance, highlighting a growing appetite among some European capitals to re‑engage Moscow directly amid worsening U.S.–Ukraine relations after the Trump administration eased certain sanctions.

## Nordic Neutrality and Strategic Autonomy

Finland and Norway have historically balanced close ties with Russia against their NATO commitments. Their decision to initiate the call reflects a pragmatic assessment that silence will not end the conflict, as President Stubb warned. By acting independently, the Nordics aim to preserve their own diplomatic channels while testing whether Russia is willing to consider a negotiated settlement that does not compromise its perceived security interests. This move also underscores a broader trend of smaller European states seeking autonomous foreign‑policy tools amid EU internal divisions.

## Russian Messaging and Red Lines

Putin’s response during the call reiterated his long‑standing narrative that a “just and lasting peace” hinges on Russia’s “legal interests” and that Ukraine’s desire for sovereign existence is a root cause of the war. By framing the conflict in legalistic terms, Moscow seeks to legitimize its territorial claims and set negotiation parameters that favor its position. The Kremlin’s emphasis on neighbour‑to‑neighbour dialogue also serves to sideline the EU, portraying European unity as fragmented and reinforcing Russia’s strategy of playing individual states against the bloc.

## Implications for EU Energy and Security Policy

The lack of an official EU reaction, coupled with comments from Swedish Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson that Europe should “stick together rather than going solo,” reveals ongoing strategic dissonance. If the Nordic initiative yields tangible progress, it could pressure the EU to adopt a more cohesive engagement strategy, potentially easing energy‑security tensions by opening dialogue with a major supplier. Conversely, a failure to produce concrete outcomes may embolden hardliners in Moscow and deepen European skepticism about back‑channel diplomacy, reinforcing reliance on sanctions and military aid to Kyiv.

## Key facts

- The call lasted 90 minutes and was initiated by Finland and Norway, not coordinated with Brussels.
- President Alexander Stubb and Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre described the conversation as “frank and substantive” but noted “big differences in views” with Putin.
- Putin conditioned peace on Russia’s “legal interests” and reiterated his view that Ukraine’s sovereignty desire underpins the conflict.
- The dialogue follows a trilateral meeting in Oslo involving Zelensky and U.S. envoy Steve Witkoff, with a tentative US‑Ukraine‑Russia summit planned for early November in Abu Dhabi.
- The Kremlin praised the talks as “very constructive and practical,” while the EU has not issued an official statement.

## Implications

- Potential opening of new diplomatic channels that could bypass EU consensus, risking fragmentation of European policy.
- Increased pressure on the EU to formulate a coordinated response, possibly affecting energy‑security and sanctions frameworks.
- Reinforcement of Russia’s narrative that it can negotiate directly with individual states, weakening collective Western leverage.
- Possible shift in Nordic security posture, prompting deeper defence cooperation within NATO to balance diplomatic outreach.

## Outlook

If the Abu Dhabi trilateral talks build on the Helsinki dialogue, the Nordic initiative could become a template for incremental confidence‑building measures between Moscow and Europe. However, given Putin’s insistence on pre‑conditions that align with existing Russian territorial claims, substantive progress remains unlikely without significant concessions from Kyiv and its Western allies. The coming months will test whether the EU can reconcile divergent national interests and present a united front, or whether ad‑hoc engagements like the Norway‑Finland call will remain the primary, albeit limited, avenue for de‑escalation.
